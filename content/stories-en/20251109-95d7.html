1

Overview


Thai Chinese women are an indispensable force in Thailand's socio-economic development. As one of the largest immigrant ethnic groups in Thailand, Chinese women play a key role in multiple sectors including business, education, and services (Institute for Cultural Diplomacy, 2025).


In 1947, the total population of overseas Chinese in Thailand reached 2.124 million, accounting for approximately 12% of Thailand's total population. According to the 2010 Thai census, about 14% of the population had Chinese ancestry. This number has continued to grow, exceeding 9 million people (Liu Wenzheng, 2025; Lertpusit, 2023). Although there is currently no specific data on the size of the Thai Chinese female population, Thai Chinese women occupy a significant position in Thailand's labor market, particularly in family businesses, retail trade, and food and beverage services—traditional sectors where Chinese communities have long held an advantage. In recent years, with the influx of a new generation of Chinese immigrants and rising educational attainment, Chinese women have begun to emerge in modern industries such as professional services, technological innovation, and financial investment.


However, they also face multiple challenges, including cultural integration and gender discrimination. This report systematically presents the historical evolution, current workplace conditions, and major issues faced by Thai Chinese women, based on the latest research data and historical literature.


2

Classification and Characteristics of Thai Chinese Women


Based on characteristics such as migration time, degree of social integration, and career development, Thai Chinese women can be categorized into the following groups:


2.1 | Established Chinese-Thai Women


This group primarily includes ethnic Chinese women born in Thailand. They are typically fully integrated into Thai society, use Thai as their first language, and hold Thai citizenship. Most work in professional fields such as business management, education, healthcare, and law, and generally have high levels of education (Hau, 2016). The Mastercard Index of Women's Entrepreneurship measures three core dimensions: women's advancement, access to knowledge and capital, and entrepreneurial support. The index shows that in 2022, Thailand scored 61.8 points on the women's entrepreneurship index, ranking 19th globally. Thai Chinese women entrepreneurs constitute a significant proportion of this figure, reflecting both Thailand's supportive environment for female entrepreneurship and the strong momentum of Chinese women in business (Mastercard, 2022)..


2.2 | New Immigrant Women


With the continuous advancement of China's reform and opening-up policy and deepening economic globalization, new Chinese immigrant women in Thailand have exhibited diversified migration methods, varied career choices, and higher levels of education. Unlike the labor-intensive labor exports that dominated in the 1980s and 1990s, since the 21st century, in addition to traditional labor migrants, there has been a growing number of professionals and skilled migrants moving to Thailand, resulting in more diverse occupational identities. This group mainly consists of short-term residents holding work or business visas. As of 2022, approximately 110,000 to 130,000 new Chinese immigrants lived in Thailand, including those engaged in business, education (study abroad), labor or technical work, and lifestyle migration. They are primarily concentrated in Bangkok and "secondary investment zones" such as Chonburi and Chachoengsao provinces, working in international trade, cross-border e-commerce, tourism services, education and training (e.g., Confucius Institutes), and freelance or remote work (e.g., bloggers, digital nomads) (Gatchalee, 2022).


Among the new immigrant group, there are also undocumented female workers. It is estimated that Thailand has about 15,000 to 16,000 undocumented Chinese laborers, mainly concentrated in Rayong and Chonburi provinces, engaged in labor-intensive jobs or "gray industries" such as restaurant cleaning, domestic work, and informal retail. This group often faces challenges including language barriers, undocumented status, and inadequate protection of basic rights. Moreover, the actual number may be much higher than official statistics suggest (Lertpusit, 2023).


3

Historical Development of Modern Thai Chinese Women's Migration


The migration of Chinese women to Thailand has evolved from a period of minimal presence to gradual expansion and then to reduced restrictions. Throughout this process, they have faced numerous challenges, including gender imbalance, difficult livelihoods, limited access to education, and low social status. Nevertheless, through hard work, Chinese women have made significant contributions to the development of Thailand's industry, commerce, and agriculture, exerting a profound impact on Thai society.


3.1 | Early Period: Female Migration Was Extremely Rare


As early as the 13th century, there were records of Chinese people traveling to Thailand for trade, and by the 16th century, many Chinese immigrants had settled in Thailand. However, prior to the modern era, the gender composition of Chinese migrants to Thailand was extremely unbalanced, dominated almost entirely by men, with female migration being extremely rare.


For a long time, feudal Chinese society upheld the model of "men work outside, women manage the household," requiring women to remain at home to serve in-laws and raise children. Moreover, during the Ming and Qing dynasties, the government strictly prohibited women from traveling overseas, resulting in very few Chinese women leaving the country (Wu Fengbin, 1988).


3.2 | Around the Opium Wars: A Period of Minimal Female Migration


Around the time of the Opium Wars, as maritime restrictions were relaxed, a small number of concubines and prostitutes began to be smuggled overseas. The 1909 Thai census revealed a severe gender imbalance among Bangkok's Chinese population, with men significantly outnumbering women. Before 1914, very few Chinese men had the means to bring their families, and "the sight of a Chinese woman on the streets of Bangkok was extremely rare" (Fan Ruolan, 1996).


3.3 | Late 19th to Early 20th Century: Gradual Expansion in Scale


After the end of World War I (1918), Thailand's economic development created increased demand for labor, leading to a noticeable rise in the number of Chinese women migrating to Thailand. This growth was driven both by "pull" factors from the destination country and "push" factors from southern China, where frequent warfare and natural disasters plagued the region. For example, from the 1920s to the 1940s, the Chaoshan region suffered frequent natural disasters, prompting large numbers of poor farmers from Fujian, Guangdong, and especially Chaoshan to migrate overseas to Thailand. Some with financial means brought their wives and children, while others were victims of human traffickers who smuggled women abroad for prostitution. Between 1918 and 1935, over 220,000 Chinese women entered Thailand, while over 140,000 left, resulting in a net increase of more than 80,000 women remaining in Thailand (Yang Jiancheng, 1986).


3.4 | 1930s–1940s: Period of Restrictions and Decline


Starting in 1927, Thailand began restricting immigration by increasing entry taxes, which were raised annually. By 1934, the Thai government implemented strict immigration controls, particularly targeting Chinese women, leading to a sharp decline in female immigration after 1934, far fewer than those leaving Thailand during the same period (Fan Ruolan, 1996). At the same time, Thailand enacted a series of laws and measures that excluded ethnic Chinese from approximately 27 different industries. Certain economic sectors were nationalized and placed under strict government control, explicitly excluding Chinese participation. Faced with this situation, many Chinese chose to leave Thailand for other parts of Asia. Some attempted to "conceal their identity" by adopting Thai names to reduce scrutiny (Minority Rights Group, 2018).


3.5 | 1960s–1970s to Present: Prominent Contributions by Women


In the 1960s and 1970s, Thailand began transitioning toward an export-oriented economic model. This strategic shift created significant development opportunities and advantages for the Chinese business community, strongly promoting their expansion and deepening presence in Thailand's economy (Minority Rights Group, 2018).


Today, with the continuous deepening and strengthening of economic, trade, and cultural exchanges and cooperation between China and Thailand, the Chinese diaspora—including those with partial Chinese ancestry and recent immigrants—has become widely distributed across all levels of Thai society, forming an indispensable component of Thailand's diverse social structure. Notably, since the 21st century, the new generation of Chinese immigrants has exhibited several distinct and unique characteristics:


(1) Significantly improved educational attainment. Compared to the past, the proportion of new Chinese immigrants with university or higher education has greatly increased, and their high academic qualifications have laid a solid knowledge foundation for their long-term development in Thai society.


(2) A clear trend toward family-based migration. The proportion of immigrants moving as entire families has continuously risen. In this process, the number of female immigrants has significantly increased, and women now constitute a much larger share of the immigrant population, altering the previous gender imbalance within migrant groups.


(3) Increasing occupational diversification. Chinese immigrants' career choices are no longer limited to traditional commerce and trade but are actively expanding into emerging, high-value-added fields such as technology, finance, and cultural creativity, demonstrating strong adaptability and innovative spirit.


(4) Enhanced two-way mobility. The new generation of Chinese immigrants maintains frequent back-and-forth exchanges between China and Thailand. They are deeply integrated into Thai society while maintaining close ties with their ancestral homeland, forming a unique dual cultural identity that has become a distinctive advantage (Liu Wenzheng, 2025).


At the same time, Chinese women have seen their voice and influence continuously rise in business and other social spheres, gradually becoming an important force driving social development. In terms of political participation, Chinese-Thai women hold approximately 19.4% of the seats in Thailand's National Assembly. Although this proportion is slightly below the global average, it has shown a consistent upward trend in recent years (World Bank Gender Data Portal, 2025). In Thai politics, a number of prominent Thai-Chinese women have emerged, such as Thailand's first female Prime Minister Yingluck Shinawatra and former cabinet minister Sudarat Keyuraphan, who have played significant roles on the national political stage through their outstanding leadership and political wisdom..


4

Challenges Faced by Modern Chinese Women Migrants in Employment


4.1 | Severe Economic Dependence


Most Chinese women who migrated to Thailand were married women who moved with their husbands. According to marital status data from Chinese immigrants between 1936 and 1937, there were 7,110 married women, 215 unmarried women over the age of 15, and 2,806 girls under the age of 14. At that time, most Chinese women relied on their husbands or families for livelihood and were typically categorized as unemployed or housewives in government labor surveys. Their labor was internalized as a familial duty, classifying them as unpaid workers whose contributions were not formally recognized by society (Yang Jiancheng, 1986).


4.2 | Low levels of education, livelihood difficulties, and some forced into the sex industry


In modern times, most female Chinese immigrants engaged in heavy physical labor. Due to their impoverished backgrounds and illiteracy, they could only take low-income, low-status jobs such as domestic servants, laundry workers, and nannies. In the early 20th century, ethnic Chinese women accounted for about 20% of domestic servants and nannies in Thailand, and about 10% of laundry workers. Many Chinese wives and daughters participated in family-run businesses, such as grocery stores, but women from impoverished families still struggled to secure basic household income.


At the same time, due to the imbalanced sex ratio among Chinese immigrants and the large number of single men, Thailand's prostitution industry flourished, and some Chinese women were forced into prostitution. In 1929, there were numerous brothels operated by Chinese in Bangkok, along with many public or privately run sex workers. Although local authorities and places like Hong Kong implemented some rescue measures, the phenomenon of Chinese women in prostitution persisted for a long time (Yang Jiancheng, 1986; Fan Ruolan, 1996).


4.3 | Subordinate status under the influence of feudal moral values


Modern Thai Chinese society fully inherited the feudal moral view of "men superior, women inferior." Leaders of Chinese clan associations and chambers of commerce in Thailand were without exception male, and polygamy was common within the Chinese community, resulting in extremely low social and familial status for Chinese women. However, with the rise of Western civil rights movements and the gradual introduction of gender equality concepts into Thailand, the Chinese community began to be influenced. In 1939, the "Siam Chaoyang Ancestral Association" passed regulations for the first time to admit female members, stating, "All Chaoyang people, regardless of gender, are eligible to join the association," aiming to achieve gender equality. However, this clause was soon abolished by opponents of gender equality. After World War II, Chinese hometown associations across Thailand became active again, and many organizations adopted gender-equal systems. The Chaoyang Ancestral Association also revised its bylaws once more to admit female members (Fan Ruolan, 1996).


5

Contemporary career development of Thai Chinese women


5.1 | Labor participation of women in Thailand


World Bank data shows that among Thailand's population of over 60 million, the gender structure is relatively balanced, with women accounting for 51.31%, slightly higher than men (World Bank, 2025). In terms of labor force participation, the labor force participation rate for women aged 15 and above in Thailand is approximately 58.9%, while the male rate for the same period is 74.7%, indicating a slightly lower participation rate for women. (World Bank Gender Data Portal, 2025) .


At the cultural and educational level, literacy among Thailand's adult population shows a high degree of convergence between men and women. Statistical data from 2022 shows that literacy rates among Thai adults are nearly equal between genders and rank relatively high in the Southeast Asian region. Adult literacy refers to the proportion of the population aged 15 and above who can read and accurately understand a short text related to daily life and possess writing ability. This indicator not only reflects the basic cultural literacy of the population but also to some extent reflects the effectiveness of social education resource allocation and utilization (World Bank Gender Data Portal, 2025).


From the perspective of employment quality, since 1991, the situation of vulnerable employment (i.e., precarious employment) among Thai women has gradually improved. Vulnerably employed individuals typically lack formal employment contracts, comprehensive social security systems, and safety buffers to cope with economic risks, making them more vulnerable to poverty. Statistics from 2023 show that the vulnerable employment rate for Thai women is 50%, compared to 49.4% for men. Compared to the regional average in East Asia and the Pacific, both Thai men and women have relatively high rates of vulnerable employment. This reflects that Thailand still faces certain challenges in improving employment quality and building stable employment environments (World Bank Gender Data Portal, 2025).


5.2 | Workplace treatment and legal protections


Thai labor law applies to employers and employees both within Thailand and in cross-border contexts. Although its level of protection may not match that of some developed countries, the protection it offers to employees cannot be overlooked.


Key written labor laws in Thailand include: the Labour Protection Act B.E. 2541 (1998) and its amendments, the Labour Relations Act B.E. 2518, the Civil and Commercial Code B.E. 2535, the Social Security Act B.E. 2533, the Workman Compensation Act B.E. 2537, and regulations concerning minimum wage.


Under this framework, the Labour Protection Act marks a new starting point for employee rights protection, and its amendments have further strengthened employee rights (LORENZ & PARTNERS, 2020). In addition, announcements and decrees issued by the Ministry of Labour also have legal force. Furthermore, Thailand's 2017 Constitution stipulates certain fundamental rights related to labor law. For example, the Constitution guarantees free trade and economic competition, freedom of occupation, the right to own private property, and the right to gender equality (Constitution of the Kingdom of Thailand, 2017).


5.2.1 Discrimination

The Thai Constitution generally prohibits discrimination based on various factors including race, gender, and nationality. The Labour Protection Act also explicitly prohibits gender-based discrimination. Violators may face fines of up to 20,000 Thai baht (approximately 4,000 RMB). For contract workers (outsourced workers), since their working arrangements are similar to those of employees directly contracted with employers, they should enjoy equal benefits and treatment and should not face discrimination (LORENZ & PARTNERS, 2020).


Meanwhile, according to the Persons with Disabilities Empowerment Act, employers must hire one person with disabilities for every 100 employees. Otherwise, for each unfilled position, the employer is obligated to make a financial contribution to the Foundation for the Disabled equivalent to the daily minimum wage (since January 1, 2020, the statutory minimum daily wage in Thailand has been 313 baht) multiplied by 365 days. The exact amount varies depending on the location of the employer's company or factory.



5.2.2 Working hours and leave

Thailand's Labour Protection Act sets standard working hours, typically limited to no more than 8 hours per day and 48 hours per week. For hazardous work or work that may threaten employee health (such as work involving radiation, welding, dangerous chemicals, or extreme temperatures), standard working hours must not exceed 7 hours per day or 42 hours per week. Any work exceeding these standard hours is considered overtime and must be compensated accordingly.


Employers must clearly define and publish standard working hours. If employing 10 or more employees, employers must establish and publish written work rules in Thai. These rules must specify not only standard working hours but also regulations on overtime, leave, severance pay, and disciplinary measures, and must be publicly displayed in the workplace.


Pregnant employees are entitled to 98 days of maternity leave (including prenatal checkups), during which the employer must pay wages for 45 days. These 98 days include all regular weekly holidays and statutory public holidays. Additionally, if a pregnant employee provides a medical certificate stating she is unable to continue her original work and the employer deems it reasonable, she has the right to request reassignment to another suitable position (Suwanvajukkasikij & Vorawanichar, 2025). Pregnant women may not work between 10 p.m. and 6 a.m., may not work overtime, may not work on public holidays, and may not work in hazardous environments. If employers violate these legal provisions, they will be penalized under the Labour Protection Act, even if the employee consents. Moreover, terminating an employee due to pregnancy is invalid and will be considered "unfair termination" (LORENZ & PARTNERS, 2020).



5.2.3 Protection of women and children

After the 2008 amendment to the Labour Protection Act, women were generally allowed to be employed between midnight and 6 a.m., whereas previously this was only permitted under specific exceptional circumstances. However, the employment of women during this period is subject to supervision by labor inspectors. If working during this time poses any health risk to women, the Director-General of Labor has the authority to require adjustment of working hours. Furthermore, women may not be employed in hazardous or health-threatening work, with particularly strict restrictions in mining operations and the production and transportation of dangerous goods. The 2019 amendment to the Labour Protection Act, formally implemented that year, clearly stipulates that male and female employees have equal rights in terms of wages and compensation (Somwaiya, 2019).


At the same time, the Labour Protection Act explicitly prohibits the employment of children under the age of 15 and sets specific restrictions on the types of work, processes, working hours, overtime, and workplaces permitted for employees under 18. Employers who wish to hire minors under 18 must follow specific procedures. For example, they must notify labor inspectors when hiring or dismissing such employees and maintain employment records (LORENZ & PARTNERS, 2020).



5.2.4 Employment of foreigners

The Alien Working Act of Thailand aims to regulate the employment of non-Thai nationals and plays an important role in ensuring legal compliance and protection within the Thai labor market. Foreign workers must first obtain a Type B or Type O non-immigrant visa, then apply for a work permit using valid visa documents and an employment contract before they can legally begin work. On the employer side, under the Alien Working Act, employers must report to the Department of Employment within 15 days of hiring or dismissing a foreign employee. Any changes in the foreign employee's job duties must also be reported. Employers must also ensure that the foreign employee's work remains consistent with the scope specified on the work permit; otherwise, they may face fines and suspension of sponsorship privileges. Additionally, employers must ensure that foreign employees fulfill their 90-day address reporting obligation, often assigning a compliance officer or legal counsel to supervise this requirement.


At the same time, the Thai government has been continuously improving policies for foreign workers, promoting digitalization of application processes, establishing a unified database, and setting up one-stop service centers, reducing processing time to just three days. Meanwhile, the Smart Visa system established under Section 64/1 of the Immigration Act allows high-potential foreign nationals to live and work in Thailand without applying separately for a work permit (Benoit & Partners, 2025).


5.3 | WBL Index


The Women, Business and the Law (WBL) index, published by the World Bank (hereinafter referred to as the WBL index), aims to measure the legal environment, supportive policies, and actual implementation of women's economic empowerment in countries, covering three aspects: 1) legal framework, referring to women's legal rights in economic activities; 2) supportive framework, referring to policy tools and practical measures for women's economic empowerment; and 3) expert opinions, referring to experts' assessments of the actual implementation of women's economic empowerment.


In 2024, Thailand scored 60.0 in the legal framework, below the global average but above the East Asia and Pacific regional average. Thailand performed well in women's property and inheritance rights, but legislation on safety (such as domestic violence and sexual harassment) still requires significant improvement; the supportive framework score was 31.7, also below global and regional averages, with relatively comprehensive policies in marriage, but clear shortcomings in wage transparency, childcare services, and entrepreneurship support; the expert opinion score was 77.5, higher than both global and regional averages, indicating strong recognition of Thailand's actual implementation in women's freedom of movement, entrepreneurial opportunities, property rights, and pensions, though practical support for childcare services still has room for improvement (World Bank, 2024).


6

Major Challenge: Gender Stereotypes Limiting Career Development


Despite Thailand's leading levels of female education and female labor force participation in Southeast Asia, Thai-Chinese and new migrant women face significant glass ceiling barriers in the workplace, among which industry concentration and gender discrimination are two interwoven factors that have become the main constraints on career advancement (Hansatit, 2014).


In terms of industry distribution, Thailand's service and light industries (such as textiles, footwear manufacturing) absorb a large number of female workers. However, these industries commonly adopt piece-rate pay systems and lack comprehensive welfare protection. According to 2023 data, women account for over 60% of informal employment. Informal employment often means poor job stability, inadequate protection of labor rights, and narrow career advancement pathways (Liu Runshi, 2023). Women remain trapped in such industries and employment forms for long periods, unable to access opportunities to improve their occupational status and income levels, severely limiting their career development.


In Thailand, typical local organizations across industries often exhibit a "glass ceiling" phenomenon that hinders women's promotion to managerial positions (Amornvivat, 2020). Long-standing gender norms also influence contemporary Thai women's decisions and preferences. In male-dominated industries, these deeply entrenched norms create internal barriers for women, greatly undermining their confidence, ambition, and career aspirations (Buasuwan & Niyamajan, 2019). Such discrimination manifests in traditional Chinese-Thai family businesses as the biased belief of "passing responsibilities to sons, not daughters." This belief is not based on ability, but heavily influenced by traditional gender roles, holding that men are more suitable to inherit and manage family businesses. Even when women possess outstanding management skills, extensive business experience, and overseas education backgrounds, they are often excluded from core management positions (Samthong, 2022). For new migrant women, disadvantages in language, culture, and social networks already pose significant challenges when entering the Thai workforce. The combined impact of linguistic and cultural barriers with gender discrimination further exacerbates their difficulties in career advancement.


7

Conclusion


Reviewing the migration and career development history of Thai-Chinese women, a clear intergenerational development pattern emerges. From rising educational attainment, diversified career choices, to expanded decision-making participation and voice, Thai-Chinese women have gradually enhanced their economic independence, and Thai society is transitioning from the past feudal tradition of "male superiority and female inferiority" toward a modern society of "gender equality." Chinese-Thai women, combining strength and resilience, serve as important bridges connecting China and Thailand and driving regional economic development. However, gender stereotypes remain a major obstacle on their career paths. Nevertheless, it is foreseeable that with continuous social progress, deepening awareness of equality, and collaborative efforts from all sectors, Thai-Chinese women will embrace a fairer and more diverse development environment, with greater possibilities to realize their self-worth.
