One

Research Background


The Israeli-Palestinian conflict is one of the most complex and protracted regional conflicts in the world today, with roots tracing back over a century. This conflict involves multiple dimensions—territory, sovereignty, security, and national identity—and shapes the fates of both the Israeli and Palestinian peoples. Core issues include Israeli settlements, the status of Jerusalem, Palestinian refugee rights, and border delineation. In recent years, the peace process has stalled and the situation has deteriorated due to the worsening humanitarian crisis in the Gaza Strip, deepening political divisions within Palestine, and the rightward shift in Israeli domestic politics. These intertwined factors have escalated the conflict and triggered a severe humanitarian crisis. Since the military conflict in October 2023, the high number of civilian casualties and displaced persons has drawn widespread international concern.


Against this backdrop, the plight of ethnic minority women becomes even more pronounced. Women living in the Israeli-Palestinian region, especially those in marginalized communities, face compounded challenges due to their gender and ethnic identity. They endure multiple pressures, including threats of violence, economic hardship, lack of medical resources, social exclusion, and disrupted education. Living in conflict zones places them in prolonged insecurity, and gender-based violence—including sexual assault—becomes particularly prevalent during military occupation and heightened tensions. Economic instability in conflict areas forces ethnic minority women into low-paid, unstable jobs. Severe shortages in medical resources disproportionately affect maternal health, chronic disease treatment, and reproductive health services. Moreover, the lack of safe educational opportunities undermines the future prospects of young girls and limits their economic independence. The social marginalization of ethnic minority women further restricts their ability to participate in decision-making and peace processes, and their needs are often ignored. Stereotypical media portrayals silence their authentic voices, deepening their sense of isolation. The intersection of gender, ethnicity, and conflict creates multifaceted oppression that urgently demands attention. Despite these challenges, ethnic minority women in the Israeli-Palestinian region continue to demonstrate resilience and leadership. Many Palestinian women play vital roles in community organizing and political advocacy, speaking out for peace and justice. Their courage underscores the importance of including women's voices in discussions on peace and conflict resolution.


Against this backdrop, this study focuses on the situation of ethnic minority women in the context of the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, exploring the multiple challenges they face in daily life, employment, and education, as well as their underlying causes. The study employs case analysis and literature review methods to examine, from an intersectional theory perspective, how the interplay of race, gender, and national identity shapes the lived experiences of ethnic minority women. The report first outlines the historical background of the Israeli-Palestinian conflict and clarifies its specific impacts on women. It then focuses on the situation of Palestinian women in Israeli society, including the systemic injustices they face in legal protection, employment opportunities, and access to education. The findings reveal that ethnic minority women in Israel exist within a highly racialized and gendered social structure, encountering a "glass ceiling" in the labor market, facing language barriers and cultural identity crises in education, and often experiencing institutional neglect when seeking legal protection. Additionally, the study examines the opportunities and challenges for Israeli and Palestinian women in cross-cultural cooperation, finding that although positive initiatives such as "Jerusalem Link" exist, the effectiveness of cross-cultural dialogue remains limited due to the avoidance of structural political issues and fundamental differences in lived experiences. Based on these findings, the report analyzes the root causes of these problems, explores the role of women in peace processes, and proposes policy recommendations such as strengthening legal protection systems, advancing gender-sensitive peace initiatives, and promoting multicultural education, aiming to offer meaningful insights and suggestions for advancing gender equality and social justice in the Israeli-Palestinian region.


Two

The Situation and Challenges of Ethnic Minority Women in Israel


In Israel, Palestinian women live within a highly racialized and gendered social structure, and their situation reflects the complex intersection of race, gender, class, and national identity. This intersection not only places them at a disadvantage within legal and institutional frameworks but also subjects them to widespread social exclusion in daily life. Israel defines itself as a "Jewish state," and although it formally grants equal rights to all citizens, Palestinian Arab citizens have long been marginalized in practice. For Palestinian women, this marginalization is further intensified by their gender identity, as they face unique oppression and challenges in both public and private spheres.


This unique situation forces Palestinian women to constantly negotiate their identity within a "third space." The "third space" refers to the space where they simultaneously face racialized exclusion from the Jewish state and gendered constraints within Palestinian communities. The interplay of these dual identities creates significant tensions in their daily lives, career development, and education. For example, in the labor market, they face discrimination not only due to their Palestinian identity but are also confined to low-wage, low-skill positions due to their gender. In the education system, they are forced to balance between the dominant Hebrew culture and their own Arab culture, a cultural conflict that profoundly affects their academic performance and mental health. Moreover, Palestinian women encounter systemic neglect when seeking judicial protection. Israeli legal and law enforcement institutions often display indifference and bias when handling violence cases involving Palestinian women. This not only perpetuates their victim status within families and communities but also further erodes their trust in state institutions. In public discourse, their identities are frequently stereotyped as "potential enemies" or a "fifth column," a stigmatization that subjects them to greater isolation and hostility in society.


This section will examine, from four perspectives—social exclusion, the legal system, employment environment, and educational environment—the phenomenon of marginalization experienced by Palestinian women in Israeli society. By analyzing the challenges and contradictions they face in these areas, this paper seeks to reveal how racialization, gendering, and class stratification collectively shape the survival struggles and identity formation of Palestinian women. At the same time, the paper will also highlight how, under these multiple oppressions, Palestinian women strive to gain greater voice and social recognition through knowledge, social networks, and self-expression.



Social Exclusion

Highly educated Palestinian women occupy a "third space," constantly negotiating their identity at the complex intersection of race, gender, class, and national identity. Although they formally possess equal rights, the fact that Israel defines itself as a Jewish state results in the de facto marginalization of Palestinian Arab citizens. Interviewed women commonly report experiencing discrimination in Israeli society due to their Arab identity. For instance, one woman mentioned avoiding speaking Arabic in shopping malls because "if they hear me, they immediately subject me to strict checks and searches." This social exclusion is particularly evident in employment and housing. Some women have been denied rental housing by landlords after revealing their Arab identity, even after prior agreements had been reached, demonstrating how racialized everyday realities profoundly affect their living conditions (Herzog, 2004).


In cross-cultural exchanges within peacebuilding initiatives, the issue of Palestinian women's identity becomes even more pronounced. Palestinian-Israeli women often face identity challenges, caught between Palestinian and Israeli identities. For example, one woman noted being frequently asked in school to confirm her identity—whether she is Palestinian or Israeli. She stated: "Sometimes we have to be Israeli girls, sometimes Palestinian girls, but we are always seen as 'Arab-Israeli girls'—a contradictory identity that requires balancing between Jerusalem and Tel Aviv" (Collier, 2009). This hybrid identity creates multiple identity tensions, generating conflict not only at the personal level but also deeply affecting public life. They must constantly adjust their identities in education, employment, and social participation to conform to racialized norms in different contexts (Sharoni, 2012).


This dual identity intensifies contradictions and hostility at both social and political levels. For example, during the Second Intifada, many Palestinian citizens participated in protests against Israeli occupation policies. However, the Israeli government and society viewed these protests as threats to national security, further deepening distrust toward the Palestinian minority. In Israeli media and political discourse, Palestinian citizens are often portrayed as a "fifth column" or "potential enemy." This stereotype not only damages their social relations but also negatively affects their political rights. For instance, in 2010, the Israeli Knesset passed a series of laws restricting the activities of Arab political parties and members of parliament, imposing harsher penalties for acts deemed "disloyal to the state" (Rouhana & Sultany, 2003). Moreover, gender inequality in Israel is intertwined with cultural frameworks of security and national identity, placing Palestinian women in a doubly marginalized position due to both their gender and ethnic identity. They are not only confined to the domestic sphere due to gender norms but also excluded from national security and public affairs due to their ethnic identity. This social structure further reinforces their symbolic status as the "other" (Herzog, 1998).



Legal system

Palestinian women face numerous difficulties when seeking legal protection. Research by Shalhoub-Kevorkian (2004) shows that the Israeli legal and police systems frequently ignore cases of violence against Palestinian women. Herzog (2004), through interviews, found that violence experienced by Palestinian women within their communities due to their gender often goes unresolved, further worsening their situation. In cases of domestic violence, police responses to Palestinian women's pleas for help are often marked by indifference and neglect. This law enforcement bias is frequently justified under the guise of "cultural tolerance," treating domestic or gender-based violence as a "cultural issue" within Palestinian society, thereby reducing police responsibility for intervention and leaving such matters to families or communities to resolve (Herzog, 1998). This attitude reflects institutional discrimination (Sharoni, 2012) and perpetuates the social marginalization of Palestinian women, making victims even more vulnerable to danger (Rouhana & Sultany, 2003). In 2020, a series of cases in which Palestinian women died from domestic violence sparked public questioning of existing legal protections. Although civil society organizations have called for a more comprehensive Family Protection Law, legislative progress has been repeatedly blocked by traditional forces. The current legal and support systems fail to provide effective protection for domestic violence survivors, leaving many Palestinian women trapped in violent environments.


Palestinian women also frequently face unjust treatment in the judicial system due to their identity and language. In 2024, Ms. Ismail, a Palestinian-American citizen, was detained by Israeli authorities in the West Bank for allegedly posting incitement content on social media. During her detention, despite suffering from severe asthma and anxiety, she did not receive timely or adequate medical care. In the military court, she faced language barriers and insufficient legal representation, which limited her right to defense. Ultimately, Ms. Ismail was sentenced to six months in prison (Frankel, 2024). This case highlights the systemic injustices Palestinian women face within Israel's military judicial system, including inadequate medical care, procedural discrimination, and suppression of freedom of expression. Meanwhile, human rights organizations have reported that detained Palestinian women are frequently subjected to physical and psychological abuse, including sexual violence, solitary confinement, and lack of access to basic hygiene products. However, victims face numerous obstacles when seeking legal redress, such as lack of legal aid, difficulties in evidence collection, and pressure from families and communities. These challenges make it difficult for them to receive fair legal treatment, further exacerbating their plight (United Nations Human Rights Office of the High Commissioner, 2024).



Structural discrimination in the employment environment

Palestinian women in Israel's labor market face dual pressures: one is racial discrimination against Arabs in Israeli society, and the other is gender restrictions within Palestinian communities (Herzog, 2004) . Although many Palestinian women have attained higher education, they still encounter a "glass ceiling" in their career development (Herzog, 1998). This career barrier first stems from their ethnicity. For example, a woman who worked at a bank for 13 years, despite excellent performance, never received a promotion, while Jewish female colleagues with similar or even inferior performance were promoted (Herzog, 2004). Another woman with a law degree was directly asked about her religious beliefs during a job interview, as Jewish employers tend to be more accepting of Christian Arabs than Muslim Arabs (Herzog, 1998). Even when they are equally or more qualified than their Jewish counterparts, they are often excluded from many high-paying and high-status professions due to their identity. One interviewee noted: "Even though we live in Israel and speak Hebrew, we still have to constantly prove our worth—this extra effort seems to be because of our Palestinian identity" (Sharoni, 2012). Despite her strong performance, she still had to exert additional effort to demonstrate her professional competence and value. Such biases based on race and religion further marginalize Palestinian women. In 2023, Israel passed a law allowing education authorities to dismiss teachers based on their political views, particularly targeting educators who support Palestinian rights. This legislation has drawn widespread criticism for targeting the Palestinian community and potentially leading to Palestinian women losing their jobs, thereby intensifying their marginalization in the education sector (Shpigel, 2024). This case illustrates how structural discrimination is legitimized through legal means, further restricting career choices for women from minority groups.


Secondly, it is due to gender discrimination. In the labor market, Palestinian women not only face occupational segregation but are also forced into low-wage, low-skill jobs such as cleaning or food service, which not only limits their economic independence but also further entrenches their low social status (Yarchi, 2014). Employers hold deep-seated biases, perceiving them as less capable and unsuitable for certain "high-pressure" or "high-responsibility" positions. This discrimination is particularly evident in hiring and promotion processes, making it difficult for them to advance professionally even after entering the workforce (Collier, 2009). The militarized social environment further restricts employment opportunities for Palestinian women. Israeli society generally views individuals who have not served in the military as lacking professional competence, and some companies explicitly state a preference for candidates who have completed military service (Sharoni, 2012). However, most Palestinian women are unable to serve in the military, placing them at a disadvantage in the labor market (Herzog, 1998). Other gender-based issues include lack of access to vocational training, hiring discrimination, and the glass ceiling they face in the workplace. These structural barriers make it difficult for them to access opportunities in high-paying sectors such as technology and finance, further reinforcing their marginalized status in society (Shalhoub-Kevorkian, 2004). Overall, the inequality Palestinian women face in the labor market is not due to individual shortcomings, but rather a manifestation of structural problems.



Educational environment

Many Palestinian women first have deep contact with Israeli society within educational settings (Herzog, 2004), but they often face discrimination due to their ethnicity and language. In Israeli universities, many Palestinian women must live in off-campus housing, yet they frequently encounter racial discrimination when searching for accommodation. Some landlords explicitly refuse to rent to Arabs, and even after agreeing on rental terms, applicants may be rejected at the time of signing. One interviewee recalled: "I've had several experiences where the apartment was already agreed upon, but as soon as the landlord found out I was Palestinian, they immediately backed out." This housing discrimination not only increases their living stress but also intensifies their marginalization in society. At the same time, Israeli universities primarily use Hebrew as the language of instruction, while most Palestinian women's native language is Arabic. This language barrier makes it difficult for them to express themselves freely in classroom discussions. One interviewee described: "I stood there, as if I had a label on my forehead saying 'oppressed person,' and I simply didn't dare to voice my opinions." This sense of linguistic discomfort not only hinders their academic performance but also leaves them feeling isolated and helpless in the classroom.


Conflicts over cultural identity in the curriculum further exacerbate the challenges faced by Palestinian female students. During university, they were exposed to a broader critical knowledge system, including histories and social issues from multiple perspectives (Erdreich & Rapoport, 2002). These women gradually realized that their ethnic and cultural identities were systematically marginalized within mainstream academic discourse. Israel's curriculum is highly centralized around Jewish history and Zionist narratives, while portrayals of Palestinian history and culture are extremely limited or even negative (Shalhoub-Kevorkian, 2004). For example, when learning the Zionist narrative, they found their own cultural history depicted as a "threat" or "outsider." One respondent stated: "What we were taught in school was completely different from what our families and communities taught us. In school, I felt like I had to hide my identity to avoid being seen as an enemy." The exclusionary environment in higher education also had a profound impact on the mental health of Palestinian female students. On one hand, they hoped to improve their social status through higher education; on the other, they constantly questioned their cultural identity and self-worth due to the exclusion embedded in educational content and campus culture (Yarchi, 2014). This psychological distress was particularly evident in their language learning process. They not only needed to master academic knowledge in Hebrew and English, but also had to seek a balance of identity between the two languages. One student admitted: "Every time I express myself in Hebrew, I feel a sense of betrayal, as if I'm gradually drifting away from my own culture and language."


At the same time, social isolation on campus further intensified the sense of isolation and psychological burden experienced by Palestinian women. Many respondents mentioned that they found it difficult to integrate into mainstream social circles. In classrooms, they often hesitated to voice their opinions, fearing exclusion due to their ethnicity or political stance. One student recalled: "During a class discussion, I mentioned the Palestinian perspective, and the teacher publicly criticized me for lacking objectivity. After that, I never dared to speak up again in class." While education is regarded as an important tool for social mobility, for Palestinian women, it feels more like a struggle over culture and identity (Sharoni, 2012). The systemic exclusion they face within Israel's education system not only affects their academic performance but also impacts their self-identity and mental health.


Three

Women's Political Participation and Peace Initiatives



Women's Roles in the Peace Process

One of the key reasons behind the difficult situations faced by Israeli and Palestinian women is the ongoing Israeli-Palestinian conflict, and to resolve this issue—building and sustaining peace, women's participation is crucial. First, women often face specific challenges during conflicts, including gender-based violence and economic marginalization. Women's participation helps ensure these issues receive greater attention in peacebuilding, and their broader, more comprehensive perspectives contribute to more sustainable peace (Carey, 2019). At the same time, women are often deeply rooted in their communities and can serve as grassroots peace mediators, helping rebuild communities and reduce the likelihood of renewed conflict (Carey, 2019). The adoption of UN Security Council Resolution (UNSCR) 1325 in 2000 called for increased participation of women in all aspects of peace processes, including enhancing women's representation at negotiation tables to ensure peace agreements are gender-sensitive. It also urged measures to protect women from sexual and gender-based violence in conflict situations and to ensure their access to healthcare, psychological support, and economic resources during reconstruction (George & Shepherd, 2016).


In response to the call of Resolution 1325, both Israel and Palestine have taken certain steps. Although the Israeli government has yet to officially launch a related National Action Plan (NAP), civil society organizations such as "Itach Maaki – Women Lawyers for Social Justice" have consistently worked to promote the implementation of Resolution 1325 in Israel (Bouri, 2024). Meanwhile, the Palestinian Ministry of Women's Affairs developed an NAP based on Resolution 1325, covering two phases: 2017–2019 and 2020–2024. The first phase primarily focused on recovery and relief, participation, accountability, and protection and prevention. The second phase added elements such as protecting girls and women from the impacts of conflict and occupation and enhancing their participation in international and local political decision-making processes (GPPAC, 2022).


However, despite growing recognition of women's roles in peacebuilding, their actual participation continues to be constrained by multiple structural barriers. In Israel, women have gained more seats in parliament, increasing from seven female members in 1988 to thirty in 2021. Yet, due to cultural and religious norms, female representatives in government are not directly involved in negotiations on resolving the Israeli-Palestinian conflict or in peacebuilding processes. The increase in the number of women parliamentarians has not translated into substantial influence on peacebuilding (Itzkovitch-Malka & Friedberg, 2018). Israel's mandatory military service also poses a barrier for women; during service, women frequently face gender-based violence and are excluded from high-level decision-making (McLeod, 2019). Some Israeli women, such as Julia Chaitin, advocate for reconciliation-oriented approaches, acknowledging Palestinian suffering and criticizing militaristic policies, but these voices are often suppressed by the dominant nationalist discourse (Chaitin, 2019).


Similarly, due to men's deeply entrenched dominance in political structures and patriarchal norms in society, Palestinian women remain excluded from official political decision-making. Nevertheless, Palestinian women have creatively advanced peacebuilding through grassroots actions—unlike traditional narratives that rely on official archives, Palestinian women often use oral history, drawing on their own experiences and voices to celebrate women's culture, offer new perspectives on the conflict, and amplify the voices of marginalized groups affected by the conflict (Hasabelnaby & Nasr, 2022). In addition, organizations such as the Palestinian Center for Peace and Democracy and the Women's Peace and Humanitarian Fund Rapid Response Window (WPHF RRW) have provided platforms for Palestinian women to speak out. These organizations offer training for women of all ages to better engage with policymakers and increase women's representation in peacebuilding and political decision-making (Bouri, 2024).



Opportunities and Challenges in Cross-Cultural Collaboration between Israeli and Palestinian Women

As discussed above, women face specific hardships in the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, and these hardships are cross-cultural for both Palestinian and Israeli women. Yuval-Davis (1997) introduced the concept of "transversal politics," emphasizing that women from different cultural and ethnic backgrounds can build connections through dialogue, forming a mode of cooperation that transcends identity politics. In transversal dialogue, women recognize the "rootedness" of each other's nations and cultures, but through dialogue, they "shift" or transcend these differences. This form of cross-cultural solidarity is not based on shared identities as oppressed groups, but on shared normative values (Yuval-Davis, 1997).


Indeed, cross-cultural dialogue initiatives between Israeli and Palestinian women have gradually emerged, aiming to build mutual trust, promote peace, and drive broader social change. Among them, the signing of the 1993 Declaration of Principles of the Oslo Accords was a significant milestone, institutionalizing and providing funding for civil society dialogue between Israelis and Palestinians. In this context, hundreds of grassroots cross-cultural dialogue organizations emerged (Rynhold, 2007). In the field of women's peace movements, Jerusalem Link—formally established in 1994—is a representative example, jointly founded by the Israeli group Bat Shalom ("Peace Women") and the Palestinian group Marcaz al-Quds la l-Nissah ("Jerusalem Women's Center"). Although the two organizations operate independently, they share a common goal—promoting "justice, peace, democracy, human rights, and women's rights"—which forms the basis of their coexistence and cooperation as Jewish and Palestinian groups (Byrne, 2022). Additionally, with support from the United Nations, elite women from Palestine, Israel, and the international community established the International Women's Coalition (IWC) in 2005, dedicated to "ending Israel's occupation and achieving a just and lawful peace based on international law, human rights, and equality" (Byrne, 2022).


However, Palestinian and Israeli women's groups face numerous challenges in cross-cultural collaboration, primarily stemming from differing social positions, expectations, and experiences. First, mainstream Israeli feminist movements are largely led by middle-class, university-educated Jewish Israeli women, who often struggle to fully recognize the unique experiences of Palestinian women, especially those from Arab backgrounds or living in occupied territories (Abdu, 2007).


Moreover, the planning and implementation of cross-cultural collaboration initiatives often overlook the most critical structural political issue—the territorial dispute between Israel and Palestine. The fundamental identity difference between Palestinian and Jewish Israeli women—as members of "occupied" and "occupying" collectives respectively—and their accompanying divergent experiences have not received sufficient attention. Cross-cultural dialogues between Israeli and Palestinian women often rest on the assumption that "shared female experiences can bridge political and cultural differences," but in practice, this assumption oversimplifies the distinct challenges faced by women in different cultural groups. Jewish Israeli women tend to focus on their own experiences of oppression and advocate for gender equality within peacebuilding, whereas Palestinian women view these dialogues as crucial platforms for achieving national liberation and addressing the issue of occupation (Sharoni, 2012). However, the emphasis on shared experiences often sidelines critical discussions around the central issue of "occupation," making it difficult for Palestinian women's needs to be met. This problem is further exacerbated by third-party facilitators, who may unintentionally prioritize Jewish Israeli women's perspectives, allowing them to dominate cross-cultural collaborations while relatively marginalizing Palestinian women's concerns (Sharoni, 2012). As a result, Palestinian women often express skepticism toward cross-cultural initiatives, perceiving them as failing to genuinely represent their interests or bring about meaningful change (Daniele, 2011).


This neglect of intersectional complexities undermines the potential for truly effective communication and cooperation between Israeli and Palestinian women. As the Oslo Accords entered their second decade, the vision of cross-cultural collaboration between Israeli and Palestinian women began to unravel. The outbreak of the Second Palestinian Intifada in 2000 led to the fragmentation of organizations such as Jerusalem Link, a situation that worsened after the 2008–2009 Gaza War, culminating in the formal dissolution of the Israel Women's Coalition (IWC) in 2011 (Richter-Devroe, 2018). To this day, peace remains elusive, and the separation wall built by Israel has made face-to-face exchanges between Israeli and Palestinian women even more difficult.

In the context of the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, the U.S. government's of

foreign policy stance and domestic governance


Beyond Israel and Palestine, the United States—as one of the most influential external actors in the Israeli-Palestinian conflict—exerts a profound impact on the situation through its foreign policy. At the same time, the lives of Palestinian, Arab, and Israeli diasporic communities in the United States are also affected by these foreign policies. In response to the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, the defining feature of the Biden administration's foreign policy has been its ambiguity. On key issues such as the future of Jerusalem, statehood, and Palestinian refugees, the Biden administration has not offered concrete solutions or goals. This approach differs from Obama's "New Beginning" initiative in Cairo or Trump's "Peace to Prosperity" plan. During the Trump administration, a fully pro-Israel policy led to the Palestinian withdrawal from peace negotiations, whereas the Biden administration has restored diplomatic relations with the Palestinians, attempting to bring them back to the negotiating table (Huczko, 2023). Meanwhile, following escalations in Gaza, the White House has criticized Israel's military operations there, accusing it of indiscriminate bombing. Yet, on the other hand, the Biden administration has declared that Israel's use of U.S. weapons does not constitute a violation of international law, continuing to supply Israel with weapons and aid. This ambiguous diplomatic strategy may leave minority communities, particularly Palestinian and Arab Americans, feeling that the government's stance fails to adequately address their needs and expectations (Warasti et al., 2022).


Amid heightened tensions in the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, legislative processes within the United States have also been affected—H.R.6408 being a representative example. Introduced in November 2023 and passed in mid-April this year with bipartisan support, the bill authorizes government officials, particularly the Secretary of the Treasury, to unilaterally revoke the tax-exempt status of nonprofit organizations deemed to support terrorism (Congress, 2023). The legislation is motivated by concerns that certain organizations, particularly American Muslims for Palestine, may indirectly support Hamas, as these groups are alleged to have links with the previously shut-down Holy Land Foundation. Supporters of the bill argue that it is necessary to prevent activities that use charity as a cover for supporting terrorism (Lehmann, 2024).


However, the implementation of H.R.6408 has also raised concerns about freedom of speech and potential abuse of power. Because the bill allows a single official to exercise significant discretionary authority without judicial oversight or standard legal procedures, it risks further marginalizing Muslim American communities (Lehmann, 2024). By labeling organizations and affecting their funding sources, this approach may intensify social distrust and prejudice against minority group members, thereby suppressing the public expression of groups advocating for Palestinian rights.


Five

Legal Policies and Social Change from a Gender Perspective


In contemporary international relations and social development, gender perspectives have become key factors in understanding legal policies and advancing social change. Women, especially ethnic minority women, face unique challenges and hardships during conflict and social upheaval. This section will explore in depth how their situation can be improved through gender-sensitive peace initiatives, social inclusion and multicultural education, and comprehensive legal protections. We will analyze the limitations of existing international frameworks, propose concrete reform recommendations, and emphasize the important role of diverse stakeholders in providing legal aid. Through these discussions, we aim to offer strong theoretical support and practical pathways toward achieving gender equality and social justice.



Gender-Sensitive Peace Initiatives

United Nations Security Council Resolution 1325 represents a major milestone in integrating gender perspectives into the peace and security agenda, particularly in response to the various hardships women face in conflict. This resolution and its follow-up resolutions form the four pillars of the "Women, Peace and Security Agenda": Participation, Protection, Prevention, and Relief and Recovery. This international framework has since been strengthened through a series of Security Council resolutions: UNSC Resolution 1820 recognized sexual violence as a weapon of war; UNSC Resolutions 1888 and 1889 established protection mechanisms; UNSC Resolutions 1960, 2106, and 2122 strengthened accountability; and UNSC Resolution 2242 integrated gender equality into emerging security issues.


However, these international frameworks face significant challenges in practice. Research shows that between 1992 and 2019, women's representation among negotiators, signatories, and mediators in peace processes never exceeded 13% (UN Women, 2021). Particularly within the Israeli-Palestinian context, female participation is even more strikingly low. This low participation reflects deep structural barriers, including sociocultural restrictions and institutional discrimination (Sharoni, 2012).


An important direction for policy reform is to establish "gender-equal participation" quotas to ensure women's voices in peace processes. Concrete measures could include setting clear gender participation targets and reserving seats for women representatives in peace negotiation teams (Sharoni, 2012). Research indicates that substantive female participation increases the durability of peace agreements by 35% (Krause et al., 2018). Gender perspectives should be integrated throughout the entire policy framework of peace initiatives, especially in the heavily conflict-affected Palestinian occupied territories. Establishing a gender-sensitive policy framework can effectively reduce oppression against women (Abdo & Lentin, 2002). For example, when designing reconstruction plans, special attention must be paid to women's unique needs in accessing resources and rebuilding their lives, ensuring that public resources benefit men and women equitably. Moreover, emphasizing the inclusion of gender issues in peace initiatives encourages policymakers to consider the long-term impacts of gender equality in planning and implementation. The United Nations and other international organizations play a crucial role in advancing gender-equality policies. For instance, UN Women can support women-led peace initiatives through dedicated funding, providing support in financial, technical, and policy dimensions for related research and practice. The establishment of special funds can ensure sustained financial support for women's groups and grassroots organizations in post-conflict reconstruction and mediation. In addition, international organizations can help minority women in Israel and Palestine enhance their influence in the struggle for equal rights through policy coordination (Holt & Jawad, 2013).



Social Inclusion and Multicultural Education

In conflict-affected areas, ethnic minority women often face social isolation and double oppression. Establishing support networks through community-led organizations can effectively help these women cope with the daily challenges posed by gender and racial discrimination. Community organizations can provide legal aid, employment counseling, and cultural adaptation training for ethnic minority women in Israel, helping them better integrate into local society. For ethnic minority women in Israel, such support networks not only offer emotional support but also strengthen their awareness of rights (Herzog, 2004).


Some successful examples include:




Jerusalem Link


The Jerusalem Link project, which promotes dialogue between Israeli and Palestinian women's organizations, helping both sides deepen understanding, build trust, and advance cooperation (Daniele, 2014).



Machsom Watch


The Machsom Watch organization, which deploys female volunteers to monitor checkpoints, documenting and reducing harassment incidents against Palestinian women, thereby creating a safer living environment for them.



 Coalition of Women for Peace


The Coalition of Women for Peace, which works through peace education and anti-militarization initiatives to break down hostile sentiments and promote social justice (Richter-Devroe, 2018).



Women Wage Peace


Women Wage Peace is another successful example. This grassroots organization, composed of Israeli and Palestinian women, is dedicated to advancing the peace process through nonviolent means. They organize demonstrations and petition campaigns, calling on political leaders to resume peace negotiations. The group's diverse membership structure itself serves as a powerful symbol, demonstrating courage and determination in bridging ethnic and cultural divides (Byrne, 2022).


Gender and racial discrimination in the workplace also need to be addressed through systematic inclusive policies to improve Israeli and Palestinian women's opportunities in education and employment (Erdreich & Rapoport, 2002; Herzog, 2004). Specifically, anti-discrimination regulations can be established, and gender equality training can be implemented in workplaces to raise gender sensitivity among managers and employees. To encourage employers to hire minority women, incentive mechanisms could be considered, such as tax reductions or subsidies for companies that employ women (Al-Haj, 2002). At the same time, establishing employment quotas for women and ethnic minorities can help guarantee women's right to employment to some extent, especially addressing the double inequality faced by minority women in the labor market.



Legal protection for minority women urgently needs improvement.

Currently, legal protection for minority women in Israel and the Palestinian territories is primarily reflected in the combination of domestic laws, international treaties, and human rights conventions. Although these legal frameworks aim to safeguard the basic rights of minority women, various challenges in implementation significantly undermine their protective effectiveness.


In anti-discrimination legislation, Israel's Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty, as a quasi-constitutional framework, ensures a certain level of equality. The Equal Opportunities in Employment Law (1988) prohibits discrimination based on race, religion, and gender, aiming to protect minority women—including Arab and Bedouin women—from workplace discrimination (Shalhoub-Kevorkian, 2012). However, despite these laws, systemic inequalities remain widespread. Minority women (such as Arab, Ethiopian, and Bedouin women) often face deep-rooted discrimination in the labor market, limiting their employment opportunities (Haj-Yahia, 2013). The Palestinian Basic Law emphasizes equality and anti-discrimination, but the lack of supporting detailed legislation results in insufficient enforcement in eliminating discrimination in employment and public life (UN Women, 2020).


Regarding employment equality, Israel's Women's Employment Law and related policies, along with various affirmative action plans introduced by government agencies, aim to promote employment opportunities for minority women, such as Arab women. However, the effectiveness of these measures is often unsatisfactory due to the impact of cultural discrimination, geographic isolation, and unequal access to education (Batshon, 2023). Although Palestine's Labor Law stipulates gender equality, there are significant gaps in enforcing equal pay and eliminating workplace discrimination. Key reasons for low labor force participation include sociocultural restrictions, economic instability, and lack of effective legal remedies (United Nations Economic and Social Commission for Western Asia, 2024).


In terms of legal protection against domestic violence, Israel provides protection orders and victim support services for domestic violence survivors under the Prevention of Family Violence Law (1991). However, due to cultural, linguistic, and social barriers, minority women often struggle to fully access these legal protections. Moreover, stigma within communities against reporting violence discourages them from seeking help. In Palestine, legal protection against domestic violence is limited, and existing protective measures are difficult to effectively implement under the influence of sociocultural norms. Although Palestine has ratified the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW), the practical effectiveness of protective measures is constrained by weak enforcement (United Nations, 2017).


International human rights treaties theoretically offer additional protection for minority women. Both Israel and Palestine have signed multiple treaties requiring the elimination of discrimination and the promotion of gender equality, such as the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW). However, both face practical challenges in translating these international obligations into effective domestic protections. Israel, despite having legal obligations, suffers from weak enforcement; Palestine, although having ratified numerous international agreements, sees these commitments weakened by a fragmented legal system and reliance on traditional customs (United Nations, 2017).


In summary, although the legal frameworks in Israel and Palestine formally provide a certain level of protection for minority women, they still face numerous obstacles in practice. Weak enforcement of anti-discrimination laws, difficulties in achieving employment equality, and limited access to effective help for domestic violence survivors all persist. The impact of international human rights treaties is also diminished by deficiencies in domestic legal systems. These issues highlight the necessity of comprehensive legal reform.



Reform recommendations to enhance legal protection for minority women

First, it is recommended to establish a multilingual legal aid system. Language barriers are a major factor preventing minority women from accessing legal support. Providing multilingual legal aid services—including Arabic, Hebrew, and English—can help them better understand their rights and participate in legal procedures. At the same time, legal documents should be simplified and made more readable to ensure that legal aid is accessible to minority women (Shalhoub-Kevorkian, 2009). Multilingual support would help overcome the language gap highlighted in the case of Samah Ismail.


Second, anti-discrimination training for law enforcement personnel must be strengthened. Bias among law enforcement officials can hinder minority women's access to justice. Therefore, mandatory anti-discrimination and gender sensitivity training should be provided for police, prosecutors, judges, and others to enhance their cultural sensitivity and ensure fairness in law enforcement. Training content should include combating racial and gender discrimination, respecting multiculturalism, and protecting the rights of vulnerable groups (Haj-Yahia, 2013). Fair law enforcement is key to breaking the cycle of minority women being unable to seek help.


Third, efforts should be made to improve the accessibility of legal aid. Providing more legal service options for minority women—such as mobile legal clinics and online consultation platforms—can help them overcome geographical and economic barriers to obtain necessary assistance. Collaborating with local community organizations and using women's mutual support networks to disseminate legal knowledge are also effective ways to enhance accessibility. Increasing funding for legal aid targeted at marginalized groups, and incorporating a gender perspective into both funding allocation and service delivery, is crucial for expanding coverage (Chopra & Isser, 2012).


Finally, establishing specialized courts or tribunals is another important measure. Specialized courts handling cases involving minority women—such as domestic violence and employment discrimination—can concentrate resources to provide more professional, efficient, and compassionate services. Such courts can accumulate expertise in handling these cases and help avoid potential biases in mainstream courts. For example, South Africa has established specialized sexual violence courts, where trial processes are smoother and victims report greater support (Department of Justice and Constitutional Development Republic of South Africa, 2013).



Multi-stakeholder participation is crucial for legal aid.

In providing legal aid to minority women, governments, non-governmental organizations (NGOs), and legal professionals such as lawyers must all play key roles.


Regarding government, the judiciary bears the responsibility of establishing a public legal aid system. However, in Israel and Palestine, legal aid services targeting minority groups remain weak. Israel's Ministry of Justice legal aid agency falls short in cultural sensitivity and service accessibility, making it difficult to effectively meet the needs of Palestinian women. Government agencies should increase investment, improve service delivery—such as establishing dedicated legal aid centers, offering multilingual services, simplifying application procedures—and strengthen training for legal aid personnel to enhance their cultural sensitivity and gender awareness. Furthermore, minority women's voices should be actively heard during policy-making to ensure their needs are fully considered (United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime, 2016).


NGO NGOs serve as important supplementary forces in legal aid. Through funding support and volunteer services, NGOs can provide culturally sensitive assistance. For example, organizations such as Women Against Violence focus on defending the rights of Arab women, offering legal counseling and representation while challenging patriarchal norms within families and communities, making them trusted allies for minority women. Supporting NGO development and encouraging their collaboration with government services is essential for building a comprehensive legal aid network.


International NGOs also play a unique role. As external actors, international NGOs can draw international attention to these issues and advocate for the implementation of international human rights standards locally. For instance, Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch reveal the legal challenges faced by minority women through field research and report publications, advocate for policy reforms, and seek international pressure for change. Collaboration between international and local NGOs helps advance solutions within the context of global feminism.
