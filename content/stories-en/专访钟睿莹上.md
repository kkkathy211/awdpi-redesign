<strong>"Caring about gender issues is not a choice that suddenly appeared one day, but rather a capacity gradually accumulated and internalized from my reading and emotional experiences during adolescence."</strong>

  

  

  

In this issue of the "Her Perspective Column," AWDPI invites Zhong Ruiying, who is currently pursuing a master's degree in Interdisciplinary Gender Studies at the University of Cambridge. Her research focuses on <strong>gender-based violence, cultural heritage, and collective trauma.</strong>

  

From her adolescence, <strong>reading books related to gender studies, </strong>to working at the London-based support organization Hestia Life Beyond Crisis managing domestic violence hotlines, <strong>and further supporting the Cape Town-based international organization Gender Rights In Tech by co-authoring support manuals for survivors of sexual violence, </strong>Zhong Ruiying has consistently practiced feminism while deepening her reflections on it.

  

Through her narrative, she shows us how she embarked on the path of gender studies, how she drew strength from feminist literature at different stages of life, and how she encountered and supported women of diverse nationalities and races through volunteer work.

  

This piece is the <strong>first part </strong>of a series. Zhong Ruiying shares with AWDPI the story of a girl passionate about literature and poetry who chose the path of feminism: we are not born knowing what "feminism" is; rather, at a certain moment, we are touched by a book, a passage, or an emotion, and then for the first time realize:

  

<strong>those indescribable feelings can indeed be articulated.</strong>



<strong>Trauma can be spoken of.</strong>

<strong>I first read Sylvia Plath when I was fourteen. </strong>Her poetry was so raw, so direct. Her sharp, incisive style cut through a misogynistic world filled with pretense and fantasy. She transformed her private traumatic experiences into poetic language. I was completely shaken by this confessional mode of writing.

  

Plath's uncompromising honesty allowed me, for the first time, to experience an emotional expression from a "female perspective." Women's long-ignored experiences, intense emotions, and subjectivity found a place in her poems.

  

At that time, I knew nothing about poetry, yet I immersed myself in her words almost every day. Strangely, what I felt from them was not only sadness, but also a powerful sense of vitality: <strong>apparently, some repression and unease could be understood and articulated. </strong>Narrating trauma is not merely a reenactment of pain; the more we confront our wounds, the more we find the strength to keep moving forward."

This realization sparked my strong interest in mainstream theories of understanding trauma during high school—particularly Freudian psychoanalysis. Yet when I read Freud, I felt a profound sense of distance, <strong>until Judith Herman's *Trauma and Recovery* helped me identify where this discomfort came from.</strong>

  

Psychoanalysis originated closely with trauma studies: at the end of the 19th century, Freud and Breuer gradually realized the impact of traumatic memories on psychological symptoms by listening to women diagnosed with "hysteria," nearly all of whom had experienced sexual violence from their families or society.

  

But soon, psychoanalysis abandoned this trauma-centered explanation: <strong>acknowledging these traumas as real would mean naming a kind of invisible violence, and the perpetrators belonged precisely to the respectable middle-class communities to which scholars themselves were attached.</strong>

  

Under political and social pressures, Freud and others turned to denying women's trauma, anger, and suffering, instead constructing psychological theories centered on <strong>men and heterosexuality, </strong>such as the "Oedipus complex" and "penis envy."

  

To me, such theories could not truly respond to the emotions and difficulties women face in real life.

  

However, I did not completely reject psychoanalytic theory; <strong>Freud made me realize that gender and psychology are not determined by biological nature, but are continuously constructed through desire, fantasy, and relationships with others."</strong>

My adolescent reading led me to ponder "how trauma can be spoken," but it was the Introduction to Gender Studies summer course at the University of Cambridge that truly moved this insight from emotional experience to theoretical reflection.

  

In that course, I first encountered <strong>"feminist psychoanalytic theory." </strong>It reworks Freud-centered psychoanalytic theory by reinserting traditional psychoanalytic insights about the psyche and social relationships back into women's experiences and conditions.

  

In my current understanding, feminist psychoanalytic approaches to trauma emphasize its <strong>"relational" </strong>nature. For example, Herman argues that trauma is not an isolated psychological event, but an experience systematically denied by society, and can only regain recognition through storytelling and witnessing by others.

  

Jessica Benjamin points out that <strong>the essence of trauma is a rupture in relationship—</strong>when a person is not recognized by others, their subjectivity is damaged. Therefore, healing trauma must also occur within new relationships, through "mutual recognition" to rebuild the self.

  

Reading these gender studies texts often brings me moments of sudden clarity. These moments are transformative, confirming for me that gender studies is the path I want to pursue. I fell in love with its quality of constantly challenging disciplinary boundaries and knowledge authority—it never treats theory as an endpoint, but repeatedly returns to lived experience and everyday life to understand and to change.

  


<strong>Seeking understanding and balance across different narratives</strong>

Feminist psychoanalysis opened up a theoretical lens for me to understand trauma and subjectivity. Yet, as an East Asian woman, I realized the intricate connections between different cultural contexts and feminism. Because of this, I began turning to writings by local feminist scholars. Professor Song Shaopeng was the first scholar who guided me back to the East Asian context.

I first encountered Professor Song Shaopeng's work in my second year of high school. At that time, the views among my peers could roughly be divided into two:

  

one was a patriarchal, traditional national narrative <strong>that tied women's value to family and national continuity;</strong>

  

the other was a neoliberal co-opted feminist discourse emphasizing "self-empowerment" and "free choice," <strong>yet often defining what a "modern woman" is by Western standards, failing to address the institutional and power structures that sustain inequality. This discourse carries, to a large extent, the colonial undertones of white feminism.</strong>

  

Both left me feeling lost at the time.

  

Then, I encountered Song Shaopeng's *China and Women Under the Western Gaze*. She revealed how China's modern women's liberation movement, from its inception, was entangled with national narratives: <strong>late Qing intellectuals, when introducing Western ideas, tightly linked women's issues with national issues. Women were called to become modern, yet their bodies and labor remained incorporated into the political framework of "saving the nation."</strong>

  

It was precisely with this historical perspective that I realized today's neoliberal and nationalist feminist discourses, though seemingly opposed, are in some ways structurally similar: <strong>one demands women to be "modern enough," the other demands them to be "sufficiently devoted"—both point toward the goal of the "ideal subject" as defined within the global order, with women serving as tools relied upon to enter colonial imaginaries.</strong>

  

As a Chinese woman studying in the UK, I am often framed by two gazes: in the West, I am expected to rebel against a certain "East Asian woman" image to gain permission to enter a "more modern" world; back home, I am expected to inherit traditional roles.

  

Yet, Professor Song Shaopeng taught me not to rush into choosing between <strong>"modern/backward" </strong>or <strong>"self/nation" </strong>binary opposition, but placing myself in the crack to observe and question. This has gradually enabled me to approach my identity conflicts and multiple marginalizations with greater composure.

  


<strong>Returning from abstract theoretical speculation to a "tender dialogue" with the world  
</strong>


  

The critical frameworks built through reading <strong>are only truly tested and expanded when they collide with real life. </strong>In discussing feminism with the outside world, I have encountered some skepticism, which can roughly be divided into two types.

  
The first comes from men, <strong>who often feel that terms like "patriarchy" and "feminism" carry hostility, as if personally accusing them. </strong>But patriarchy is actually a historical and social structure that not only oppresses women, but also forces men to constantly prove their "masculinity," burdening them with heavy expectations and anxiety. When they realize this is a structure that constrains all genders, dialogue becomes smoother.

  

The second type of skepticism comes more from female friends. When I raise certain critical viewpoints, the other person often feels uneasy. For example, when I discuss whether the institution of marriage should be abolished, some friends in stable heterosexual relationships become anxious about their own choice to embrace heterosexuality, fearing they might be seen as "not feminist enough."

  

I acknowledge that as an academic researcher, I sometimes overlook the vast gap between theory and reality. In many debates and conversations, I have regretted certain things I've said and reflected on how I could do better. I believe <strong>only through mutual respect and </strong><strong>empathetic communication can critique truly unfold. Genuine critique maintains its sharpness while making people feel understood and invited into reflection.</strong>

  

For me, feminism has never been a label, but a continuous way of learning, thinking, and practicing. Every book, every conversation, every moment of being misunderstood or understood, is part of how I come to understand the world.

  

Today, when I look back at those moments that led me toward gender studies, I realize:

  

<strong>every ending I once thought was final was never truly the end. Perhaps the feminism I practice is an ever-expanding, endless journey. All I can do is listen, think critically, act, and gently engage in dialogue with the world.</strong>
