From May to July this year, two trending incidents thrust "covert filming and rights protection" into the spotlight: Shen, a student at Shanghai University of Political Science and Law, was administratively detained and expelled for secretly filming in a restroom partition at a study room; meanwhile, stand-up comedian Tang Xiangyu was ruled to have infringed on reputation rights for her 2023 post denouncing a peer's covert filming and other "sexual harassment" behaviors, and after refusing to apologize—claiming she accepted the penalty but not the wrongdoing—was subject to court-enforced execution, sparking the topic #TangXiangyuRefusesToApologizeAndIsForcedToComply#. One incident distant, the other close; one punished, the other accused—this contrast not only outlines the hidden struggles women face in public spaces, but also pierces at the painful reality where "those who speak out end up in the defendant's seat."


These are not minor waves of public opinion that quietly fade once judicial rulings are issued. On the contrary, they are like a hammer striking open long-suppressed social emotions.


On social media platforms, countless women's comments contain no lofty narratives, only densely packed real-life experiences: some were followed closely on escalators in subway stations, some discovered phones aimed at their skirts in library study rooms, some saw a trembling lens above the partition in public restrooms.


People standing behind Tang Xiangyu are essentially expressing the same stance: that "refusing to apologize" is not defiance against the law, but the final cry of an individual who has been violated by secret filming and repeatedly battered by the struggle to seek justice.


Public opinion is never one-sided. Amid the overwhelming wave of support, there are still piercing sneers and sarcasm:


"Being filmed once won't make you lose a piece of flesh—does it really matter?"


"Dressed so lightly, aren't you asking for others to look?"

"Always labeling things as harassment—aren't you being too sensitive?"




This intense cognitive divide reveals a reality: secret filming is no longer an isolated, occasional malicious act. It is a structural predicament silently spreading through public spaces, constantly eroding women's sense of safety.


We push "anger" one step further, using law and international experience to seriously discuss "What can be done after being secretly filmed?".



Secret filming, rooted in the male gaze, is a form of compensating for power in lived experience.


Let us revisit the origins and development of the Tang Xiangyu incident.


● In December 2023, Aikebai'er A'erken (Ai Ke), a stand-up comedian from Shanghai Moduo Comedy, was accused of secretly photographing a female performer wearing shorts backstage and making comments such as "She wears shorts—her beautiful legs stir desire" in a WeChat group for male comedians.

● Tang Xiangyu was not a direct victim, but she joined several other female performers in publishing a long post titled "Named Resistance: Reject Sexual Harassment, Reject Victim-Blaming," naming Ai Ke directly.

● The court ultimately ruled that existing evidence supported Ai Ke's inappropriate behavior and speech, but was insufficient to constitute "sexual harassment" under judicial definitions; Tang Xiangyu's direct use of terms like "sexual harassment" and "habitual offender" exceeded the boundaries of reasonable criticism and thus constituted defamation.


As sociologist Laura Mulvey noted in her discussion of the "male gaze": The subject establishes absolute visual dominance by "visualizing" the object.


Women's anger toward secret filming is never just about a single photo, but about the pervasive sense of "violation." In the vast majority of secret filming cases, the gendered opposition between perpetrator and victim is no coincidence. While secret filming is certainly driven by voyeuristic desires and illegal profits, its common psychological motivations often go far beyond sexuality: it can become a delusional compensation for some people facing real-life powerlessness.


As sociologist Michel Foucault noted in his discussion of "surveillance and power": The experience of control does not necessarily come from overt authority, but sometimes precisely from "observing from the shadows while the other remains unaware."


Through this extremely low-cost boundary violation, secret filmers briefly steal a sense of superiority in their own lives. They reduce women's bodies into "trophies" that can be detached and consumed at will, using these hidden acts to compensate for their own everyday powerlessness.



Women's anger is not only about personal privacy being trampled, but also about realizing their daily lives have been reduced to cheap tools for others to soothe their petty insecurities—or even to be packaged and sold.

Do not use the lightness of punishment as self-comfort—administrative cases are the foundation of criminal cases. Many secret filming incidents only qualify as criminal when aggravating factors such as "repeated acts," "distribution," or "profit-making" accumulate, and the starting point of all this is your reporting the incident.



When the world uses criminal law to target secret filmers


If we look overseas, we find that recent legislative trends in many countries and regions are highly consistent: "upskirting" is being redefined from a general public order offense to a sexual crime or specific criminal offense. What we need is support strong enough to serve as a deterrent.

We need sharper laws, and we also need a more united us.


Tang Xiangyu's choice to "accept penalty but not fault" in the face of enforced execution is touching, but also heartbreaking. If a society puts those who expose secret filmers in the defendant's seat, we must ask: why?


Compared to Singapore's caning or South Korea's five-year detention, we indeed need clearer, more explicit laws to regulate society. But before legal changes come, what we can do is firmly grasp every existing legal tool: reporting to police, collecting evidence, filing civil claims, lodging platform complaints, organizing solidarity.


Every serious police report increases the cost for secret filmers; every successful civil claim paves the way for silent victims.


The administrative detention and expulsion of Shen from Shanghai University of Political Science and Law is a small victory, and it is a starting point, not an end.


As UK legislative advocate Gina Martin said:

"We must build a picture of how frequently secret filming occurs, because it happens far too often."


Every complete piece of evidence, every careful yet firm public statement, adds an indispensable piece to this picture.


If one day, that despicable camera lens happens to point at you or someone close to you, please remember:


You did nothing wrong.

You don't need to feel ashamed, nor should you remain trapped in helpless rage.

Stay calm, and use evidence and law to tear apart the disguises in the shadows.


Every rational counterattack

is fighting for all women

to claim a safer public space.


When the lens points at someone's skirt,

behind that lens will also be a pair of eyes watching him.


That can be you, me, or all of us.
