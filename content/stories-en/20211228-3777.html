Part 01. Introduction


One in three women globally will experience physical or sexual violence by an intimate partner or sexual violence by a non-partner in their lifetime—a figure that has remained largely unchanged over the past decade, the World Health Organization and UN Women pointed out in a joint report released this March. The outbreak of the novel coronavirus has further worsened the global situation of domestic violence.


In France, reports of domestic violence increased since the lockdown began on March 17, 2020; 30%in Cyprus and Singapore, helpline calls increased by 30%and 33%respectively; in Argentina, since the lockdown began on March 20, 2020, emergency calls related to domestic violence increased by 25%. Canada, Germany, Spain, the UK, and the US have all reported increases in domestic violence cases and demand for emergency shelters.


No matter where they live in the world, if women or girls have family members or intimate partners with violent tendencies, movement restrictions, economic pressures, and psychological disturbances brought by the pandemic may intensify perpetrators' violent behaviors. The invisible time bomb of domestic violence has quietly exploded around the world.

Part 02. Main Body


(I)

How has the pandemic increased the risk of violence against women overseas?


1. Reduced attention. During the pandemic, public attention may be overwhelmingly focused on the virus itself. How many new cases today? Which area is locked down next? Attention and protection for women experiencing violence are correspondingly weakened.

 

2. Isolation measures. With stay-at-home orders in place, family members spend more time in close contact, and victims may suffer more severe violence from perpetrators. During isolation, victims may be unable to access phones or the internet due to prolonged cohabitation with abusers, losing contact with the outside world and making escape even harder. Perpetrators may also control access to news or media, becoming the sole source of information.

 

3. Economic exploitation. Under the pandemic's impact, rising unemployment in many countries leaves affected women economically dependent on abusers and unable to leave—for example, they may not afford extra hotel costs or living alone. Perpetrators may further exploit victims financially.

 

4. The pandemic has weakened social support systems for domestic violence survivors. For example, staff reductions and lower efficiency in domestic violence support organizations worldwide; shelters, hotlines, and legal aid services may be scaled back; hotels and guesthouses may suspend operations; and in some severely affected countries, courts may offer only online services—these factors further hinder abused women from receiving help.


Image source: rawpixels


UN Secretary-General António Guterres expressed deep concern about this issue as early as early April last year. He said: "We know that lockdowns and quarantines are essential to controlling the virus, but they can trap women with abusers... Healthcare providers and police are overwhelmed and understaffed. Local support groups are paralyzed or underfunded. Some domestic violence shelters have closed, while others are overcrowded. I urge all governments to prevent and address violence against women. "

(II)

How victims can help themselves during the pandemic


1. Self-rescue at the moment of violence: Immediately retreat into a room and lock the door; then call the police or contact a domestic violence support organization. Keep police documentation, including incident reports, call receipts, and interrogation records.


2. Collect evidence promptly: Use your phone to record videos and take photos of your injuries, and inform trusted friends.


3. Set up a "safe room" at home: If an argument occurs, identify a room in the house without sharp objects. For some victims, especially those isolated with abusers during the pandemic, no place may be truly safe—so this room is only relatively safe. Try to identify a few lower-risk areas in the home.


4. To avoid being abused again after self-rescue: If possible, victims should move out of the current residence and live separately from the abuser. Consider staying temporarily at a domestic violence shelter, but keep your location confidential. Alternatively, contact relatives or friends to stay at their homes, or stay in a hotel.


5. Apply for a "Personal Safety Protection Order": To prevent possible (continued) domestic violence, applicants can request the court to restrict the respondent's behavior, obtaining protection from any or specific forms of violence.


6. Victims injured by domestic violence should seek medical help promptly: Immediately call the police or emergency medical services, or go to a hospital with assistance from domestic violence support staff. Alternatively, go to a hospital independently if possible. Be sure to keep medical records and injury assessments throughout: including medical charts, injury reports, forensic evaluation documents, and medical bills.


Image source: rawpixels



Additional tips:

1. Agree on a "code word" with at least two trusted friends: When in trouble, ask them to help using a pre-arranged code. Clearly define what they should do when you send the code. Discuss an escape plan in advance and identify which trusted relatives or friends you could stay with if you flee.


2. Prepare an emergency kit: It should ideally contain a spare set of keys, your clothes, and copies of important documents.


3. Take photos of important documents and send backups to trusted friends or family: Important documents include: ID cards, social security cards, birth certificates, medical insurance information, and proof of relationship with the abuser, such as a marriage certificate. When sending files via phone or computer, use email accounts and platforms the abuser cannot access.

(III)

Practices adopted by some countries in response to the pandemic


Some countries and regions have adapted to pandemic-related isolation by implementing measures that may reduce domestic violence. For example, France has set up alert systems in grocery stores and pharmacies, allowing domestic violence victims to discreetly alert authorities; victims can also use pre-established code words to signal staff for help. The U.S. National Domestic Violence Hotline continues to offer services via online text chat, enabling victims to seek help discreetly.


Image source: rawpixels


(IV)

Summary 


Before the pandemic, 243 million women and girls globally had experienced abuse by an intimate partner in the past year. The pandemic has further intensified violence, making it harder for many social service organizations to provide adequate support and making it more difficult for victims to access help.


This pandemic is a test for global gender equality efforts. In current and future pandemics, community workers, media, doctors, police, and others must work more closely together to address gender-based violence.


Ending violence against women is everyone's responsibility!
