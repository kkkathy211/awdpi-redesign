Introduction

According to the United Nations Human Development Report, in terms of gender equality rankings, Singapore ranked 13th out of 155 countries and regions evaluated, placing first among Asian countries. However, gender equality does not mean the elimination of domestic violence. According to statistics from Singapore's Ministry of Social and Family Development, between 2012 and 2016, domestic violence against women accounted for 74%–76%, while domestic violence against men accounted for 24%–25% during the same period.

One: Current Social Situation of Domestic Violence in Singapore


01

Domestic Violence During the Pandemic


Prolonged cohabitation and economic pressures became triggers for domestic violence during the pandemic. In 2020, Singapore police reported 5,135 domestic violence cases, of which 1,115 were referred to family violence service centers and protection centers—an increase of 24% compared to 2018. The Senior Minister of State said, from April to December 2020, domestic violence cases rose by 10%, while consultations related to domestic violence increased by nearly 60% (Villar, 2020)..


In addition, some community service centers in Singapore pointed out that foreign spouses experiencing domestic violence are often afraid to report their abusers due to concerns about their visa status. Especially during the pandemic, home quarantine and other control measures have left victims of domestic violence and their children trapped in a situation where they cannot seek help—unable to access legal aid or confide in friends and family nearby..



02

The Impact of Immigration Trends on Domestic Violence


According to a report by Singapore's Lianhe Zaobao, in the past two years, there has been a significant increase in new immigrants seeking help at PAVE (Promoting Alternatives to Violence) Centre, rising from 30 the year before last to 55 last year, but only about 20% sought assistance voluntarily. These 85 individuals came from 14 countries, with the largest number being new immigrants from China, followed by India, Malaysia, and the Philippines. Among them, 13 are now Singapore citizens, 56 are Singapore permanent residents, and the remaining 16 hold long-term social visit passes. The new immigrants seeking help from PAVE include both abusers and victims, with victims predominantly female—among the 71 victims, only one was male; additionally, 14 male abusers sought counseling..


PAVE primarily handles domestic violence cases between spouses. At the first Family Violence Symposium co-organized by the Asia Research Institute at the National University of Singapore and PAVE on October 21, 2010, PAVE senior social worker Lim Kiong Yeo and social worker Audrey presented a paper titled "Breaking Barriers: Understanding the Help-Seeking Behaviors and Obstacles of Local New Immigrants Affected by Family Violence," which for the first time revealed the behaviors and barriers faced by new immigrants trapped in domestic violence when seeking help.


PAVE receives an average of 250 new cases annually. A few years ago, after noticing an increasing trend in cases involving new immigrants, PAVE began detailed categorization of such cases starting in 2008. The term "new immigrant cases" refers to cases where one or both spouses are new immigrants, although PAVE does not further specify how many of the help-seekers are foreign brides married into Singapore.


The number of new immigrants affected by domestic violence is increasing, partly due to the significant rise in new immigrants, and partly possibly because their awareness of domestic violence is growing.


According to the 2010 census, Singapore had 3.23 million citizens, 540,000 permanent residents, and approximately 1.31 million non-residents living locally holding work permits, employment passes, long-term social visit passes, or student passes. Only 77.2% of Singapore citizens and permanent residents were born in Singapore. Among non-locally born residents, the largest group was born in Malaysia, followed by those from mainland China, Hong Kong, and Macau; the third-largest group came from India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Sri Lanka.


03

Measures to Address Domestic Violence


In response to domestic violence within Singapore, the government has implemented a series of policies, including legal and community support measures. At the legal level, Singapore enacted the Women's Charter in 1961, providing legal protection for women to safeguard their rights and interests. Subsequently, as social conditions evolved, the Singapore government revised the Women's Charter in 1997, incorporating domestic violence into the Charter and establishing clear definitions and penalties for such acts. The revised Charter stipulates that any act by a family member against another family member—including intentional harm, intentionally or attempting to cause fear of harm, unlawful imprisonment, or harassment—constitutes domestic violence. For example, if a husband physically assaults his wife, upon her complaint, he may be fined up to SGD 2,000 (approximately SGD 1.5 to USD 1) or imprisoned for up to six months, or both. For repeat offenders, the penalty may be a fine of up to SGD 5,000 or imprisonment for up to 12 months, or both. In 1995, Singapore also established the Family Court to better handle family disputes and, when necessary, issue special protection orders prohibiting abusive husbands from entering the home. At the community level, under joint promotion by the Singapore government and community organizations, Singapore established the National Family Violence Networking System. This system facilitates collaboration between government and non-governmental organizations in handling domestic violence cases and promotes domestic violence prevention efforts within communities. Additionally, Singapore has implemented mandatory counseling programs for both abusers and victims, which begin upon the issuance of a Personal Protection Order (Zhu Yuhong & Chen Gaoling, 2014).


Singapore currently experiences about 2,000 domestic violence incidents annually, with spousal violence accounting for 90% of domestic violence cases. According to a recent survey conducted in Singapore, an increasing number of men are also applying for Personal Protection Orders from the Family and Juvenile Justice Centre due to domestic violence. In 2004, the Centre received 2,522 protection order applications, of which 450 were from men, accounting for 18%, compared to only 8% in 1998. Men apply for protection for various reasons, including preventing physical abuse by their wives or being assaulted by siblings or other family members. In a random sample of 1,918 cases, most were related to spouses, with half of the couples married for over 10 years. Among applicants for protection orders, the majority were ethnic Chinese, with 37% aged between 30 and 39.


On October 21, 2010, Singapore's Ministry of Community Development, Youth and Sports announced the launch of a revised version of the Comprehensive Management Guidelines on Family Violence. At the National Family Violence Conference, Minister Vivian Balakrishnan stated that the revised Guidelines include new chapters for institutions such as community courts, schools, and integrated medical clinics, ensuring better coordination among agencies in handling domestic violence cases and providing timely protection and support to affected family members.


To support government initiatives, some civil society women's organizations in Singapore have taken active steps, working closely with the government or government-linked organizations to implement concrete actions against domestic violence and protect women's rights. In addition to official women's organizations, non-governmental women's groups in Singapore are also involved in addressing domestic violence issues. For example, the Singapore Association of Women Lawyers (SAWL) aims to prevent and reduce domestic violence incidents and encourages the provision of direct services to victims of domestic violence.



Two: Root Cause Analysis of Domestic Violence in Singapore


01

Historical Causes of Domestic Violence in Singapore


After Singapore's independence in 1965, the country faced a series of social and economic challenges, especially food shortages. In 1966, the Singapore government introduced a population control policy. Ten years after its implementation, by 1977, Singapore's total fertility rate rapidly dropped to 1.82, falling below replacement level for the first time. Later, the government recognized the need to adjust its population policy and introduced its first pro-natalist policy in 1987. The rising median age reflects an overall increase in the age of women of childbearing age. Generally, women aged 20–35 are considered the primary childbearing group; as the size of this group significantly declines, the general fertility rate also drops sharply, creating a negative feedback loop.


Presser and Baldwin argue that women who are unable to work due to lack of childcare have relatively lower fertility intentions (Presser & Baldwin, 1980), which often leads to dissatisfaction from the male head of household and results in domestic violence. At the same time, although women's economic status has improved, the traditional family norm that women bear primary responsibility for housework and childcare has not changed (STIER H, LEWIN-EPSTEIN N, 2007). The lack of gender-equitable family-friendly policies, leading to conflict between women's work and family responsibilities, is also a potential cause of domestic violence (MCDONALD P. Gender equity, 2000).. In response to these issues, family-friendly policies promoting gender equality are seen as an effective approach in low-fertility societies to balance economic development with increased fertility rates; public policies emphasizing gender equality can help alleviate the conflict between women's work and family roles (Ji Yingchun & Zheng Zhenzhen, 2018).


02

Social Causes of Domestic Violence in Singapore


1. Relationship Between Women's Rights and State Patriarchy

Patriarchy, also known as male dominance, overlaps in academic definition with terms such as male supremacy, masculinism, paternal authority, and patriarchal system, and these expressions are used interchangeably to refer to men's dominant privileges within the family and society. State patriarchy refers to the implementation of patriarchy through state ideology, manifesting both in the nationwide reach of patriarchal influence and in the state itself being the agent enforcing patriarchy. This means state patriarchy is extensive and coercive in nature, making it impossible for women to overturn their social roles through individual efforts. Currently, all nation-states in the world fall under state patriarchy, and Singapore's state patriarchy is the primary social cause behind changes in women's rights and the issue of domestic violence.


As a typical patriarchal state, Singapore upholds the principles of "state above all" and "family as the root," with changes in women's rights remaining under government control.. State patriarchy establishes a hierarchical power structure in which gender, class, and race jointly constitute its elements. Under state patriarchy, "the state not only finds it difficult to treat women equally, but also constructs relationships between men and women to varying degrees. In many ways, the exercise of state power serves to strengthen control over women" (Robin Cohen, 2001). State patriarchy elevates women's roles from family and clan control to state-level control, inheriting yet differentiating itself from private (household) patriarchy: the hallmark of private patriarchy is the dominance of patriarchal relations within the family, while public (state) patriarchy is dominated by employment and the state. The mode of deprivation in the former is individual—deprivation by a husband or father; the mode in the latter is collective, resulting from the shared actions of many men. The dominant strategy of the former can be summarized as exclusion—excluding women from public sphere activities and thus confining them to the household; the dominant strategy of the latter is segregation—allowing women to enter all domains, yet isolating them within those domains and maintaining their subordinate status (Sylvia Walby, 2004).


From a gender perspective, Singapore's state patriarchy, with its "family as the root" principle, inherits household patriarchy, as emphasized by the government, "the husband is the pillar and core of the family, while the wife is the husband's helper," (Sun Xiaoying, 1995). Therefore, the Singaporean government's first positioning of women is as "virtuous wives and good mothers": taking care of the family and supporting their husbands. Meanwhile, "state above all" demands dedication to the nation, so the second positioning of women is to contribute to national economic growth. Shaped by both modern and traditional expectations, Singaporean women are expected to fulfill traditional roles of caring for the family and raising children, while also participating in national economic development.



2. Political voicelessness

Women's groups were established to mobilize female voters to support the party, not to strengthen women's political rights, and female candidates have remained conspicuously absent. In response to a journalist's question in 1980, Goh Chok Tong, then chairman of the People's Action Party's election committee, replied: "Can you find a woman who has the same qualities as a man, is as capable as a man, and whose husband, fiancé, or boyfriend would agree to her taking up such a dangerous career?" (Jenny, 1993). In Goh's view, a woman could only enter politics if she were as capable as a man and had the consent of her husband or boyfriend. The wave of democratization has generated calls for more female parliamentarians; Prime Minister Lee Hsien Loong expressed willingness to nominate more female candidates, yet repeatedly stated, "It is more difficult to integrate women into politics than men, and with her own career and her responsibilities as a wife and mother, it is difficult for a woman to meet the demands of being a Member of Parliament" (Wil Burghoorn et al., 2008). " The "virtuous wife and good mother" role positioning hinders women's opportunities to participate in politics. .


III. Channels for Seeking Help on Domestic Violence in Singapore


In response to the actual circumstances of different victims, Singapore's official court website (Singapore Court) provides detailed guidance and self-protection advice for seeking help, which other countries can learn from.


01

Reporting to the Police for Assistance


The most direct way to seek help when experiencing domestic violence is to report it to the police, especially in the following emergency situations:

The abuser has a prior record of domestic violence or intentional harm;

The abuser uses knives or other harmful instruments to commit or threaten domestic violence;

The abuser's words or actions are becoming increasingly aggressive.



In addition to providing testimony to the police, victims can also prepare the following materials in advance:

① Records of the abuser's violent behavior;

② Testimonies from other family members;

③ Photos of injuries or medical records resulting from domestic violence.


02

Domestic violence among family members

--- Applying for a Personal Protection Order


After reporting to the police, the most common next step is to apply for a Personal Protection Order (PPO).


Victims can apply for a Personal Protection Order (PPO), Expedited Order (EO), Domestic Exclusion Order (DEO), or Counselling Order through the Family Protection Centre (FPC) by following the steps provided at the link: https://www.judiciary.gov.sg/family/how-to-file-personal-protection-order-application. The PPO applies to all Singapore citizens, including permanent residents and individuals settled in Singapore. Any victim of domestic violence aged 21 or above is eligible to apply for a PPO. If the victim is under 21, a family member, guardian, or the Ministry of Social and Family Development may apply on the victim's behalf.


Among these, the Personal Protection Order prohibits the abuser from using violence; the Expedited Order provides emergency protection effective immediately upon application before the court hearing; the Domestic Exclusion Order prohibits the abuser from entering the home and approaching the victim; and the Counselling Order mandates counselling for the abuser and the family. If the abuser violates the order and continues to commit domestic violence after the Personal Protection Order is issued, the act will be recorded. Victims can pursue criminal liability by reporting to the police, leading to fines of up to SGD 2,000 and/or imprisonment of up to 6 months; repeat offenders may be fined up to SGD 5,000 and/or imprisoned for up to 12 months. The application process does not require a lawyer, and the filing fee is only SGD 1, providing practical support for full-time women lacking financial resources.


03

Domestic violence between non-family members


In specific circumstances, domestic violence may also occur between non-family members, such as between domestic workers and employers or between unmarried couples. In such cases, Singapore's legal system can also protect victims, but not through applying for a Personal Protection Order (PPO). Instead, victims may file a Magistrate's Complaint. If unsure whether the complaint procedure is appropriate, a pre-case assessment can also be conducted. Before filing a complaint, you need to prepare the following documents:

① Identity card or passport;

② Police report or report reference number;

③ Relevant documents, such as medical reports or screenshots of messages reflecting the facts of domestic violence.


04

Assistance from Other Organizations


1. Association of Women for Action and Research (AWARE Singapore)

AWARE is a key organization in Singapore advocating for women's rights and gender equality. Victims can call the hotline 1800-777-5555 from 10 a.m. to 6 p.m. on weekdays to book counseling and legal aid services from AWARE.


2. Singapore Association of Women Lawyers (SAWL)

The Singapore Association of Women Lawyers provides free legal advisory services to female victims of domestic violence. Victims can call (+65) 6837 0611 to consult and find out whether they qualify for free legal services.
