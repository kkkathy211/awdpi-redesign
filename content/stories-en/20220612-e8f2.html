 Background:

The Philippines is one of the countries with the most severe domestic violence situations in the world. According to preliminary findings from the 2017 National Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS, 2018), a quarter (26%) of married women aged 15–49 have experienced physical, sexual, or emotional violence from their husbands or partners. One-fifth (20%) of women have experienced emotional violence, 14% have experienced physical violence, and 5% have suffered sexual violence from their current or most recent husband or partner. This report will analyze the current situation of domestic violence against women in the Philippines, explore how Philippine laws and institutions define and handle domestic violence, and outline pathways for social assistance or self-help after experiencing domestic violence, aiming to provide useful information for those in need.

I

Current Situation of Domestic Violence Against Women in the Philippines


1

Current Status of Domestic Violence


To describe the overall situation of domestic violence against women in the Philippines, this section will analyze data from the 2017 National Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS) conducted by the Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA), along with data from PSA press releases, interpreting the prevalence of domestic violence among Philippine women across dimensions of marital status, wealth status, and regional differences.


(1) Overview

The 2017 NDHS is a nationally representative survey covering nearly 31,000 households and 25,000 women aged 15–49. The survey was conducted from August 14 to October 27, 2017. According to the preliminary findings of the 2017 NDHS, approximately one-quarter (26%) of married women aged 15–29 have experienced physical, sexual, or emotional violence from their husbands or partners. Currently married women were asked about violence committed by their current husband or partner, while formerly married women were asked about violence by their most recent husband or partner. Data differences based on age gap are not significant, whereas differences by marital status, wealth status, and region are more pronounced.


(2) Differences by Marital Status

53.4% of women who are divorced, separated, or widowed have experienced physical, psychological, or sexual violence from a partner, compared to only 24.4% among currently married women in the Philippines; the proportion of divorced, separated, or widowed women experiencing sexual or physical violence is about three times that of currently married women, and the proportion experiencing both physical and sexual violence is five times higher; overall, divorced, separated, or widowed women report significantly higher rates of violence from former husbands or partners than currently married women.


(PSA, 2018)


(3) Differences by Wealth Status

In terms of wealth status, household income shows an inverse relationship with domestic violence, with lower-income households more likely to experience domestic violence. Among the lowest-income households, 31.6% of women reported experiencing partner violence, compared to only 18.3% in the highest-income households, showing a general downward trend. Statistically, lower-income households are more prone to family conflict, leading to greater harm to women from their partners.


(PSA, 2018)


(4) Regional Differences

In terms of urban-rural differences, reported domestic violence rates in cities and rural areas are not significantly different; regionally, the Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao reported the lowest rate of domestic violence, at only 6.7% of women, followed by Central Luzon (15.4%); the National Capital Region (15.6%) and the Cordillera Administrative Region (16.0%) are similar to Central Luzon; in contrast, the Caraga region has the highest reported rate of domestic violence in the Philippines, with 51.8% of married women reporting physical, emotional, or sexual violence from their partners; the Bicol Region (43.4%), Zamboanga Peninsula (43.4%), and Eastern Visayas (43.2%) also show extremely severe levels of domestic violence against women.


(PSA, 2018)


2

 Trends in Domestic Violence Since the Pandemic 


In 2021, UNFPA, UN Women, and Quilt.AI released a survey report that used big data from search engines to examine detailed reports of violence against women during pandemic lockdowns in eight Southeast Asian countries, including the Philippines. The following analysis is based on this report, but it should be noted that the survey's completeness is compromised by the limited digital skills of the target population, as the report itself points out: "Digital literacy skills must be provided to vulnerable groups to increase the use of online tools." Considering the Philippines' economic conditions and current situation, the survey is likely biased, excluding many women who cannot access social media, thus rendering them "invisible" in the data. Correspondingly, the PSA survey also shows a positive correlation between poverty level and severity of domestic violence, and given that those with online access tend to be better off economically, the actual level of violence experienced by women in the Philippines during the COVID-19 pandemic may be far higher than what the data reveals..


The report indicates that in all eight countries, reported cases of domestic violence increased significantly during or immediately after lockdowns; additionally, "from October 2019 to September 2020, search volumes related to physical violence (including 'signs of physical abuse,' 'violent relationship,' 'facial scratches') increased by 47%, 63%, and 55% in Malaysia, the Philippines, and Nepal, respectively" (UNFPA et al., 2021); for the Philippines, from April to September 2020, the main keywords with increased searches related to violence against women (VAW) included "how to stop domestic violence," "raped," "sexual assault," "OWWA (Overseas Workers Welfare Administration) hotline," "whipped with a belt," and "emotionally abusive husband," indicating that domestic violence not only increased during the pandemic but also constituted the majority of all VAW-related cases.


(UNFPA et al., 2021)


The report notes that as online misogyny intensified during lockdowns, supportive statements toward victims also increased. However, in the case of the Philippines, the situation is worse: the increase rate of misogynistic content ranked second among the eight countries (953%), a significant gap behind Thailand (22,384%) but still about 5–45 times higher than other surveyed countries; even more striking, Malaysia saw a decrease (-19%) in online misogynistic content during lockdowns; meanwhile, the Philippines was the only country where supportive statements toward victims decreased during the lockdown period. Although the survey focused on misogyny, it indirectly reflects the reasons behind the worsening domestic violence situation in the Philippines during the pandemic.


(UNFPA et al., 2021)


In terms of women's help-seeking behavior, the Philippines shows a broader range of measures compared to other countries. During the pandemic, the most increased keywords related to women seeking help in the Philippines were "women's hotline," and most related search terms did not specify the type of assistance needed, such as "women's hotline number" or "women's help center." This suggests that women in the Philippines face not only domestic violence but also other forms of violence. Ironically, compared to other countries, women in the Philippines have slightly better access to help: as the report notes, "local support services do not always appear in search results: top search engine results (when searching for services) vary by country. In some countries, the top results lead to relevant local services and information, while in others, the top results are foreign information. For example, in the Philippines, the most common help-seeking searches yield local hotlines from government and other local organizations. In Bangladesh, results are a mix of Indian and Western locations, with only a few local ones. In Indonesia, most results are foreign resources, unhelpful to Indonesian women."

Domestic Violence Assistance Mechanisms


1

 Definition of Domestic Violence in Philippine Law 


Philippine laws related to women's rights protection and preventing violence against women (VAW) include Republic Act No. 9262 of 2004 ("The Anti-Violence Against Women and Their Children Act of 2004") and Republic Act No. 9710 of 2009 ("The Magna Carta of Women"). Among these, Republic Act No. 9262 provides a more detailed definition of violence and focuses more on violence within the family context. Therefore, the following section will primarily define domestic violence based on Republic Act No. 9262, supplemented by additional descriptions of women's rights from Republic Act No. 9710 of 2009.


(1) Definition of Victims of Violence

Under Republic Act No. 9262 in 2004, victims are defined as "the wife, former wife, a woman with whom the offender has or had a sexual or dating relationship, a woman with whom the offender has a common child; or his child, whether legitimate or illegitimate." This means that the local definition of domestic violence extends beyond marital relationships or the duration of marriage, also covering periods before and after marriage. In textual terms, the recognition of "violence against women and their children" (VAW) requires that the parties must have had or currently have a relationship. Republic Act No. 9710 in 2009 provided a more comprehensive description of VAW, expanding the scope of violence victims to all women,; it should be noted that this difference in coverage is not due to a gap in the earlier law, but because lawmakers believed that women not in a relationship who suffer violence can be protected through basic tort provisions. Therefore, Republic Act No. 9262 specifically emphasized women's rights within family contexts. The 2009 Magna Carta of Women similarly serves to emphasize women's rights, and this emphasis does not directly imply that earlier laws were incomplete or inadequate, but rather indicates that previous measures aimed at balancing women's rights were insufficient in orientation.

 

(2) Forms of violence

Republic Act No. 9262 summarizes forms of violence as physical harm, sexual violence, psychological harm or emotional suffering, and economic abuse, four categories, granting women and their children who suffer violence the right to file for protection orders, and specifying the duties and penalties for officials and interveners who fail in their responsibilities. Republic Act No. 9262 further elaborates on specific situations under each of these four forms of violence. Notably, marital rape and related forms of sexual violence are explicitly defined; sexual harassment, forcing women to watch pornographic films, and compelling a wife to stay in the same room with the abuser are all included within the scope of sexual violence; stalking and psychological harm caused by sexual harassment are classified as psychological violence; prohibiting someone from pursuing a legal profession, depriving them of access to marital property, or controlling their personal assets are defined as forms of economic abuse. The inclusion of these specific examples in the law reflects not only consideration of women's possible situations within marriage, but also lawmakers' attempt to encompass all possible forms of violence and fully protect women's rights.

 

Image source: rawpixel


(3) Recognition of domestic violence

In addition to the previously mentioned conditions for recognizing domestic violence—namely, the existence of a relationship and the occurrence of abuse—Republic Act No. 9262 emphasizes that criminal acts are not limited to those that have already caused harm (result crimes), but also include acts that threaten or intend to cause harm (conduct crimes). When defining VAW, the law states that it includes acts that "cause or are likely to cause... harm"; when detailing physical violence, it lists acts that cause, threaten to cause, or attempt to cause physical injury to a woman as violent crimes; regarding economic abuse, the law also includes "depriving or threatening to deprive a woman of rightful economic support" within the scope of violent crimes, though sentencing varies depending on specific circumstances. As an additional protection for women and children, the law also stipulates that intoxication or influence of drugs cannot serve as a defense under Republic Act No. 9262; women diagnosed with Battered Woman Syndrome are not held civilly or criminally liable and will not lose custody of their children as a result. Of course, specific cases of domestic violence recognition should also be further studied in light of current judicial precedents.

 

(4) Other related legal definitions

1. Republic Act No. 8353 (1997): Marital rape

As a landmark law for women's rights, Republic Act No. 8353 expanded the definition of rape to include it as a public crime. Known as the "1997 Anti-Rape Law," it classifies rape as a serious offense punishable by life imprisonment or death, and it also penalizes marital rape. However, a limitation of this law is that if the victim and offender are in a valid marriage and the victim subsequently expresses forgiveness, the punishment for the offender may be reduced.


2. Republic Act No. 9710 (The Magna Carta of Women): Local supplementation and revision of CEDAW

Republic Act No. 9710 serves as a local revision of the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW), aiming to protect various rights of women. Its content includes principles of women's human rights, definitions of women's rights, obligations related to women's human rights, women affected by disasters, crises, and other emergency situations, mechanisms for protecting and promoting women's rights, and implementing rules and regulations related to the Magna Carta. This law emphasizes the national stance of the Philippines and plays a significant role in women's empowerment and advancing gender equality.


2

 Government and society (community)

 Introduction to domestic violence response mechanisms 


· Inter-Agency Council on Violence Against Women and Their Children (IACVAWC)

The Inter-Agency Council on Violence Against Women and Their Children (IACVAWC) is the primary coordinating and monitoring body for initiatives addressing violence against women in the Philippines. Established under Republic Act No. 9262, its main purpose is to ensure the effective implementation of relevant laws. The Council's specific functions include: advancing the implementation of Republic Act No. 9262, building the capacity of law enforcement participants, developing comprehensive programs for VAWC victims and survivors, establishing linkages with other relevant stakeholders, monitoring the enforcement of the law, and researching comprehensive approaches to eliminate VAWC.


In its "Strategic Plan 2017–2022," the Council identified strategic goals across three key results areas: first, primary prevention, aiming to systematically address the social and cultural norms that perpetuate violence against women; second, building response systems to improve the effectiveness and efficiency of services provided by IACVAWC member agencies; third, focusing on horizontal structures and related actions to continuously enhance IACVAWC's performance as the primary coordinating and monitoring body for violence against women initiatives.

 

· Women and Children Protection Desks and Women and Children Protection Desks (WCPDs): Police system

1. Organizational structure

In 1993, the Women and Children Protection Desks (WCPDs) established the first women's desk as a subunit of the Directorate for Police Community Relations (DPCR). Since 2015, after reorganization and strengthening of functions, WCPDs have become a specialized unit within the police system dedicated to handling cases of violence against women and children and combating human trafficking. Headquartered in Quezon City, it has three regional offices: Luzon, Visayas, and Mindanao.


WCPDs organizational structure


According to legal provisions, the Philippine National Police system is required to establish Women and Children Protection Desks in police stations as specialized windows for handling crimes against women and children. The law also mandates the prioritized recruitment of female police officers for these desks and requires gender sensitivity training for officers, with all desks organized and supervised by the WCPDs. Reports indicate that nearly all police stations in the Philippines have now established Women and Children Protection Desks.


2. Functions and procedures

The specific functions of the WCPDs include investigating perpetrators of violence against women and children and human trafficking, taking necessary actions including arrests to ensure prompt prosecution; handling cases of battered and abused women and children; conducting rescue operations for victims of violence against women and children and victims of human trafficking; collecting information on related crimes, processing and analyzing the data to formulate corresponding policies and strategies; and coordinating with relevant agencies to meet the social, legal, rehabilitation, and health service needs of rescued victims.


At each police station's Women and Children Protection Desk, the supervising officer interviews victims reporting domestic violence and assists them in subsequent legal procedures, such as accompanying them to see a forensic doctor and preparing affidavits.

 

· Women and Children Protection Units (WCPUs): Collaboration between the government health sector and other organizations

In addition to initial coordination and cooperation with the WCPDs under the police system, under Section 40 of Republic Act No. 9262, partnerships have been established between the Department of Health, the University of the Philippines Manila, the Child Protection Network Foundation, some local government units, and other institutions to set up Women and Children Protection Units (WCPUs) in hospitals under the Department of Health and in hospitals supported by local government units.


The primary function of WCPUs is to provide medical assistance to victims and survivors of violence against women and children. Their services specifically include: medical examination and treatment; laboratory tests; issuance of medical certificates; forensic interviews; crisis counseling; psychological therapy when necessary; provision of food, transportation, and medicine; home visits; surgery when needed; other medical services; and court testimony. In addition to these services, WCPUs also refer victims to organizations that provide counseling, legal services, and temporary shelter.


Facilities of the Women and Children Protection Units are open 24 hours a day, and consent from the victim or a legally authorized guardian not involved in the case must be obtained before any service is provided. According to 2016 data, 94 Women and Children Protection Units have been established across the Philippines, providing services to approximately 8,000 cases in the past year.

 

· Barangay Violence Against Women Desks: Local Government Frontline Response

1. Overview and Functions

Republic Act No. 9710 mandates that each barangay establish a Violence Against Women (VAW) Desk to provide frontline assistance to women victims of physical, psychological, sexual, economic, and other forms of abuse.


The Barangay VAW Desk has the following functions:

(1) Respond to gender-based violence cases reported to the barangay.

(2) Record the number of gender-based violence incidents handled by the barangay and submit a quarterly report on all violence cases to the government's Department of Social Welfare and Development (DSWD).

(3) Maintain confidentiality and secure storage of records on violence against women cases, ensuring that only authorized personnel can access them.

(4) Assist victims of violence against women in obtaining a Barangay Protection Order (BPO) and accessing necessary services.

(5) Develop a barangay response plan for gender-based violence, including support services, capacity building, and referral systems to address gender-based violence.

(6) Coordinate and refer cases to government agencies, non-governmental organizations, and other service providers when necessary.

(7) Address other forms of abuse against women, particularly elderly citizens, women with disabilities, and other marginalized groups.

(8) Conduct community awareness campaigns on eliminating violence against women.

The desk operates from 7:00 a.m. to 7:00 p.m., during which the responsible officer can be reached at any time via mobile or landline phone.


2. Service Procedure

(1) The desk officer confirms the complainant's status, ensures she is comfortable and in a safe and private area, and provides water and other necessary supplies.

(2) Gather basic information to assess the current and potential risks the complainant faces due to the violence.

(3) In emergencies, the desk immediately provides needed services: if further medical care is required, the officer will transport the complainant to the nearest clinic or hospital; if further legal assistance or protection services are needed, especially regarding her personal safety, the officer will refer her to the appropriate office or service agency best able to protect her; afterward, the officer will complete the information form and the referral form for the complainant.

(4) Once the complainant's condition is stable, the officer will conduct an interview.

(5) In accordance with Republic Act No. 9262, the officer will inform the complainant of her rights, available remedies, and relevant legal procedures. The officer will also explain the Barangay Protection Order (BPO), how to obtain it, and its effects on her and her children. If the complainant decides to apply for a protection order, the desk will assist in filing the application.


If a third party, not the direct victim of violence against women, reports the case to the desk, the Barangay VAW Desk will still investigate and accept the case.


· Petition for Protection Order: Personal Protection

To prevent further violence against women or their children and to provide necessary relief, relevant authorities may issue protection orders for victims of domestic violence. There are three main types of protection orders: First, the "Barangay Protection Order" (BPO), issued by barangay officials, which, under Sections 5a and 5b of Republic Act No. 9262, commands the respondent to cease acts of violence against family members, especially women and their children. It is valid for 15 days. Second, the "Temporary Protection Order" (TPO), issued by the court upon application and unilateral determination of necessity, valid for 30 days. This order may also be issued during hearings, either on the court's own motion or upon motion by a party. Third, the "Permanent Protection Order" (PPO), issued by the court after a hearing.

 

Once issued, a protection order has the following effects:

(1) Prohibits the respondent from threatening to commit or personally or through others committing acts of violence against the victim.

(2) Prohibits the respondent from directly or indirectly harassing, calling, contacting, or otherwise communicating with the victim in any form.

(3) Removes the respondent from the victim's residence, regardless of ownership, allowing the respondent to temporarily leave for the victim's protection, or permanently leave without infringing on property rights. If the respondent must retrieve personal belongings from the residence, the court shall direct a law enforcement officer to accompany the respondent to the residence, assist in packing personal items, and escort the respondent away.

(4) Requires the respondent to stay a specified distance away from the victim and any designated family members, as determined by the court.

(5) Requires the respondent to stay away from the residence, school, workplace, or any other specific place frequently visited by the victim and any designated family members.

(6) Ensures the victim's lawful possession and use of a vehicle and other essential personal property, regardless of ownership, and directs appropriate law enforcement officers to accompany the victim to the shared residence to ensure safe access to and use of the vehicle and other essential personal items.

(7) Orders temporary or permanent custody of children to be granted to the victim, considering the best interests of the child. Victims suffering from battered woman syndrome shall not be disqualified from custody of their children. Under no circumstances shall custody of minor children be granted to the abuser.

(8) Directs the respondent to provide support to the woman and her children. The court shall order a proportionate amount of the respondent's income to be regularly withheld by his employer and automatically remitted to the victim. Failure to withhold, remit, or delay in remitting payments to the victim without just cause shall render the respondent or his employer liable for indirect contempt of court.

(9) Prohibits the respondent from carrying or possessing any firearm or deadly weapon, and orders the surrender of such weapons to the court for appropriate disposition, including license revocation and disqualification from applying for any license to carry or possess firearms. If the respondent is a law enforcement officer, the court shall order the surrender of the firearm and direct the relevant agency to conduct an investigation and take appropriate action.

(10) Directs the Department of Social Welfare and Development (DSWD) or any appropriate agency to prepare an intervention program for the victim, providing advocacy, temporary shelter, crisis intervention, treatment, counseling, education, training, and other social services the victim may need.

(11) Requires the respondent to undergo professional counseling from an agency or individual with expertise and experience in emotional regulation, alcohol and drug abuse management, and other forms of intervention to stop violence. The intervention program for the offender must be approved by the court. The agency or individual must provide the court with regular reports on the progress and outcomes of the counseling, and the court may order the respondent to pay the costs.

(12) Awards the victim actual damages caused by the violence, including but not limited to property damage, medical expenses, childcare costs, and loss of income, as well as compensatory, moral, and exemplary damages.

 

Protection Order Application Process:

Generally, the Women and Children Protection Desk, Women and Children Protection Unit, or Barangay VAW Desk that receives the victim's complaint will assist the domestic violence victim in applying for a protection order.


Any of the following parties may file an application for a protection order:

(1) The domestic violence victim

(2) The victim's parents or legal guardian

(3) A relative, descendant, or collateral relative within the fourth civil degree of consanguinity

(4) An officer, social worker, or local government unit social worker from the Department of Social Welfare and Development

(5) A police officer, preferably one designated for women and children's affairs

(6) The barangay captain or a barangay council member

(7) The victim's lawyer, counselor, therapist, or healthcare provider

(8) At least two responsible citizens from the city or municipality where violence against women and their children occurred, who have personal knowledge of the offense.


After the victim reports the incident, the Barangay VAW Desk officer files the application for a Barangay Protection Order (BPO). Applications for a Temporary Protection Order (TPO) or Permanent Protection Order (PPO) may be filed with the Regional Trial Court, Metropolitan Trial Court, Municipal Trial Court, or Municipal Circuit Trial Court that has territorial jurisdiction over the applicant's residence. If there is a Family Court in the applicant's place of residence, the application shall be filed with that court.

The application form includes the following information:

(1) Names and addresses of the applicant and the respondent

(2) Description of the relationship between the applicant and the respondent

(3) Statement regarding the circumstances of the abuse

(4) Description of the assistance requested by the applicant

(5) Request for and reasons supporting the appointment of a lawyer

(6) Request for exemption from payment of filing fees prior to the hearing

(7) Certification that no other pending application for a protection order is filed in another court

· Special Task Force for the Protection of Women and Children: Investigation and Prosecution

To handle crimes defined under Republic Act No. 9262, the Department of Justice (DOJ) established the Special Task Force for the Protection of Women and Children, composed of national prosecutors responsible for the preliminary investigation and prosecution of crimes against women and children. Cases of domestic violence are adjudicated by Family Courts.


Women's Temporary Shelters

The Department of Social Welfare and Development has established 45 temporary shelters for victims of violence against women and children, including:

1. Shelters: Providing direct intervention for female victims of gender-based violence or women vulnerable to abuse and exploitation, offering protective services they need as well as other services that promote healing and recovery. According to a UNICEF survey, victims of violence are usually brought to shelters by local government units, towns, police, or other civil society organizations; very few arrive on their own. The average length of stay is 6–12 months.

2. Girls' Home: Offering protection, care, treatment, and rehabilitation services for girls under 18 who have suffered abuse and exploitation.

3. Maria Clara Pavilion (also known as the National Training School for Girls): Providing care and rehabilitation services for girls under 18 who have been abused and exploited.

4. Children's Reception and Learning Centers: Providing psychosocial services for children aged 0 to 6, aiming to support children's survival, development, and protection.


Paid leave for victims of domestic violence

Under Republic Act No. 9262, female employees who are victims of violence against women are entitled to a maximum of ten fully paid days of leave. If necessary, this leave should be extended in accordance with protection orders issued by municipalities or courts. This leave benefit should include days needed by the employee to address medical and legal matters.


The only requirement to apply for this leave is for the victim employee to present to her employer a certification from the Punong Barangay (village chief), barangay kagawad (village councilor), prosecutor, or court clerk (as applicable), confirming that legal proceedings related to the case are ongoing.

3

 Non-governmental assistance organizations and civil society aid 


In safeguarding women's rights and addressing violence against women, in addition to government agencies, civil society organizations in the Philippines also play a role. Many temporary shelters for victims of violence against women are provided by non-governmental organizations and religious groups. Legal NGOs, civil legal groups, and some private firms offer legal aid to victims. Some private clinics also collaborate with government agencies to provide medical services. Moreover, specialized NGOs provide livelihood development training and employment assistance to victims. Below are some of the main civil society organizations in the Philippines addressing violence against women:

 

· GABRIELA: National women's alliance organization

The General Assembly Binding Women for Reforms, Integrity, Equality, Leadership, and Action, abbreviated as GABRIELA, is a leftist organization in the Philippines that brings together groups advocating for issues concerning women and children. It consists of over 200 organizations and institutions focused on the welfare of Filipino women. GABRIELA was founded in April 1984, when 10,000 women gathered to protest against the Marcos government. It was during this significant historical moment that members decided to name the organization "GABRIELA" in honor of Gabriela Silang, an 18th-century woman who fought alongside the Katipuneros against the Spanish.


GABRIELA focuses on addressing issues affecting women, such as domestic violence, sexual abuse, gender inequality, sex trafficking, and rape culture, as well as broader national issues including poverty, militarism, health, and globalization.

 

· SAVE Our Women

Stop Abuse and Violence against Women (SAVE Our Women) was established in 2007 in Baguio City. As a local non-governmental organization, it provides psychosocial support and referral services to female survivors of domestic violence and intimate partner violence, as well as their children. The organization also aims to raise awareness about the current situation and prevalence of domestic and intimate partner violence by disseminating information on actual cases, statistics, and legal procedures in the Philippines for protecting women, prosecuting perpetrators, and preventing further abuse. SAVE Our Women primarily serves Baguio City and its surrounding areas, collaborating with local agencies and volunteers, including lawyers, guidance counselors, and other service providers, to promote and implement its mission.

 

· Bathaluman Crisis Center Foundation

The Bathaluman Crisis Center Foundation was established in 1991. This non-profit organization aims to assist survivors of violence against women. The foundation provides a support system for survivors, including a service and referral center to help them heal from physical trauma.

 

· Women's Crisis Center

The Women's Crisis Center (WCC) was established in 1989. This institution provides a supportive and affirming environment for women who have experienced violence, enabling them to interact with other survivors through group counseling, educational programs, recreational activities, and skills training.

 

· Bantay Bata

Bantay Bata was established in 1997. This welfare organization and foundation primarily focuses on harm and exploitation affecting children. The organization handles cases related to domestic violence and child abuse, such as providing online counseling for victims of child abuse and family violence, as well as support for children with serious or rare diseases. Bantay Bata's emergency hotline is 1-6-3. Additionally, Bantay Bata leads psychosocial activities to help victimized children cope with their difficult pasts and experiences.

 Case study and insights on assistance for women suffering domestic violence 

 — The case of Lumen 


This case analysis was published in January 2009. The subject, "Lumen" (a pseudonym), is a 36-year-old mother living in Banawa, Cebu City. After she and her children suffered abuse from her husband, she sought help from the Women and Children-Friendly Center at Vicente Sotto Memorial Medical Center.


1. Family analysis

(1) The victim plays a subordinate role in the family, has low self-esteem, and obeys her partner

"When we first got married, I always stayed at home cooking for him because I only finished third grade, and I didn't know if there was any work I could do. Our life was peaceful because whenever he got angry at me, I would stay quiet."


Lumen plays the typical role of a "good wife" in her family. From her testimony, it is evident that she lacks self-esteem, always obeys her husband's commands, and remains silent within the household. According to Stuart and Sundeen's theory (1995), in almost all forms of domestic violence, the abuser maintains some form of authority or control over the victim. Lumen assumes a subordinate role rather than a complementary one in her marriage. She had no job at the time, and when she told the interviewer about her low level of education, she cried. In addition, she is an immigrant from Leyte with no close relatives nearby. The lack of economic, educational, and familial support systems are key factors contributing to a wife's sense of inferiority toward her husband (Nguyen, 2004).


(2) The husband holds authority and uses force to maintain his role and family order

"If he's not in a bad mood, our relationship is fine. As long as I follow his rhythm, our relationship is good."


When asked who makes decisions in the family, Lumen immediately and without hesitation answered, "My husband." The family is clearly patriarchal, with the husband dominating in authority and decision-making. Lumen's response reflects that the direction of the family is entirely at the mercy of her husband's decisions and emotions. The WCC information guide states that in family structures, some husbands and fathers use force or the threat of force as a mechanism to establish family roles and maintain discipline.


(3) One-way, unhealthy communication patterns

"(Our communication) depends on his mood. Most of the time he is angry, and we rarely talk about our children's future or other important matters."

 

Lumen describes her communication with her husband in this way, showing that everything in their family communication revolves around her husband's state. The communication between Lumen and her husband is one-way and unhealthy. Shives (1990) points out that poor communication skills and patterns can lead one party to resort to verbal or physical violence.


2. Domestic violence situation

(1) Jealousy and control over partner expressed through verbal and physical abuse

"I had a daytime cleaning job. My boss gave us extra food, so I asked my husband if I could give some to our neighbor. He agreed, but told me not to stay too long because the baby might wake up crying. I went to the neighbor's house with the food, and then he shouted at me for being away too long. In fact, I was only gone about 10 minutes. When I returned, he scolded and hit me. He did this because he was jealous of our neighbor's husband."


Physical abuse against Lumen occurs frequently (pulling her hair, hitting her face, sides, and back, etc.). Her husband justifies this by claiming he is jealous that Lumen has another man, though most of the time he does not name any specific man. His suspicions appear baseless. Sometimes, he asks the older children if any man came to the house while he was away. When his suspicions are not confirmed, he becomes frustrated. According to Stuart and Sundeen (1995), common causes of domestic violence include extreme jealousy and controlling behavior toward a spouse.


(2) Violence used to emphasize the husband's power within the family

Although Lumen was the one who brought food home and contributed to the family income, she still sought her husband's permission. Her husband shouted at her because the child was crying, indicating the phenomenon of "single parenting" in the family, where the husband did not participate in childcare. This unreasonable act of violence reflects the husband's desire to maintain male power both inside and outside the family. Power is a core factor in wife abuse, and although abusers often justify their violent behavior with trivial incidents, the violence is often related to the husband's desire for complete domination over his wife (McCue, 1995; Stuart & Sundeen, 1995).


3. Impact of domestic violence

(1) Repeated fleeing and returning to the abuser

After the first incident of domestic violence, Lumen fled to her hometown in Leyte, but soon her husband found her, begged for her forgiveness, and promised never to harm her again. She believed his promise and returned to Cebu. Later, she said, the domestic violence continued, and whenever it occurred, "I stayed quiet, careful not to further provoke him, still serving him, because afterward he would calm down and the household would become peaceful again."


When domestic violence occurs for the first time, the victim's initial reaction is usually to escape the painful experience. However, due to sociocultural norms that demand tolerance and view endurance as part of marriage, abused wives often return to their families (Nguyen, 2004). As a typical wife raised in Asian culture, Lumen believed her husband had the right to abuse her. Her silence after being abused shows she had long become accustomed to being mistreated. She believed that if she was willing to endure, it would please her husband and eventually stop the abuse in the future (Lamanna & Riedmann, 1999). She usually did not take decisive action to stop the domestic violence because she believed her husband would change. Lumen's behavior reflects helplessness and a sense of powerlessness, and she is unable to realize she might have options other than endurance. She seems unaware of how to escape the situation, and more importantly, she does not believe it is necessary to escape.


Image source: rawpixel


(2) Continuing to live with the abuser

"I believe it's just a cycle—he hits me, then he asks for forgiveness, and we become peaceful again, until the next domestic violence happens."


From a social perspective, women influenced by patriarchal culture may view violence as a normal part of intimate relationships and accept male authority and the right to control the entire family when needed (Riley, 1997). Although Lumen fled from her abusive husband, she later returned because he promised not to harm her again. Johnson's (1990) research shows that before permanently leaving an abusive environment, victims typically go through multiple cycles of leaving and returning, sometimes as many as 20 times. According to Stuart & Sundeen (1995), this is not a sign of weakness but a normal and common pattern, primarily influenced by the level of social support and assistance available to the abused woman and the abuser's behavior, rather than by the woman's own psychological factors.


(3) Failure to recognize the negative impact of domestic violence and difficulty in taking proactive action to solve the problem

"At first, I hated him, but later I learned to forgive his actions. I stayed silent to prevent his anger from escalating and being directed at me. The abuse is temporary, and I can heal the wounds it causes. I didn't see any harmful effects of this abuse on me until one thing happened."


Lumen was unable to recognize the impact of the abuse on herself. She seemed to have accepted all the abuse inflicted upon her as a normal part of life. This condition is described as battered woman syndrome, in which individuals are unable to perceive abnormal and distressing feelings in their real situation (McCue, 1995). Her behavior can also be attributed to common characteristics of abused wives—victims may develop emotional dependence on the abuser, strictly adhere to patriarchal gender roles, and suffer from low self-esteem and insecurity (Johnson, 1990).


When Lumen was asked about her current plans, she hesitated and said, "I might go back to Leyte, we won't go home." Later that day, she requested the center's permission to leave, and when asked where she was going, she replied, "We will return to our home in Banawa."


There was a contradiction between what Lumen verbally expressed during the interview and her later actions. This behavior shows Lumen's strong hesitation when making decisions. According to Stuart & Sundeen (1995), abused women face difficulties in taking action to address domestic violence. Due to prolonged severe beatings and control, their initiative and ability to solve problems are seriously impaired.


4. Awakening factors

(1) Considering the impact on children

"It didn't matter if I got hurt, but when he started using violence against my children—when he grabbed my child by the neck and lifted her up as if hanging her—that was when I decided to come here. This can't go on. His violence is now targeting my children." Even before one of her children suffered physical abuse, they had already endured verbal abuse: "He shouted at my children and said he would kill them, while showing them a knife."


Only after her children were abused did she realize her husband's behavior was completely inappropriate, but her statements suggest she did not believe, or was uncertain, that his violence against her was also wrong. According to Stuart and Sundeen (1995), women often make moral judgments by weighing consequences for others rather than for themselves. Therefore, concern for children is a major factor in abused women's decision-making process. Lumen found her strength in her children because she could no longer bear to see them harmed.


(2) Peer and external support

In addition, support from peers was also a major factor in Lumen's awakening. She recalled that her close friend advised her to report the incident to the police, after which she was referred to VSMMC-WCFC and spent the night there with her children. During the interview, she emphasized her husband's abuse of her daughter and did not mention the abuse she herself suffered until further questioning. This indicates that when she sought counseling and help, she did not consider her own domestic violence situation as something that required consultation.

III.

Identifying domestic violence and self-rescue


1

 Self-assessment methods for identifying domestic violence 


Understanding the definition and manifestations of domestic violence can help victims take more effective action against various forms of abuse. In some cases, abusers may not realize they are committing domestic violence against others; on the other hand, if victims do not recognize that what they are experiencing is indeed domestic violence, they may not take action against the abuser. The previous section has already detailed the definition of domestic violence under Philippine local law (Republic Act No. 9262), including the targets of domestic violence, its forms, and legal standards for identifying such acts, so it will not be repeated here. Here, we will focus on several types of domestic violence and how women can determine whether they are experiencing domestic violence.


Potential behaviors indicating domestic violence

For most people, identifying domestic violence may not be easy at first. Although some relationships clearly involve abuse from the beginning, the ways and degrees of such behaviors are often not obvious at first but intensify over time and develop into severe domestic violence. Potential behaviors of domestic violence often do not manifest physically; instead, abusers more commonly exert psychological and economic control over victims. If victims show obedience, forgiveness, or even indifference at this stage, the abuser is likely to escalate the level of violence. Therefore, effectively identifying potential behaviors of domestic violence is crucial for preventing severe abuse. If you experience any of the following events in a relationship, you may be experiencing potential domestic violence:


  • privately or publicly insulting or belittling you;

  • blaming you for negative outcomes;

  • frequently accusing you of being unfaithful in the relationship;

  • attempting to forcefully interfere in your daily life (e.g., what clothes to wear or where to go);

  • attempting to control whether you receive medical care;

  • attempting to abuse your pets;

  • attempting to control how you spend money;

  • drinking alcohol or using drugs and then verbally or physically abusing you;

 

physical, psychological, sexual violence, and economic abuse

According to the classification of violence against women and their children under the Philippines' Republic Act No. 9262, domestic violence includes four main types: physical violence, psychological violence, sexual violence, and economic control. If you believe that behaviors matching the following descriptions have occurred or are occurring in a relationship, you may be experiencing domestic violence.


"Physical violence" refers to acts that cause bodily harm to the victim.


"Psychological violence" refers to acts or omissions that cause or are likely to cause mental or emotional suffering to the victim, such as but not limited to intimidation, harassment, stalking, damaging property, publicly mocking or humiliating, repeated verbal abuse, and marital infidelity. It includes causing or allowing the victim to witness physical, sexual, or psychological abuse of a family member of the victim, to witness any form of pornography, to witness abuse of pets, or the illegal or involuntary deprivation of custody and/or visitation rights to ordinary children.


"Sexual violence" refers to sexual acts directed against women or their children. It includes, but is not limited to:


  • rape, sexual harassment, lewd acts, treating women or their children as sexual objects, making derogatory and sexually suggestive remarks, physically assaulting sexual parts of the victim's body, forcing her to watch obscene publications or indecent programs, or forcing women or their children to perform indecent acts and/or film indecent videos; forcing a wife and mistress/lover to live in the marital home, or to sleep in the same room with the abuser;

  • using force, threats of force, physical or other harm, or threats of physical or other harm to compel or attempt to compel the victim to participate in any sexual act;

  • causing women or children to engage in prostitution.

 

"Economic abuse" refers to acts that cause or attempt to cause a woman to become economically dependent, including, but not limited to, the following acts:


  • withdrawing financial support or preventing the victim from engaging in any lawful occupation, business, or activity, unless the spouse/partner raises a valid, serious, and ethical objection under Section 73 of the Family Law;

    *Section 73 of the Family Law: Either spouse may engage in any lawful occupation, business, or activity without the other's consent. The other party may only object on valid, serious, and ethical grounds.

  • depriving or threatening to deprive the victim of financial resources, and of the right to use and enjoy marital, community, or jointly owned property;

  • destroying household property;

  • controlling the victim's own money or property, or solely controlling the couple's joint money or property.


2

 Identifying potential domestic abusers 


Anyone can become an abuser. They come from all groups, all cultures, all religions, all economic levels, and all backgrounds, and they may even be highly attractive (Taylor, 2013). While abusers do not have a typical, detectable personality profile, they often exhibit common personality or behavioral traits. Identifying potential abusers and promptly ending unhealthy relationships is the most effective way to prevent domestic violence.


Personality traits of potential abusers:

  • low self-esteem;

  • poor emotional regulation;

  • extreme controlling and possessive tendencies;

  • extreme suspicion and jealousy;

  • outdated and rigid views about gender and relationships.

  • Behavioral traits of potential abusers:

  • attempting to isolate the victim;

  • disrespecting personal privacy;

  • constantly pressuring the relationship to progress quickly;

  • frequently exhibiting behaviors that indicate potential domestic violence.


Other traits of potential abusers:

  • history of abuse in the family or in past relationships;

  • experiences of abuse during childhood;

  • untreated mental illness;

  • alcohol and/or drug abuse.

3

 Recommended methods for collecting evidence of domestic violence 


Victims and survivors of domestic violence often have to become their own investigators, gathering as much evidence as possible to prove that abuse has occurred. Lack of evidence can be a major challenge in prosecuting domestic violence cases. However, during the evidence collection process, one must pay attention to personal safety and the validity of the evidence. This article lists some recommended, locally legal methods for collecting evidence of domestic violence and important considerations, for reference by survivors.


Collecting physical evidence

Survivors of domestic violence should collect any evidence that can prove abuse has occurred, including but not limited to:


  • audio recordings, videos, or photos (with dates labeled) taken during the abuse

  • messages, emails, or phone recordings from the abuser that show violent tendencies or threats

  • photos (with dates labeled) of injuries caused by the abuser's abusive behavior

  • medical documentation of injuries caused by the abuser's abuse (such as medical reports, treatment records, and counseling records)

  • police reports filed by the victim or witnesses reporting the abuse

  • household items destroyed by the abuser or photos of such items

  • photos of the scene after the abuse occurred

  • photos of weapons or objects used by the abuser to harm the victim

  • personal records detailing when, where, and how the abusive acts occurred (such as diaries, calendars, etc.)


Finding witnesses

Witnesses are a crucial part of the evidence chain. A bystander witness refers to someone the victim identifies who can confirm her account of abuse or who has personally witnessed or heard the domestic violence. An expert witness refers to a qualified mental health counselor trained in trauma from domestic violence who can testify on behalf of the victim. Cases supported by testimony from both types of witnesses are more persuasive.


The victim's own testimony alone is not enough

Although this sounds deeply unfair, it is a harsh reality for survivors of domestic violence. A victim's own testimony about the abuse is often not sufficient as standalone evidence, because the credibility of their testimony is frequently challenged. Giugi Carmunati, JD, a lawyer, activist, and author of the blog Argue Like a Girl, says: "Victims are hard to believe, especially when their accounts of abuse are attacked. They are often accused of suffering from post-abuse symptoms—such as cognitive impairments, memory problems, and delusions—so their testimony may be questioned." To avoid having their credibility challenged, domestic violence survivors should remain honest and rational in court. Emotions cannot prove facts in court, but facts can.


Choosing safe methods for recording evidence

When victims believe that recording abuse incidents alone may put them at greater risk, Narrah Patton, a domestic violence advocate at Safe Austin, suggests that victims contact trusted family members or friends to help store evidence and related information. However, this approach also carries certain risks, such as the abuser having already successfully isolated the victim from her family and friends, or the victim having difficulty finding a safe moment to make calls or contact the outside world. In such cases, victims may choose to use technology to securely store information. For example, an app called ICE BlackBox allows victims to record videos, which are automatically stored in the cloud and sent to the victim's "emergency contacts" to prevent destruction. Alternatively, if the victim already has a lawyer, she may choose to email the evidence to the lawyer after collecting it.


Do not leave evidence where it can be found

Survivors of domestic violence sometimes fall into the trap of keeping evidence in places accessible to the abuser, such as on an unencrypted phone or hidden in a secret corner at home. This not only exposes survivors to additional risks, but also means the abuser may discover and permanently destroy the evidence. Therefore, survivors should adopt the evidence recording methods recommended above and, while ensuring their own safety, entrust evidence to trusted individuals for safekeeping.


4

 Self-help measures 


1. Contact trusted family and friends

This action not only provides material and emotional support for victims attempting self-rescue, but also offers an opportunity for victims to break free from isolation and rebuild healthy interpersonal relationships. Entrusting collected evidence to trusted individuals, discussing escape plans together, and receiving financial and emotional support from them are all factors that help victims escape domestic violence and unhealthy intimate relationships. Victims can take this as the first step in an escape plan, having trusted individuals contact the outside world and professional aid organizations to reduce the abuser's suspicion and increase the likelihood of a successful escape.


2. Prepare an emergency kit

The emergency kit should include items needed for leaving, such as but not limited to: spare clothing, enough cash for several days' expenses, and essential contact numbers (carrying a phone may allow the abuser to track the victim). Important personal documents (such as driver's license or passport), money, and medication should be kept within easy reach for quick access. At the same time, the victim should keep a spare set of car keys in a safe and accessible location to prevent the abuser from taking the keys and blocking the victim's escape.


If fleeing with children, the emergency kit should also include formula, diapers, items that make the child feel safe, and the child's important documents.


Image source: pixabay


The emergency kit should be stored in a safe place, such as at a trusted friend's or neighbor's home.


The contents listed here are only recommendations. What to prepare specifically depends on the survivor's own judgment.


3. Develop a safety plan

To escape an abuser, creating a safe departure plan is essential—it is the key to leaving safely. A safety plan includes determining when to leave, where to go, what to take, and how to keep this information confidential from the abuser. Whether discussing with family, friends, or support organizations, as a survivor, these decisions must ultimately be made by you. Only you know best when and where it is safest to leave.


When to leave—It is best to consider leaving when the abuser is not at home. If this is not possible, consider other moments, such as when the abuser is out shopping or walking a pet. Before the actual departure, survivors should practice leaving in these scenarios multiple times.


Where to go—Safe destinations include, but are not limited to, domestic violence support organizations and shelters listed in this article, the residence of a trusted person unknown to the abuser, or another secure location in a different city. If time allows, survivors may consider creating false leads—for example, calling a hotel or school at least six hours' travel distance from the planned safe destination—to mislead the abuser's tracking and keep them as far as possible from the intended route.


Even after safely arriving at the planned location, survivors should not let their guard down. Consider getting a new mobile phone to avoid being tracked or monitored, changing work hours and workplace address, and changing children's schools to prevent possible tracking.


4. Apply for a protection order 

For detailed information on this item, please refer to the previous section.


5. Contact government and non-governmental support organizations, law firms, and others to seek professional help

Victims are best supported by trained domestic violence advocates who can help them create a safety plan tailored to their unique circumstances. Their expertise can assist victims in leaving dangerous situations in the most strategic and intelligent way. Advocates can also help you locate local resources, including shelters, counseling, non-legal advice, and court assistance. Domestic violence victims may contact government and civil society organizations, such as the Women and Children Protection Desk (WCPD) and Women and Children Protection Units (WCPUs) within Philippine police stations, government-partnered temporary women's shelters, and non-governmental organizations such as SAVE Our Women and Bathaluman Crisis Center Foundation. Detailed information and contact details for these organizations are provided earlier in this article. Additionally, victims may contact lawyers to obtain targeted legal services based on their specific situation.

Advancing Women's Rights: The Philippines' International Engagement


Addressing violence against women requires not only updated social attitudes and legal safeguards but also an overall improvement in women's social status and protection of their rights. These are not only challenges faced by the Philippines but also issues of global concern. Since the 20th century, the Philippines has participated in numerous international initiatives and actions aimed at advancing women's status and, with support from international organizations, has strengthened the protection of women's rights.


1

 The Philippines' International Engagement in Women's Rights Protection 


Goal Setting and International Commitments

The Philippines is one of 189 countries that ratified the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW), adopted in 1979, which is described as a landmark international bill of women's rights. The Philippines is also among the United Nations member states that adopted the Beijing Platform for Action (BPfA) by consensus. The BPfA is the outcome document of the Fourth World Conference on Women, held in Beijing, China, in 1995.


The Philippines played a crucial role in shaping the Beijing Platform for Action, as the chair of the main committee negotiating the declaration was a Filipino. The Platform aims to empower all women and calls on governments and the international community to take action in 12 critical areas to advance women's status and achieve gender equality. The Philippines has also participated in the subsequent meetings held every five years to assess the implementation of the Beijing Platform for Action.


In September 2015, the Philippines joined other UN member states in adopting the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, which calls on countries to take action over the next 15 years to achieve 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Goal 5 calls for "achieving gender equality and empowering all women and girls." The Philippines has incorporated gender equality into its national framework, "The Sustainable Development Goals in the Philippines."


Additionally, the Philippines is one of the founding members of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC). Empowering women economically and further integrating women into the regional economy are priority issues on APEC's agenda, primarily advanced through the Policy Partnership on Women and the Economy (PPWE). Since 2013, the Department of Trade and Industry (DTI) of the Philippines has closely collaborated with the Philippine Commission on Women (PCW), the Philippine Women's Business Council (WomenBiz.ph), and the Department of Foreign Affairs (DFA) to ensure that the Philippines' participation in APEC activities promotes gender equality and women's economic empowerment across all relevant sectors and issues. At the 2014 APEC Women and the Economy Forum, DTI Undersecretary Nora K. Terrado emphasized the significant role of Asia-Pacific women in business, highlighted the benefits of women's participation in the regional economy for society as a whole, and joined other participants in calling on APEC economies to adopt more concrete and effective measures to mainstream gender perspectives, eliminate barriers hindering women's full development, and create a more supportive environment for women's advancement.


Actions Taken

To achieve the gender equality targets of the 2030 Sustainable Development Goals, the Philippines has implemented numerous government programs, campaigns, and projects, such as the "18-Day Campaign to End Violence Against Women," organized by the Philippine Commission on Women (PCW). Held annually from November 25 to December 12 since 2016, the campaign aims to raise awareness among all stakeholders that violence against women is a public issue of national concern through a series of activities and advocacy efforts. The 2021 campaign focused on increasing public awareness of the newly revised Safe Spaces Act, which defines and establishes penalties for gender-based sexual harassment in public streets and spaces, workplaces, and online platforms.

International Support for Women's Development in the Philippines


The Philippine government has maintained close cooperation with international organizations such as the United Nations. Various UN agencies have launched programs and initiatives to assist the Philippine government, civil society, labor unions, the private sector, and other institutions in removing barriers to gender equality. For example, the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA) collaborates with local Philippine institutions to support the full implementation of the Magna Carta of Women, helping partners transform social norms and eliminate harmful practices against women and girls. 2

 Domestic Violence Assessment Tools 


Below are some of the most commonly used domestic violence assessment tools, enabling victims to understand their situation as truthfully and accurately as possible.


· Conflict Tactics Scale (CTS)

The Conflict Tactics Scale (CTS), developed by Straus in 1979, was the first tool to provide strong quantitative support for research on domestic violence. The original CTS primarily assessed physical violence. In 1996, Straus and colleagues revised and released the CTS2, adding items on sexual coercion and psychological aggression. The full CTS contains 78 items, assessing both the respondent and their partner's experiences of abuse and violence over the past 12 months across five dimensions: negotiation, psychological aggression, physical violence, sexual coercion, and injury. The scale uses an 8-point scoring system ranging from 0 to 7. A positive response to any item indicates the presence of victimization or perpetration of domestic violence. Completing the full CTS assessment takes 10–15 minutes. For specific situations, Straus extracted 20 items from the CTS2 to create a short-form CTS2, which can be completed in under 3 minutes.


The internal consistency reliability coefficient of the CTS ranges from 0.79 to 0.95, with agreement between partner self-reports on victimization and perpetration ranging from 0.62 to 0.90, and specificity of assessment at 92%. The short-form CTS2 shows correlation coefficients of 0.65 to 0.89 with the subscales of the full version, demonstrating high accuracy and the ability to detect most forms of domestic violence.

 

· HITS Scale (Hurt, Insult, Threaten, and Scream, HITS)

The HITS scale was developed by Sherin et al. in 1998, with the acronym derived from the first letters of the words Hurt, Insult, Threaten, and Scream, representing four forms of domestic violence: physical harm, insult, threat, and screaming. Each form is represented by one question, forming a questionnaire consisting of two verbal violence items and two physical violence items. The English version of the HITS scale uses a 5-point scoring system, with a score greater than 10 considered positive, indicating the presence of domestic violence. The HITS scale features concise and easily understandable items, a simple scoring mechanism, and a short completion time, making it highly suitable for self-assessment by victims in busy settings or for emergency screening.


The correlation between the HITS scale and relevant items of the CTS scale ranges from r = 0.75 to 0.86. It can identify 91% of non-abused individuals and 96% of abused individuals, with a clinical specificity of 99% in screening for abusive behaviors, demonstrating a high level of detection accuracy.


· Abuse Assessment Screen (AAS)

The Abuse Assessment Screen (AAS) was developed by McFarlane et al. in 1995 and is currently the only screening tool available for assessing abuse among pregnant women. The AAS consists of eight items evaluating psychological and physical violence experienced by women across three timeframes: lifetime, past 12 months, and during pregnancy. It also includes assessments of sexual coercion and fear of the abuser. All eight items are yes/no questions; any single "yes" response is considered positive, indicating the presence of domestic violence.


The test-retest reliability of the AAS is 0.91. Its sensitivity for detecting mild violence ranges from 32%, while for severe violence it ranges from 61% to 94%, with specificity between 97% and 99%. This indicates that the AAS is less accurate in assessing mild violence but is relatively reliable in detecting severe violence.


· Ongoing Violence Assessment Tool (OVAT)

The Ongoing Violence Assessment Tool (OVAT) was developed in 2003 to assess ongoing domestic violence. The scale contains four yes/no questions: three related to physical violence and one to non-physical violence. Any single "yes" response is considered positive, indicating the individual is currently experiencing domestic violence.


The OVAT has an internal consistency reliability of 0.88, with a sensitivity of 93% and specificity of 86% in screening for domestic violence. Its positive predictive value is 75%, and negative predictive value is 97%, indicating that the OVAT has a high level of identification capability for ongoing domestic violence.


· Woman Abuse Screening Tool (WAST)

The Woman Abuse Screening Tool (WAST) was developed by Brown et al. in 1996 to screen for women's experiences of emotional and/or physical abuse. The scale consists of eight items, and any single positive response identifies the individual as abused. The first two items of the WAST can also form a brief version of the WAST.


The reliability coefficient of the WAST is 0.95, with a clinical specificity of 96% in screening for abuse. Its positive predictive value ranges from 66% to 91%, and negative predictive value from 54% to 100%. The brief version of the WAST correctly identifies 100% of non-abused women and 92% of abused women.
