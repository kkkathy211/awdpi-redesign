In Portugal, many Chinese immigrant families make a living by running shops, "minding the shop" is an almost unavoidable part of their children's daily lives.


Today, we interviewed four Chinese immigrant women who grew up in Portugal:


Xiao Chen, 25, who developed tenosynovitis and shoulder periarthritis from working in the family business, and used her own savings to secure the freedom to take online courses; Xiao Dan, 24, " who has worked for sixteen years without ever receiving a salary, only hoping her parents will retire soon and return her freedom; Jia, 27, who treats the family shop as a "fallback," continuing to help out while pursuing graduate studies and internships, striving not to repeat her parents' life path; Xinyan, 29, who finally enrolled in university after years of hesitation, and has now decided to leave Portugal and restart her life back in China.



Across these four stories, "minding the shop" and "escaping" are constantly intertwined—these women repeatedly struggle between family responsibilities and personal choices, gradually carving out breathing space for themselves in the cracks.


01

CHEN

Xiao Chen, 25:

My birthday gift is "a day off"


I was born in Europe and moved to Portugal with my parents when I was five or six. My parents are from a rural village in China, from an ordinary background. After arriving in Portugal, they opened a restaurant. I have five sisters, and I'm the second oldest.


When I first arrived in Portugal, I didn't speak Portuguese and was resistant to learning it. After starting school, I didn't socialize much with local children—partly because of language barriers, and partly because my parents rarely allowed me to go out, fearing I'd be led astray. At that time, they had just arrived in Portugal, didn't speak the language, and had few acquaintances, so they actually felt very insecure.


So most of my childhood was spent at home. I always consumed Chinese content online, which is why I've remained fluent in Chinese. My favorite social setting was Chinese school, where I could interact more freely with classmates.


My parents have always held a mindset of "being law-abiding and content with the status quo." They avoid trouble, but are also unwilling to change. Even when I was discriminated against and bullied by classmates as a child, my parents would only repeatedly tell me "Don't cause trouble." But I gradually realized that "not causing trouble" wasn't enough. In middle school, I began to resist, no longer staying silent, but directly confronting and arguing back against those who bullied me, and the situation did improve afterward.


When choosing a major in vocational high school, my parents wanted me to study business, believing it would be easier to earn money. But I followed my own interests and chose a program related to media and video editing. They didn't look favorably on this choice, thinking it would lead to poor job prospects. Eventually, after persistent pleading, my parents agreed to let me enroll.


When it came to career choices, my parents believed the best path was either taking over the family business or opening my own shop later. The most common businesses run by Chinese immigrants in Portugal are restaurants, grocery stores, or tobacco shops.


After graduation, I interned at a television station for a while. My parents felt proud of this; they would even tell relatives back in China during phone calls that I had "appeared on TV," even though I was only working behind the scenes. So, if I can achieve something based on my own interests and plans, they will support me. It's just that the only work and income my parents can envision is running their own shop, so they believe this is the best career path.


I started helping in the family restaurant at a very young age. When I was little, I just served dishes and cleared tables, and customers would think, "The kid is cute." But as I grew older, it gradually became a responsibility. After high school, I worked as a freelancer for a while, but then my health declined, so I returned to help in the shop, and continued for five years. During the pandemic, it was especially busy—constant delivery orders and many phone complaints. I handled orders at the front desk, my mom ran back and forth, and my dad managed the kitchen. The entire system was under long-term pressure and overcapacity.


The longer I stayed in the shop, the more suffocated I felt. The most obvious problem was that work and life were completely blended. It seemed convenient on the surface, but any matter related to the shop easily sparked arguments. Often it wasn't about who was right or wrong, but that everyone insisted on their own views and refused to compromise. In fact, our relationship improved when I wasn't in the shop or when my parents returned to China. As soon as we resumed working together, conflicts would reappear. So I often wondered why, even at home, we had to communicate—or even argue—like we were at work.


The work I did in the shop was basically the most miscellaneous and exhausting tasks. During the pandemic, I mainly worked at the front desk; during peak hours, orders piled up, and I felt completely overwhelmed. I also had to deal with various customer issues. For example, the shop had a rule that certain self-serve items couldn't be packed to go, since they were charged per person, but some customers didn't understand and would argue repeatedly, even shouting loudly in the dining area, accusing us of being unreasonable and greedy.


More seriously, I've also experienced sexual harassment. This kind of work has taken a huge toll on both my body and emotions. Over the years, I've accumulated several physical conditions: tenosynovitis, tendonitis, and shoulder periarthritis, and I'm currently undergoing rehabilitation.


There are very few rest periods in the shop. At first, I didn't even have a day off, because in my parents' view, "family members aren't employees," so there was no need to discuss rest days as one would with hired staff. Only after persistent requests did I gradually go from half-days off to nearly a full day off. I even told my mom once, my birthday gift is a day off. Looking back now, it feels a bit absurd, but at the time, rest was something I had to "fight for."


The issue of wages is also very real. My family doesn't completely refuse to give me money, but when I work in the shop, my pay is calculated by hours, and this also serves as my living expense. Precisely because of this, I'm always automatically included in the shift schedule—because if they don't use me, they'd have to hire another employee, not only paying wages but also bearing training costs.


Later, when I started taking online courses, I kept communicating with them, asking not to be included in the regular shift schedule because my coursework was already demanding. At first, they found it hard to accept, but eventually gave in, though it turned into "helping on weekends." So even now, I still go to the shop on Friday and Saturday evenings.


These years of hardship and exhaustion from working in the shop have made me even more determined not to follow the old path of inheriting the restaurant or opening my own shop.


Currently, I've enrolled in an online program to learn 3D modeling. I paid the tuition myself; if I had asked my parents, they definitely wouldn't have agreed. Only after enrolling did I tell them I now had classes and couldn't work at the shop during weekdays. My parents weren't happy about it, but I said, "The money is already paid; if I don't attend, it'll be wasted," so they couldn't really object.


At first, they said they wouldn't give me living expenses since I wasn't working at the shop, but my mom couldn't bear it and still gives me money. Since starting this online course, my life has basically become a routine of studying at home, helping at the restaurant, and working out. Though still busy, at least I'm doing something I enjoy, and my lifestyle is healthier.


02

DAN

Xiao Dan, 24:

My dad says he'll only retire and close the shop after I get married


I was born in Portugal, lived in China with my parents for a while when I was three, then returned to Portugal. I have an older sister, quite a bit older than me, who left home years ago and now works near Lisbon.


I've been helping at home continuously—counting it, I've "worked" for sixteen years. I started helping in the shop daily when I was five or six. Back then, we didn't run the current restaurant but a clothing store. I'd help elderly customers try on shoes and pick clothes. Now we run a restaurant. We don't hire other staff—my parents work in the kitchen, and I manage the front, so it's basically the three of us holding everything together.


During school, I'd come to the shop after class, and later it turned into full-time work. I don't have a fixed salary. My mom has almost never given me money from childhood to now. My dad secretly gives me a little, so I have some cash for daily use at school. Working at home is different from working at someone else's shop. If I'm ten minutes late at home, my mom says I'd have been fired already if I worked elsewhere, but I think if I worked elsewhere, at least I'd be paid before getting fired.


When I was little, the biggest feeling about minding the shop was that I couldn't go out and play. As I grew up, the biggest problem became that I have no time for myself.


My work hours are from noon to 3 p.m., and from 6 p.m. to around 10 p.m. Even during breaks, I have no energy left to do things I enjoy. Even though the restaurant closes at 10 p.m., by the time we finish cleaning and get home, it's nearly midnight. Then, wanting to make up for lost time during the day, I often stay up late on my phone or watching dramas. I wake up very late the next day, but as soon as I get up, I have to go back to the shop again.


What hurts is that my parents don't see me as helping them—they think it's simply my duty. It's as if because they gave birth to me, I naturally belong here. But I don't think so. I feel I'm just spending my time helping them.


My father always says, "All of this will be yours someday," but I've never asked for it. I just want to use my time to do what I truly want to do. Sometimes I wonder, even though our family has made money, how can I ever get back the youth I've lost over these years?


I've thought about leaving before. I once considered going to work somewhere else—I even helped out at a milk tea shop run by someone we knew. Sometimes I'd go in the afternoon, just to stay a while and help out. Time would fly and I'd feel happier there. It's not like that at home, where the atmosphere is always so negative.


But I've never left—not because I really want to stay, but because there's truly no one else at home to help. My parents are both around sixty years old, their Portuguese isn't good, and they've been working nonstop. I know how hard they work, and I feel for them, so that's why I've stayed. Yet when I do stay, my parents and I often argue, and every fight makes me want to leave even more.


We've argued many times about running the shop. I've always hoped they would close it and retire, so I could finally live my own life without any burden.


I asked my father when exactly they would retire and shut down the shop to return to China. At first, he said in two or three years. Two years passed, and then he said they'd leave only after I got married. I was completely baffled. I work every day—when would I even have time to find a partner? I told him I was exhausted and suggested hiring someone to help, but he just replied, "Then go find a partner." I really don't understand—what does one thing have to do with the other?


In high school, I chose the arts track. I wanted to apply to university the first couple of years after graduation, but my parents didn't support it. My grades were average, and since it had already been a while since I graduated, I'd need to attend tutoring classes. I saved up the money for those classes myself.


I'd sneak out before the restaurant opened at 10 a.m. to attend classes, then come home during my afternoon break to study on my own. I studied like this for a year, but it was extremely difficult. In the end, my results weren't good at all. I felt like I was just "faking effort"—appearing hardworking but achieving nothing—so eventually, I just gave up.


I also get anxious easily. Even before exams, I start thinking, "Oh no, the exam will be too hard." Even before getting into university, I worry about all the things I'd have to do and all the people I'd have to interact with. But I also believe that going to university might open up a different path for me.


I remember telling my father I wanted to go to university, and he said fine—he'd leave something for me when he retired, they'd return to China, and I could stay here to study. A few days later, though, he told me to go to university now and have my sister come back to help at the shop instead.


But my sister worked so hard to get out—how could I possibly ask her to come back? My mother holds more traditional views; she says if I go to university at my age, when will I ever get married? She says they won't buy me a house if I don't get married.


Recently, my father is finally returning to China to process his retirement. Although he's handling retirement procedures, our family restaurant in Portugal will continue operating. But during the time he's back in China, I'll finally get a two-week vacation! This will be my first break in nine years of working at the restaurant—I'm definitely going to enjoy it!



03

JIA

Jia, 27:

My colleagues go to the gym after work,

I go to the restaurant to "wait tables"


I'm a third-generation immigrant, born in Portugal. I went to school in China as a child, up to about first grade, then returned to Portugal and grew up in the Lisbon metropolitan area. My family followed the typical immigrant business path—my grandparents came to Portugal very early, starting with street vending, then opening a restaurant, which my father later took over.


I started at around fifteen helping with simple tasks at home, but the workload wasn't heavy and I didn't feel burdened. It was more like casually pitching in. I don't really see myself as one of those typical "trapped-in-the-shop" people, because my family never forced me to stay. They treated the restaurant more like a safety net: If you have another path, go for it; if not, the family shop is still there.


Regarding my studies, they often said "Keep studying if you can; if you can't make it, then work at the shop." My parents didn't push me to study, but they still believed going to university was important. Even if graduation doesn't guarantee a good job, having more knowledge and broader perspectives benefits personal development. My mother, in particular, shared stories from her childhood that made me believe education could lead to a better future.


I'm now pursuing a master's degree and have also secured an internship. I truly feel that going to university has given me more choices, so I don't have to stay tied to the family shop like my parents.


I thought they might oppose my master's due to the high tuition, but they didn't object and even helped pay the fees. I think they saw how strongly I wanted to study, so they chose to support me.


When I chose my major for my bachelor's, I mostly decided on my own, since my parents couldn't offer much guidance. They mainly thought, as long as I liked it and could find a job later, it was fine. They also support me in finding a better, more interesting job for myself.



Even though I'm studying and working now, I still help out at the shop when I have time. I actually started helping at our family restaurant as a child, but at first it wasn't like "working"—more like doing homework at the restaurant while waiting for dinner.


As I grew older, around fifteen or sixteen, I began taking on real responsibilities—mainly watching the cash register, cleaning tables, things like that. At the time, we still had workers, so there wasn't much for me to do. My parents didn't pay me then; I was just helping out.


I still help at the shop now, but in a different way. I work Monday through Friday, and if the shop is open, I go help after work. Sometimes I joke that my colleagues go to the gym after work, while I go to the restaurant to "wait tables"—it's like doing cardio.


I still help now mostly because my parents haven't retired, and it's been hard to find suitable workers since the pandemic. Recently, my parents bought a house and have become more focused on the restaurant's profits, so they're even busier. I try my best to take on some of the load.


Helping out does take up my personal time and affects how I plan my life, but it's not a major issue for me right now. At least for now, I can keep moving forward while still supporting my family a little. Still, living the same way my parents do—running a shop nonstop—is exhausting. That's exactly why I've insisted on continuing my education—to avoid repeating their life, to break the "class reproduction" of inheriting the restaurant and following the same path.


Where running the shop affects me most is probably socially. Because I need to help at the shop, I can't go out whenever I want. If I do go out, I usually have to check with my family first. If my younger sister also wants to go out the same day, I can't go—someone has to stay home to help.


Even now, when friends invite me out on weekends, my first instinct is to check whether the shop will be busy that day. If it's a weekend or the beginning of the month, I'll first ask my family if I can go out for half a day. Even if I do end up going, I still worry—wondering if my parents can manage on their own.


One of my current goals is to achieve a better balance between work and personal life. I've always believed I shouldn't let my environment become an excuse for not moving forward, even if life is tough right now. I try not to blame my surroundings, but instead look for ways to make small changes.



04

XINYAN

Xinyan, 29:

I'm going back to China,

I want to fight for a different path for myself


I came to Portugal with my parents at age twelve, starting middle school in Coimbra. Coming to Portugal was kind of a "trick" by my parents—they told me studies here were easy. When I arrived, I found the workload was indeed lighter, but I didn't understand Portuguese at all, so I couldn't follow classes. It wasn't really better. Only by high school did my Portuguese improve somewhat.


My family didn't value education and never expected me to go to university. Their plan for me was to work after finishing high school, and I used to think the same way. My parents have always run a shop, and many of my peers in Portugal dropped out to work. I thought, since we'd all end up working anyway, what was the point of studying for a few extra years?


I helped watch the shop during middle and high school, but not every day after class. Our shop is small and doesn't need many people, so mostly my parents handled procurement or I'd just cover for them when they had to run errands. For me, it was more like helping out when the family needed it, rather than being constantly stuck working at the shop.


After university, I went even less often—basically only accompanied them on errands, rarely going to the shop specifically. I didn't have a strong aversion to "minding the store," partly because there wasn't much else to do back then, and partly because I knew my parents were tired, so helping out occasionally felt completely normal to me.


I can't really say I was "forced by my family to watch the shop," because they didn't call me to go after every class, nor did they treat me as free labor and constantly demand my help.


So the conflict between me and my family wasn't strictly about "whether I should mind the store," but rather about "whether I should continue studying." They originally thought it was more realistic to start working earlier. But later, I felt precisely because I had already seen what working was like, I wanted even more to fight for a different path for myself.


I've now graduated with a master's degree. Before finishing high school, I'd never even thought about going to university. In fact, during high school, I wanted to start working, but my mom refused. Still, my mom didn't really want me to go to university either. She thought my academic performance was average, getting into a good university would be difficult, and even if I graduated, I'd eventually end up working anyway—so I might as well start earning money earlier. In my parents' original plan, the better path for me was to work. Our family shop was small and didn't need me to stay permanently, so they never expected me to "take over" the family business.


What truly made me decide to go to university was my internship at a hotel front desk. At the time, I realized this kind of work was really exhausting; if I didn't continue studying, my future would likely just be working jobs or helping at the shop—feeling like I could see my entire life laid out in front of me.


I met a friend who came here to teach at a Confucius Institute, and through talking with him, I realized that people who did well in school could live such different lives. It was from that point on that I slowly began to genuinely want to take the university entrance exam. The idea of going to university was finally sparked.


Later, I bought study materials myself and reviewed using a translator, studying for a year before taking the exam. My parents didn't support my going to university at first; they preferred that I go work and earn money. Because of their lack of support, I also felt hesitant about going to university later on. But I thought, I've already studied this much—why not just give it a try? In the end, I received an admission offer from the University of Coimbra.



Although my parents didn't support me, they didn't interfere with my decision, and in the end, they paid my university tuition. My younger brother is now doing very well in school; I think he might have been inspired by my efforts back then.


Since my Portuguese wasn't very good when I graduated high school, I chose geography for my undergraduate major—it was relatively easier for me. After finishing my bachelor's degree, the pandemic hit, making jobs hard to find, so I went on to do a master's. My family covered the tuition for both my bachelor's and master's degrees. This also shows they weren't completely against my studying—they just initially thought this path wasn't suitable for me or particularly useful. Later, they gradually realized that studying at least improved my Portuguese and enabled me to help with many things at home, like seeing doctors or renewing residence cards.


After graduating, I first worked for several years at a Chinese company in Portugal, but quit because I didn't like the work environment. After that, I helped at the family shop while looking for a job on my own. My family also felt that if I couldn't find a suitable job, coming back to help run their shop wouldn't be a bad option.


After living like this for a while, I began to feel lost. Especially after a trip to Yunnan earlier this year—I really loved it there—and when I returned to Portugal, I experienced a strong withdrawal effect, feeling completely unmotivated.


So now I've made up my mind to return to China; I've already been in Dali for over a month. I hadn't really left Portugal much before, and the thought of returning to China and starting everything anew is still scary, but excitement has overcome fear! Moreover, my family's attitude is very open-minded—they've always strongly supported my choices.
