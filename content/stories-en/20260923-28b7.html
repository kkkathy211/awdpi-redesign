Guest Introductions


Guest poster

JH: Hello everyone, I'm JH. I'm very happy to be part of this roundtable—it's actually my first time participating in this kind of discussion. I'm a PhD candidate at the School of Nursing at Queen's University Belfast. My research mainly focuses on the sexual and reproductive health and related rights of Chinese international students, and I also incorporate the concept of "masculinity" from gender studies to explore the relationship between masculinity and sexual and reproductive health. Coming from a nursing background, I've primarily focused on topics related to physical health, so it's only during my doctoral research that I've started engaging with theoretical concepts from sociology and gender studies. For me, today's discussion is also a learning process, and I hope we can spark some intellectual exchange together.

Yue: Hello everyone, I'm Yue. I'm currently a PhD candidate in the Department of Sociology at the University of Cambridge, entering my fourth year. My current research explores the lived experiences of Chinese women in intimate relationships with African men. My main method is ethnographic fieldwork: first, I spent over half a year in Guangzhou, immersing myself in local African communities and the lives of African students through participant observation. The second phase involves semi-structured interviews with Chinese women in Sino-African intimate relationships—I've interviewed about twenty so far. The fieldwork is still in its final stages, and I haven't formally begun data analysis, but I've already found some interesting preliminary insights. This is also my first time joining this kind of roundtable discussion, and I'm really looking forward to exchanging ideas with all of you.

Yiyun: Hello everyone, my name is Yiyun. I'm currently in my second year of a PhD in the Department of Sociology at the University of Cambridge, and I'm glad to be part of this discussion—the theme is deeply connected to my research. From my master's to my PhD, my work has consistently centered on women's bodies. During my master's, I focused on how women narrate their childbirth experiences on social media, especially stories about childbirth pain and birth-related injuries. In my PhD, I've shifted to the field of assisted reproduction, primarily examining the assisted reproductive practices of single Chinese women and lesbian couples. One of my core questions is: when there is no male partner involved in a family, how do these women select sperm donors? And what racialized notions or aesthetic standards lurking beneath these choices influence their decisions? Methodologically, I use multi-sited ethnography, including digital ethnography on social media, in-depth one-on-one interviews (with both the women and various practitioners along the assisted reproduction industry chain), and actual community observation, to trace these women's real fertility journeys. So I feel that whether it's the pain of childbirth during my master's research, or now the choices around assisted reproduction, my work has always circled back to one core word—body.

Jing: Thank you all for your introductions—your research is truly fascinating. Let's move to the first question, inspired by your work: at what moment did you clearly realize that the body is not a private choice?

I. Is the body a private choice?

Yiyun: I'll start from my own research. When I think about this question, my first image is a very visceral one—the process of childbirth. Childbirth may seem like the most intimate and personal bodily experience for women, but in reality, it is often a site of multiple power struggles.

You may recall a social incident that occurred on August 31, 2017, at the First Hospital of Yulin City in Shaanxi Province: a pregnant woman named Ma Rongrong, suffering unbearable pain during labor, repeatedly requested a cesarean section but never received a clear response. Eventually, she jumped to her death from the fifth floor of the hospital's maternity center. This incident sparked widespread public outrage at the time, but even now, the hospital and the family have not clarified who exactly refused the cesarean, or who should be held responsible—each side still insists on their own version.

This case reveals several critical points. First is the medical system's logic of "defensive medicine." In this incident, the woman had signed an authorization form while conscious, granting her husband full authority to make decisions on her behalf during childbirth. The hospital used this document to justify rejecting her own request for a cesarean. This exposes how, under rigid medical procedures and the hospital's defensive logic aimed at avoiding medical disputes, the laboring woman's immediate physical sensations and autonomous will are subordinated to procedural compliance. Moreover, many public hospitals in China have performance targets for "cesarean section rates"—a well-intentioned policy meant to prevent over-medicalization, but in practice, it often reduces complex bodily experiences and women's immediate needs to cold administrative data, forcing doctors' clinical judgments and patients' individual wishes to sometimes compromise with data-driven evaluations.

The second point is equally important: familial-level maternal discipline, and the cultural logic of "fetal interests above all" operating in the background. At the time, the family's reason for refusing a cesarean may have been statements like "children born vaginally are smarter" or "we think she can deliver naturally, so why have a cesarean?" In my own research on painless childbirth, I often see family members refusing epidurals for laboring women, citing "anesthesia will affect the baby" as the reason. Whether a woman can receive pain relief is often not her decision alone—it's the result of discussions among her husband, in-laws, and others around her. At such moments, a woman's body seems to become nothing more than a vessel for reproduction, stripped of the right to speak for herself. Moreover, whenever even the slightest conflict arises between maternal and fetal interests, ethics seem to instinctively demand that the woman endure—even when her life isn't under threat. Childbirth pain, in a way, becomes moralized— as if only by enduring this suffering can she pass the moral test of "being a good mother." So for me, it was precisely on the delivery bed that I saw most clearly: Decisions about the body are never private choices.

JH: My research also starts from reproduction, closely related to what Yiyun just described. From my findings, women do not have full autonomy in deciding whether to give birth—whether to bring a child into the world or to terminate a pregnancy (abortion) is often not solely her decision.

I conducted a mixed-methods study: an initial large-scale quantitative survey, followed by one-on-one semi-structured interviews selected based on survey results. In the initial survey, I found that only about 44% (less than half) of participants believed that a pregnant woman should be allowed to have an abortion or terminate pregnancy only under certain circumstances; about 30% believed that a person should be able to terminate a pregnancy for any reason; and 25% believed that abortion or pregnancy termination is wrong under any circumstances. This data surprised me—it shows that not many people believe women should have full autonomy over whether to terminate a pregnancy.

I haven't yet deeply explored why 44% of respondents believe termination should only be allowed "under certain circumstances." But some reasons may be what Yiyun just mentioned—pressure from husbands, from older generations, and various social barriers around "whether this child should be born," rather than the pregnant woman's own simple desire or lack thereof. So this data also reflects that decisions about women's bodies are not something they can fully decide on their own but are influenced by various external factors. In addition, the imposition of certain norms or traditional, stereotypical behavioral expectations from the other half—most often male partners—can also affect women's choices, whether in reproduction or in sexual behavior. Often, the decision to engage in sex is not entirely voluntary; it may result from a partner's persuasion or various pressures, leading you to engage in the act out of necessity rather than genuine personal desire. Therefore, whether in terms of sexual behavior or reproduction, women are often in a passive position.

Jing: Could you please provide more details about the background and gender distribution of your research participants?

JH: My research subjects are primarily Chinese international students—aged 18 and above, including undergraduates, master's, and doctoral students. Because the research topic involves masculinity, most participants are male, with fewer female participants.

Yiyun: Just now JH mentioned sexual consent, and it reminded me of a film called *Never, Rarely, Sometimes, Always*, which I highly recommend. It explores exactly where the boundaries of sexual consent lie within intimate relationships, including how the female protagonist, due to such experiences, has to face an abortion alone. I think this film resonates strongly with today's theme.

Still from the film *Never, Rarely, Sometimes, Always*

Yue: Earlier discussions focused more on whether bodily autonomy exists during the reproductive process. Drawing from my own research, I'd like to shift focus to the consequences of reproduction. Suppose a Chinese woman and a Black man have a Sino-African mixed-race child—what kind of situation would she face? Can her reproductive choice or partner choice truly be seen as a purely personal decision based on love?

Judging from their actual experiences, such choices are not treated as private matters. On the contrary, the public expects women's bodies and reproductive functions to serve broader national or ethnic narratives. For example, Chinese women who give birth to Sino-African mixed-race children often face widespread online harassment in both daily life and on the internet, and are surrounded by criticism in real life. Common accusations include "tainting the bloodline of the descendants of Yan and Huang," "polluting the lineage," and "being traitors to the nation." Another frequently heard phrase is "men guard the national borders, women guard the bloodline." From this expression, it becomes clear that women's bodies and their reproductive functions are viewed as tools for maintaining national or ethnic boundaries.—once the use of this "tool" exceeds what is socially acceptable, such as when, under China's existing racial ideologies still influenced by white supremacy, there is a widespread belief that "mixed-race children are more beautiful and intelligent"—though this usually refers specifically to children born with white partners. When the mixing involves Black partners, attitudes shift dramatically. Due to prevailing stereotypes that portray Black people as "backward" or "uncivilized," a Chinese woman choosing to date a Black man and bear a Sino-African mixed-race child may be seen as "violating the expected trajectory toward whiteness," thus facing widespread opposition. When we examine the outcomes of reproduction—what kind of child is acceptable, who can be recognized as Chinese or as an "excellent" descendant—it becomes evident that these are not matters fully within women's control.

II. What Does Autonomy Really Mean?

Jing: Thank you to all three guests for your insights. We've discussed reproduction, sexual consent, and partner choice, and throughout these conversations, one question has consistently emerged: What does autonomy really mean? In cases of sexual consent, if there is no explicit coercion; in the case of cesarean section options, if there is no explicit prohibition; and in partner choice, if there is no rigid requirement dictating whom one must choose—then when our surrounding social environment consistently assigns different values to different options, and when access to these options varies greatly, according to what standard should we distinguish between autonomous choices and choices constrained by structural forces?

Yiyun: I'd like to address this question from two angles. First, drawing from my fieldwork: I've conducted approximately 35 interviews so far, some of which include lesbian couples together. Among them, about half chose to undergo IVF in China, and half chose to go abroad. A key premise is this: unmarried women in China face clear institutional exclusion—the country's assisted reproductive technologies are only available to married heterosexual couples as a treatment for infertility, and cannot be used by single women or queer individuals as a means to form families. This means that if they want children, they must seek alternative paths: one is going abroad, the other is the domestic underground market.

These two paths differ significantly in terms of medical compliance and legal safeguards, with the most crucial difference being the enormous price gap—taking my interviewees as an example, completing the entire process in the US costs roughly 500,000 to 600,000 RMB; the domestic underground market is about 100,000 to 150,000 RMB; the cheapest case in my research was in a Southeast Asian country, costing 50,000 to 60,000 RMB—all these figures assume a single successful attempt. Therefore, when these women are choosing between "500,000 RMB for compliance and safety" and "100,000 RMB for high-risk underground options," is this truly an autonomous choice? No one is forcing them to go underground, but as a researcher, one criterion I use to distinguish between these two situations is to ask them a counterfactual question: "If price were not a consideration, what would you choose?" In that case, almost everyone would say without hesitation, "Then of course I would still go to a legitimate place." This reveals one point: When institutions deprive them of the legal right to reproduce in their own country, price becomes a form of invisible coercion. Women who ultimately choose underground clinics are not inherently inclined toward high risk; rather, their economic and cultural capital is insufficient to cover the 500,000 RMB premium—this premium may be for safety, compliance, or other implicit benefits, such as securing American citizenship for the child. Therefore, although they appear to be making their own choices, they are in fact operating within structurally limited options, using their bodies to take risks in exchange for economic feasibility.

The second point concerns what kind of sperm donor people choose when they seemingly have free choice—this is a question I find particularly interesting. Whether undergoing treatment abroad or domestically, in my cases, over 70% to 80% of participants actively chose foreign sperm donors, and by "foreign," they often exclude Black, Indian, or any non-white male groups. This is, of course, their "free and autonomous" choice—a common statement being: "Since I've already spent the money, already come to the U.S., and have to choose, why would I pick someone I could easily meet on the streets of China?" At this moment, they consider: should they have a fully Chinese child, or a mixed-race child? And this mixed-race child is invariably expected to be of Chinese and white descent. When people say, "I just want the best for my child, I want them to have a head start, to be born with certain physical advantages,"—I do not intend to judge these personal choices. I fully understand a woman, especially a single woman, wanting to give her child the best she can offer. But we need to ask: what lies behind this? One is the medical and commercial system's enforced quantification of sperm selection, offering them a dropdown menu to choose from; the other is that, regardless of whatever racialized implicit standards are at play, this process subtly manipulates aesthetic preferences, making everyone believe that mixed-race children with white partners represent a superior, more future-oriented choice. These are the two facets I'd like to share.

Cheryl: I wonder whether the women who choose white sperm donors consider the national composition of "whiteness"—do they only consider white people from developed countries, such as Western or Northern Europe, or do they also consider other "white" groups, such as North Indians or Eastern Europeans, who are technically Slavic white ("white other")? In other words, beyond race, do they also take socioeconomic background into account?

Yiyun: An excellent question. This point needs to be answered from two angles. First, in terms of accessibility: in China, the only channel for women choosing white sperm is through underground agencies, and these donor brokers also exist in a very underground (underground) manner. So the resources available are essentially white men living in China—such as international students, models, or foreign teachers. In terms of overall population composition, white donors from Russia and Eastern European countries are more common, while those from Western Europe and North America are fewer and less willing to donate. Therefore, for women in China who choose "foreign sperm," they don't have many options; nationality is not the primary consideration—more important is the person's appearance. As long as the individual "looks white" in their eyes, the country of origin becomes less significant. But when I specifically asked about nationality—such as, "Would you accept someone from Russia?"—some paused and said, "Maybe not, I'm afraid the child might turn out too violent." When asked, "What about Eastern Europe?" they replied, "Also not really, those places are too backward." Once nationality is specified, considerations of socioeconomic background begin to surface. But if the agency only offers donors from Eastern Europe or Russia, that may become their only available choice.

Yue: When I think about this issue of "free choice," I wonder: where exactly is the boundary between freedom and unfreedom? Often, when we make choices—like dating or selecting a partner—we believe it's entirely based on personal preference, taste, or whether there's a "chemistry" between us. It seems like a deeply personal decision. But are these choices constrained by structural limitations, or shaped by society's "correct answers"? I think a useful question to ask is: what consequences would I face if I didn't make this choice?

Is even romantic love itself a free choice? Or is choosing *not* to love a free choice? Between loving and not loving, we know that if you choose to stay single, ten years ago in our country, the discourse of "leftover women" was extremely prevalent—if you were in your late twenties, thirties, and hadn't entered marriage or a relationship, you'd face social stigma. Even female PhD students faced the label of "older leftover women" back then. At that time, women didn't truly have the freedom to remain outside marriage. Now, this "leftover women" discourse is gradually fading. Today's panelists are all female PhDs. But when it comes to freedom in romantic choices—now that we have so many dating apps, we seem to be able to freely browse, match, and follow our hearts—can we really say these choices are truly free?

For my interviewees, for example, entering a romantic or marital relationship with a Black man is often not even a thought that arises when they're swiping on dating apps—it's simply not an "option." A previous PhD student's research on Chinese women dating in the UK found that they often prioritize white men first, then Chinese men from the same country, but choices like men from the Middle East or Black men rarely appear in their minds at all. Why is this option excluded? In fact, when we make dating or marriage choices, there is already a set of social standards defining what counts as "handsome" or "beautiful," and what kind of masculinity is "ideal"—more progressive, considerate, gentle, and feminist-aware. In contrast, Black men are often hypersexualized in stereotypes, seen as rough, physically strong, but also patriarchal. Asian men, on the other hand, are frequently stereotyped as overly soft, lacking masculinity, and desexualized. There are also perceptions about capital such as education and economic status—like "people from African countries may not have received good education" or "their hometowns are backward and uncivilized." These stereotypes, along with mainstream societal definitions of "beauty" and "superior masculinity," have already invisibly excluded many people from our pool of potential partners for dating or marriage.

Jing: I have a question—what you just described involves the hypersexualization of Black men and the desexualization of Asian men in relation to Asian women. But does this process work in reverse for Asian women? For example, in the UK context, many Asian women are "Orientalized," which is also a form of sexualization. Among your interviewees, have you interviewed any Black men about their stereotypes of Chinese women?

Yue: I haven't formally interviewed any Black men—my current formal one-on-one interviewees are all Chinese women. But I've heard some views from Black men during casual conversations with them as friends: they also have a certain (I'm not sure if I can call it) fetish for Chinese women—in their eyes, Chinese women tend to fit the stereotype of being "submissive," "shy," and "embarrassed." But interestingly, one Black male interviewee told me that what he likes isn't just the label of "Chinese woman," but a sense of contrast—the surface-level shyness, submissiveness, and obedience, yet in intimate relationships, they are simultaneously very open and uninhibited. He particularly likes this contrast. Of course, this is just an individual case; I haven't broadly surveyed Black men's views on Chinese women.

Jing: Thank you. A friend in the comments wants to know more about sperm bank-based conception—do sperm banks provide information about donors' appearance, education, occupation, nationality, etc.?

Yiyun: Sperm banks come in two types. One is internationally strictly regulated and places greater emphasis on donor privacy—usually only providing childhood photos, while adult photos are shared only if the donor consents, not required. The entire sperm bank website resembles a shopping list: the most important item is ethnicity, followed by nationality, eye color, hair color, height, education, major, occupation. Health and genetic history are fundamental prerequisites. There are also optional extras—for example, donors can write a personal statement explaining why they want to donate. Women can filter step by step according to their own criteria, eventually narrowing it down to ten or a few candidates, then discuss with family—this is also "autonomous choice," but often it's not a decision one person can make alone. Some interviewees told me that after shortlisting five candidates, they held a family meeting where everyone voted together.

As for the question, "If appearance isn't disclosed, how can you ensure the child will be good-looking?" Most sperm banks do provide childhood photos of donors. Additionally, domestic sperm banks in China are relatively less strictly regulated and more like a wild, uncontrolled market—often adult photos are available, and in many cases, women can even request interviews with donors, either online or offline (though these women protect themselves, for example by wearing masks or not turning on video). But ultimately, whether the child will be "good-looking" can only be known after birth.

Jing: Do people care about the donor's education when selecting sperm? Are they worried about their future child's intelligence?

Yiyun: This actually divides into two camps. One camp highly values intelligence and insists on donors with master's or doctoral degrees, even if it means sacrificing height or appearance. The other camp believes appearance is more likely to be hereditary, while intelligence isn't necessarily, and education doesn't equal intelligence, so they don't focus much on educational background. But overall, people ultimately make a comprehensive assessment—most, even if they claim not to care about education, will still somewhat require the donor to be at least a college graduate.

III. The Racialization of Masculinity and the "Fragile" Capital

Jing: Let's return to the issue we discussed earlier with Yue—the desexualization of Chinese men. Cheryl mentioned in the comments that current trends in male aesthetics seem to be leaning more toward "de-masculinization"—softer, more vulnerable, rather than traditional masculinity. Following this trend, Asian and Black men should theoretically be more popular. But how do we explain the disadvantage Chinese men face in the dating market?

Yue: That's a great question, and I've thought about it. Recently, when I attended a conference at Ewha Womans University in Korea, one speaker mentioned that this kind of masculinity, promoted through boy bands (like BTS), has become a global phenomenon. Indeed, Korean masculinity has gained global visibility—through K-pop and Korean drama cultural exports, the more vulnerable, caring, and gentle masculinity displayed by BTS has gained a certain level of popularity and has improved the desirability of Korean men in the marriage and dating market. But I don't think this can be generalized to the entire Asian male group, because this "Korean wave" is limited strictly to Korean men.

Another point is that this "popular masculinity" or hegemonic masculinity may not only refer to personality traits like softness or gentleness, but also includes other factors we consider when evaluating a partner, such as resources, aesthetics, and so on. Recently, Jiang Sida and Lu Yu discussed the topic of "vulnerability" in an interview—Jiang Sida's view is that although vulnerability seems to be a popular trait now, people have expectations for the "outcome" of this vulnerability: vulnerability must be paired with something else; it might only serve as a narrative for "looking back on the process after you've already succeeded"—people will say, oh, so you also experienced vulnerability or failure on your path to success, and thus sympathize with you and feel more compassion for you. But if you don't have that "successful outcome," you might lose the "capital" of vulnerability. So I believe vulnerability cannot be viewed merely as a temperament—the admiration for vulnerability may not necessarily make groups with this trait more popular in the marriage and dating market; vulnerability must be considered in combination with other factors.

"Vulnerable" masculinity

Jing: I have another question: for example, in Western markets, Asian or Chinese men are often seen as more submissive or vulnerable, but in China, we also see many cases of male-perpetrated domestic violence, including what's called China-specific toxic masculinity. For the same group of people, why do they display such different masculine traits when facing different audiences?

JH: Let me speak. Although my research topic involves masculinity, I only began studying this area during my PhD and am still learning. Regarding hegemonic masculinity and toxic masculinity, whether in China or overseas, a portion of men globally exhibit these traits. As for why their behaviors differ toward different people—I think it relates to their relative social position when facing different groups. When they face more marginalized or vulnerable groups, they may feel entitled to assert their masculinity; but when facing those more powerful than them, such as superiors or people they can't afford to confront, they suppress such traits or behaviors. Especially in intimate relationships between men and women, men are generally in a dominant position, so they are more likely to express their masculinity—not seeing it as "toxic" or "domineering," but rather as fulfilling their role as head of household, taking responsibility and making decisions, such as whether to have children. But sometimes this masculinity becomes excessive, potentially leading to violent tendencies. When their actions cause harm, they may not see anything wrong, believing they are simply exercising their rights, with a sense of "establishing family rules." Later, they might say, "Sorry, I won't do it again," but in fact, they are unconsciously displaying a very negative form of hegemonic masculinity.

But when they face, for example, Chinese or Asian men in a white Western world, they may instead be in a relatively subordinate position—disadvantaged in terms of physical build shaped by power dynamics and race—so they may display a more polite, submissive (not necessarily vulnerable) side, because they are in a lower hierarchical position and thus refrain from showing their more aggressive side. In other words, their behavior changes depending on the environment.

Yue: I just thought of something: the context itself is different. For example, when talking about Asian men in the U.S., their historical background involves the restrictions faced by early Asian laborers entering America—they were channeled into certain types of jobs, possibly to prevent these laborers from threatening local job opportunities, so they were assigned to work considered more "feminine," and their romantic relationships with white women were strictly restricted. It was precisely within this historical context that the stereotype of "Asian men as gentle, non-threatening, and desexualized" emerged. But this stereotype doesn't apply in China, because the historical trajectory and context in China are different. The same group labeled "Chinese men" can carry vastly different images in different settings—the groups themselves are not the same. Although they can be collectively called "Chinese men" by nationality, Chinese men in China form a very broad group involving different classes, educational backgrounds, and urban-rural divides, with high diversity; but when placed in a Western context, such as Chinese men in the U.S., their class position and forms of capital become relatively homogenized—the Asian American population, in terms of overall income and education level, certainly differs from the general profile of Chinese men in China. So I think these two situations cannot be equated.

Jing: We just talked about toxic masculinity. Taking myself as an example, this is something I've heard repeatedly growing up: "I'm doing this for your own good; you may not understand why now, but I'm doing it for your sake." Then comes the question: when does this "for your own good" become making decisions for her?

IV. When "For Your Own Good" Becomes Control

Yue: When thinking about this question, I wonder: can this kind of "for your own good" be rejected? If someone truly has your best interests at heart, they should primarily—offer help when you need it; if you don't need it, they can withdraw, respecting your right to freely develop or make your own choices. But if it's a patronizing form of support or help disguised as concern and used to control—if you reject this help, the other person might fly into a rage, or see you as disobedient, and respond with even harsher attacks.

For example, in my research, women entering Sino-African romantic relationships often receive "well-meaning warnings" from people around them, advising them to carefully assess their partners or discouraging them from entering such intimate relationships—because there are many news stories claiming "this man will eventually run away, leaving you with the child," or warning them not to return with their husband to his hometown, as it's widely circulated online that "once you go back, he'll tear up your passport and you'll never be able to return to China." There was also a widely discussed incident: a popular blogger on Xiaohongshu, one of the earlier Sino-African couple influencers, posted a photo saying she was going to West Africa with her husband, then disappeared from updates for a long time. Netizens became extremely worried, assuming she had been deceived and gone missing in West Africa, launching a full-scale online "rescue" campaign, including contacting embassies to verify her situation. On the surface, this seemed like genuine concern, but when she later updated her status saying she was doing well and continued using her account to share daily life as a Sino-African family—this "concern" didn't disappear; instead, those who once cared for her turned into people cursing or mocking her, withdrawing their support and responding with even greater hostility to her rejection of their help with "I don't need your help."

At such moments, this "support" is no longer "I see you have a need, so I help you," but instead ignores the woman's own voice and needs—these netizens already have a fixed narrative in mind about "what will happen if a Chinese woman marries an African man," and they impose this narrative onto women entering Sino-African intimate relationships, ignoring their own explanations and stories—even when these women repeatedly clarify that their marriages and families are happy, those who once eagerly wanted to help them still use a deeply entrenched narrative of "the disastrous consequences of Sino-African relationships" to warn them. At such moments, this kind of help and support is no longer based on the woman's actual needs, but becomes a patriarchal, paternalistic form of care and control.

Yiyun: Yue just spoke very well. I think every woman has had such experiences—growing up, for example, I wanted to study humanities, but my parents insisted I study science, saying "this is for your own good"; I didn't want to marry, but they wanted me to, saying "this is for your own good, so you'll have someone to keep you company later"; I didn't want to have children, but they insisted, saying "this is for your own good, so someone can take care of you in old age"—there are always such phrases.

I'd like to share a concrete scenario closely related to my research: many people urge pregnant women to "go natural birth—it's better, recovery is faster." This advice may seem, at first glance, to come from a place of caring for women, considering their future physical health or body recovery. But the underlying logic is often something like "this way the baby will be smarter, with a rounder head," or similar reasoning. After childbirth, there's also the strong emphasis on breastfeeding—"breastfeeding is good, it clears the mammary ducts and reduces the risk of breast cancer"—again, all framed as being "for the mother's future health." I've read some medical literature; although I'm not medically trained, both natural birth and breastfeeding do have statistical support at the macro level. But precisely because of this "scientific correctness," it becomes a very subtle form of discipline or rhetoric—when the medical system or family repeatedly emphasizes these long-term benefits, it carries a clear agenda: making you feel that they care more about your macro health indicators, lower medical costs, or the fulfillment of "perfect motherhood," rather than the real, immediate person in front of them. When she's already in so much pain that she wants a C-section but can't have one, or when breastfeeding causes cracked nipples and unbearable pain, and you keep telling her "natural birth is better, breastfeeding is better"—at that moment, the so-called "benefits" at the macro level are actually used to deny, suppress, or cover up the woman's real, immediate suffering. So "for her own good" clearly turns into a form of control—something akin to the paternalism under patriarchy that Yue mentioned.

Does JH have any additional medical perspectives to share?

JH: What you just said made me reflect a bit—perhaps because my research usually focuses on human health, I tend to prioritize health above all else, but sometimes I may not fully consider her actual psychological needs in the moment. For example, I might think a certain behavior or decision is best for someone's health, so I put health first. But as you pointed out, sometimes in that very moment, a woman's psychological and physical feelings are uncomfortable, and at that point, we should pay more attention to her immediate feelings and thoughts.

V. Health: Established Fact, or Socially Constructed Knowledge?

Jing: Thank you all for your insights—I've found them very enlightening. One question I'm now reflecting on is: when we talk about "health," is this "health" an established fact? Or is it actually defined by various societal gazes and forms of discipline? For example, breastfeeding is promoted to prevent future breast cancer, but your current pain is itself a form of unhealthiness—why should these "unhealthy" experiences be sacrificed for the sake of a so-called "healthy" future?

JH: I think we need to look at this dialectically. If we disregard the individual and take the perspective of the medical or health field as a whole, certain quantifiable, straightforward data can tell us what health is—like medical check-up reports indicating which ranges are positive or negative. These are relatively objective data. In sexual health, for instance, high-risk sexual behaviors or not using condoms directly affect your likelihood of disease prevention—these are also relatively objective indicators that can tell you whether you're healthy or not.

But at the same time, something like mental health is more "abstract" for the individual, because there's no direct data to show whether you're healthy—although there are psychological scales, these are mostly tools to help doctors understand your current state, not truly comprehensive reflections of your inner well-being. So in such cases, one's personal feelings may be more important. Even though they're harder to measure and express, that doesn't make them less significant—sometimes a scale may say you're healthy, but you personally feel deeply uncomfortable. At such moments, I think we need to look at things dialectically: sometimes, you need to listen more to your inner self, and the immediate discomfort matters more, so you need to speak up. But medical interventions, I think, are more like a last resort—when you're truly at your breaking point, these medical measures are what truly support your health. Not sure if I've explained this clearly.

Yiyun: I'd like to add something about what I just said—I'm not trying to fully encourage everyone to say, "I'm in too much pain now, so I'll sacrifice my future," or to stand completely against "health." Rather, I want to emphasize that recognition of a woman's individual experience (acknowledgement) needs to be seen and heard. We can provide objective scientific health information, but we must fully return the decision-making power to the person who is actually enduring the pain or discomfort. And importantly, we must give her the right to "cry out in pain.".

In my previous research, one interviewee shared a conversation that deeply pained me: she was lying on a delivery bed with several other women, all at different stages of labor, all in pain. She had a lower pain tolerance and cried out loudly a few times. A passing doctor or nurse dismissed her sharply: "What are you shouting for? No one else is shouting—why do you have to?" This kind of remark, I think, is a complete erasure of her subjectivity. We need to grant her the right to cry out in pain, rather than using phrases like "everyone else can endure it—why can't you?" or "all women go through this," to downplay or deny her real, immediate experience. This is probably the key point I most want to express.

Yue: I'm reminded that medicine does have scientifically proven conclusions about which behaviors are healthier. But when it comes to whether this is support or control, I think the boundary lies in: who holds the final decision-making power. For example, for a woman deciding whether to breastfeed, as doctors or family members, we can provide her with this information—but must we force her to breastfeed, or can she, after considering this information along with her own feelings, have the right to decide what to do—when this right to choose disappears, it's no longer "support," but rather a form of "control.".

For example, a paternalistic mother-in-law, under the guise of "for your own good," pressures a woman to persist with breastfeeding; or in interracial relationships, online commenters or family members, under the pretense of "for your future marital happiness," try to stop women from entering cross-racial relationships—here, they're not treating women as subjects with their own judgment and autonomy, but as ignorant, naive, easily deceived, and in need of discipline and guidance. This is a very condescending form of control or belittlement, cloaked in concern—the attitude of "you don't understand, I know better than you."

Jing: I see a question in the chat—we can return to the discussion on "aesthetics of vulnerability" during the Q&A. Regarding health, everyone has touched on the fact that we're living in a patriarchal society, and the prevailing view seems to be: subjectivity should return to the individual, allowing her the right to make her own choices.

I'm not from a medical background, so JH, please feel free to correct me—but I once read an article stating that menstrual pain in women can actually be treated, yet there's currently insufficient funding or medical personnel focused on this issue. The most common solution for women's menstrual pain is simply taking painkillers, and the dosage standards for these painkillers are based on adult male benchmarks—yet men and women's bodies react differently to medication and require different dosages. Even side effects are tested primarily on adult males and may differ in women's bodies. Kris, do you have anything to share on this?

JH: Regarding clinical research on menstruation or related areas, I'm not a medical student, so my understanding may be similar to everyone else's. But this is indeed a common phenomenon—whether in clinical trials or other areas, society tends to use men as the primary research subjects. There are also objective reasons: women's anatomical structures and organ distributions are indeed more intricate and complex than men's, so using men as research subjects often makes it easier to obtain results and avoid complications in medical experiments. This is also a risk- and funding-driven consideration, leading researchers to prioritize male subjects—there is some objectivity here. But we can't deny that because our society is patriarchal and male-dominated, many things are indeed done in men's interests, without considering women's own rights and needs. So this is indeed a widespread phenomenon. But more and more researchers and experts are now paying attention to this area and making corresponding changes, such as placing greater focus on women's reproductive health research and studies on women's daily lives—progress is visible.

Jing: Thank you, Kris, for sharing. We've just talked a lot about women's bodies and masculinity—why is it that whenever we discuss women, we always end up circling back to the topic of masculinity? Can we view "masculinity" as a form of gaze imposed by society and men upon women? And how does this idea of masculinity affect women's autonomous choices?

Six: Myths about Masculinity

JH: Let me go first—I do think masculinity can be seen as a form of gaze on women, one that prevents women from making certain decisions autonomously, whether in intimate relationships or in sexual and reproductive behaviors. We live in a patriarchal society where men still hold relatively greater discursive power, which subtly influences the behaviors and decisions women make.

Yiyun: I have a very concrete example. Among my interviewees—whether single women or lesbian couples—99% said that because they were undergoing IVF, they had the option to choose their baby's sex, and most actively chose to have a girl. While a small number of them admitted this was partly due to feminist beliefs that led to a kind of "aversion to men," the larger reason was this: as women raising a child in an all-female household, they felt they wouldn't be able to raise a boy who met society's standards—"I don't know how to raise a boy" was a major consideration. In fact, one couple, when faced with no other option but to have a boy, began worrying early on about "how to cultivate his masculinity," even buying parenting books on "how to raise a boy." I think this reflects how deeply "naturalized" masculinity is in our society—so much so that these women felt they lacked the ability to foster it.

For single women, a core question is often: "My child won't have a father—how should I deal with that?" Women with stronger critical awareness or subjectivity might respond, "OK, family structures can be diverse." But for many women deeply influenced by traditional values, this is a significant concern that profoundly shapes their parenting practices—for instance, they may hope that a brother, uncle, grandfather, or male friend can serve as an important male role model in the child's life. Regardless, the presence of a male role model is seen as a prerequisite for the child's healthy or "normal" development—this is one small observation I've made.

Yue: When thinking about the concepts of masculinity and femininity, it feels as though these two ideas were constructed and designed from the beginning—masculinity is defined in a certain way, and femininity is positioned as its opposite. So, as Yiyun just mentioned, if I am a woman who identifies with traditional femininity, I might feel that I am incapable of raising a boy with masculinity—because in our imagination, masculinity and femininity are opposites: whatever masculinity is, femininity must be its reverse. Under this constructed framework, masculinity and femininity actually define and confirm each other by drawing boundaries—defining what each "should" be.

Then, are the expectations placed on men also shaping how women should grow up? I think under this binary framework, yes, they are. But I also wonder: is it possible that men and women don't have to be opposites? If masculinity and femininity aren't inherently opposed, do we still need to distinguish between these two traits?—These are just some thoughts, perhaps without clear answers.

Jing: What motivates Yiyun's interviewees to cultivate masculinity in boys?

Yiyun: I think they don't even reflect on this motivation—it's more like an unquestioned assumption: "If I have a girl, this is how she should be; if I have a boy, this is how he should be." Although these families themselves may feel they are resisting traditional heterosexual family structures or norms, many are still deeply influenced by these ideas (not necessarily "troubled" by them, but certainly shaped by them to some extent). Sometimes, they even hope their family can, to some degree, resemble the traditional heterosexual nuclear family, so they appear more integrated into mainstream society. Therefore, for some of them, the idea that "a boy should have a certain kind of masculinity" is something that should naturally and inevitably manifest in the boy.

Jing: In sociology, when we mention masculinity, we usually add an adjective in front—"toxic." I feel this concept is generally seen in sociology as not particularly healthy. At the same time, when we discuss masculinity and femininity, we are actually talking about one thing—body politics. What do we really mean when we say "the body is political"?

Yue: I don't have a direct answer to "the body is political" right now, but when you mentioned toxic masculinity, I thought: whenever we talk about masculinity, it seems to carry a negative connotation—like hegemonic masculinity, which always feels like something bad. But actually, what I want to say is that hegemonic masculinity does not necessarily equate to toxic masculinity—toxic masculinity indeed refers to something very harmful and poisonous, but hegemonic masculinity more often refers to the dominant, widely admired form of masculinity in society. For example, regarding vulnerability, in today's South Korea, hegemonic masculinity might actually be embodied by men who show vulnerability. Some scholars have even envisioned a positive form of hegemonic masculinity—a man who acknowledges his vulnerability and limitations, doesn't insist on being dominant, can depend on women, and actively pursues gender equality. This kind of hegemonic masculinity could actually become a positive form of masculinity.

JH: I also want to say that the opposite of hegemonic or toxic masculinity isn't necessarily femininity—it doesn't have to be a binary opposition. In my research, I see the other side of traditional masculinity as a more fair, just, and equal gender norm—gender equality doesn't mean the opposite of hegemonic masculinity must be fragility or gentleness. Rather, it encourages greater equality: men can still have certain masculine traits while also embracing qualities typically associated with femininity. This is fluid—everyone can have some masculinity and some tenderness; it's not black and white.

Jing: This connects closely with a comment from someone in the discussion section—it seems more and more people no longer want their children to grow up under a traditional binary gender framework, but instead hope they can grow freely, with gender traits being less important, or leaning toward a blended, fluid state. What do you all think about this?

JH: I think this is social progress, and also the direction people are more willing to see. But we can't deny that hegemonic masculinity does exist. I think researchers study these concepts and define them precisely to follow a logic of "identifying problems to solve them"—we identify a phenomenon, then work to improve it, making it less extreme, less sharp, gradually integrating it so it's no longer so oppositional. It's not that the concept should disappear, but rather that our research and efforts aim to make masculinity less "hegemonic." Masculinity can certainly continue to exist, but it can become more equal and more positive. Whether masculinity or femininity, both can coexist—only we hope they evolve toward gender equality, becoming gentler and more inclusive.

Yiyun: I'd like to add a comment on the point mentioned earlier in the conversation—that "we originally assumed non-traditional parenting groups would try to avoid binary gender norms when raising children." First, my own research (including interviews) doesn't usually go into much detail about parenting practices; it generally ends at the point when they give birth, so my data isn't comprehensive enough. I bring this up because when these women say they are women, or that they are a family of only women, and that they "shouldn't" or "can't" raise a boy well, I think there's an underlying assumption here: that a boy should be raised in a certain family environment, and that he needs a certain kind of male role model in order to grow into a socially recognized boy—that might be their premise. But I believe that even among lesbian families who ended up having a boy, they certainly wouldn't say, "Well then, we'll just raise him with toxic masculinity." Similarly, when raising girls, they wouldn't necessarily want their daughters to conform to traditional femininity. I think what's more important (and worth paying attention to) is the motivation, hesitation, and anxiety behind the assumption about "who is fit to raise boys, who is fit to raise girls"—what exactly are they worried about? Why can't two women raise a boy well? These are questions we need to keep asking.

VII. Returning to Daily Life: The "Body" in Women's Everyday Experiences

Jing: We've discussed many academic topics in this conversation—about choice, agency—so now let's bring these discussions back into real life. How do these discussions about bodies, reproduction, and intimate relationships relate to the daily lives of ordinary women like us?

Yiyun: I think these issues have always existed in every woman's daily life—whether it's looking in the mirror and feeling upset because you think you've gained weight, or worrying about being judged or even subjected to violence when visiting a gynecologist. These are all deeply tied to the anxieties and concerns we face every day. I think it's very important to bring these issues into public discussion: it helps every woman recognize which pains and anxieties have been "normalized"—for example, the idea that "every girl should just endure period pain" or "giving birth must involve pain and tearing." You need to stop self-blaming, stop thinking it's "my fault," or "everyone else can handle it, why can't I?" "Why am I not strong enough?" "Why isn't my breast milk enough or good enough? Am I not a good mother?" These things need to be repeated over and over, so more people understand that these pains are only normalized, and you have every right to speak up and say it hurts, thereby stopping self-blame. We need to see these pains, acknowledge them, and heal them, instead of constantly being told to "be strong, endure it."

Moreover, on this basis, through our storytelling and through women sharing and witnessing each other's experiences, I believe we can generate a kind of collective power. Often, when we face bodily harm, reproductive struggles, or more common issues like body image anxiety, we feel deep shame or even loneliness, thinking, "Am I the only one like this?" But when we bring these private topics into public discussion, we realize that so many others have had similar experiences. This collective power, as I wrote in my master's thesis, is actually a "power of witnessing"—because we witness each other, we form a collective imagination (collective imagination) about how our future could actually be. Seeing those invisible, hidden issues allows each person to stop internalizing pain and self-blame, and to share more openly with one another—this way, in our daily lives, we can gradually reclaim control over our own bodies.

The Empathy River @ReillyPinkFish

Yue: I'm reminded of a current interesting trend: people say, "Stop internalizing pain; if you're suffering, blame the broader environment." I think this kind of thinking, to some extent, is actually a sociological perspective. Often, when we feel drained in the workplace or in romantic relationships, it's because we've internalized structural social problems, turning them into "my wrong choices" or "my personal inadequacy," making them individual suffering. But when we place these issues within the larger society, when we see whether others have similar experiences and recognize the broader social structures—including gender hierarchies, class and racial systems—and how they shape our individual lives, this awareness, to some extent, can already dissolve part of our pain, because our gaze no longer turns inward to criticize or blame ourselves, but outward to "blame the broader environment." This process of "externalization," I think, first helps us see that others share similar experiences, creating a sense of solidarity or connection; second, it helps us reduce self-criticism.

Jing: Thank you, Yiyun and Yue, for your sharing. JH, do you have anything you'd like to say?

JH: I think these discussions about women's bodies and autonomy are indeed very meaningful. Previously, my field focused more on "health," but later experts in related areas began discussing "rights" alongside "health," reflecting a growing attention to rights that directly concern us, especially women's autonomy. These topics are increasingly being recognized. As Yiyun just said, these discussions and research are very meaningful—we can see progress, seeing how such research and dialogue are becoming more grounded in our own lives, moving beyond just objective physical health to pay more attention to inner needs and our own rights. So these discussions are very useful—they're already making a real impact and becoming more helpful to us, even if the process may still be slow. We can see progress. This discussion, in particular, focuses more on women's empowerment and has taught me a lot. We should voice our thoughts, whether saying "yes" or "no"—we have the right to make decisions about our own actions.

When She Enters Public Space

VIII. Audience Question: Are Public Spaces Designed for Masculinity?

Jing: Thank you to the three guests for your insights—I've learned a lot. Now we're moving into the audience Q&A. I just saw a very interesting question from zixin: To what extent are social public activities and spaces largely open to masculinity? Not only are medical standards often based on male bodies, but in social and power arenas, is there also a bias toward masculine traits? What do the three guests think?

JH: I think that's indeed the case. It's undeniable that within the entire society and power structures, there is indeed a bias towards men, because we are a patriarchal country and society, and men still hold more discursive power. But we are also seeing more and more women beginning to step onto the stage, expressing their own ideas, and striving to make this scale more balanced and achieve greater gender equality.

Jing: But you just said that more women may now have a voice—on their way to gaining that voice, might they also have to compromise with masculinity? For example, our understanding of "leadership" often assumes you must be tough and able to control your subordinates. Or take something as simple as dress codes for women in political or formal settings—aren't these requirements actually borrowing from masculine norms?

JH: I think yes. On the path to gaining voice, women inevitably, to some extent, lower their heads to or lean toward masculinity—because to reclaim some power from men, the process may not be entirely voluntary, but rather involves subtle behaviors that align with masculinity, or choices in clothing, which one might not even realize at the time, only later, upon reflection, realizing that we sometimes slightly bowed or yielded to masculinity. That's my view.

Yue: I'm reminded of the recent widespread discussion about smoking bans in public places—I think the weak enforcement of such bans, and the widespread social acceptance of smokers lighting up in public in China, to some extent shows that public space is indeed open to masculinity. Although both men and women smoke, I tend to see smoking more as a symbol of masculinity—many people view smoking as a representation or signifier of masculine expectations. And the difficulty of enforcing smoking bans in public spaces, to some extent, reflects this very point.
