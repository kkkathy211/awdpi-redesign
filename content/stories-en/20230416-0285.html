Axelle Jah Njiké

A Cameroonian of African descent who grew up in Paris. As a podcast creator and feminist columnist, in 2015 she founded a website called "Let's Talk About Women's Pleasure," and in 2018 launched the highly inclusive podcast *Me, My Sexe and I*, dedicated to discussing intimate relationships among Black women. In autumn 2020, her second podcast, *The Girl on the Sofa*, focused on the issue of sexual abuse against children and adolescents within Black communities. In 2021, her third podcast, which explored Francophone Black feminism from the 1960s to the present (*I Am a Black Woman and I Don't Like Beyoncé*), made her the first African-descendant podcast producer for the documentary series of France Culture.


Introduction

Axelle believes many people fail to understand the value of engaging in dialogue with Black women at different stages of their personal and emotional lives. Talking about oneself and our intimate relationships often seems abnormal—especially when you are a woman. There seem to be few platforms offering Black women the space to discuss seemingly ordinary topics such as emotional relationships, parenting, and self-construction. This is precisely why she created her own podcasts.


Intimacy is a bridge to others. She believes in a feminism of liberation from within outward—a liberation tied to women's self-embodiment, to intimacy, and to the intersection between the personal and the political. At the same time, in this article, Axelle also emphasizes the issue of sexual violence against children within families, a topic she believes needs to be known and given greater attention.


The original text comes from the French feminist online magazine *Les Ourses à plume* and *Philosophie Magazine*. The following article is translated by the French team of Avoice's language support team.


Axelle Jah Njiké, creator of the podcast *Me, My Sexe and I*, advocates for liberation through inner self-realization.


Her work *The (Black) Woman's Intimate Diary* powerfully tells the story of a Cameroonian woman reclaiming control over her destiny, drawing readers into her private life and encouraging them "to achieve self-embodiment within one's intimate life, both literally and symbolically." She classifies her diary as a form of "non-denominational" feminism—one inextricably linked to the stories of the girls and women in her family. In her family, these women were never granted equal rights over their bodies and sexuality.


Q

When did you start writing this diary?

I began writing this book at the end of the 1990s. But at that time, to get it published, I was required to agree to delete the sections about women's publications. It was then that Raphaël Sorin, who discovered my work, struck me as an extraordinary person. He looked at me and said: "It's simple. If you just want a moment of fame, accept this deal and let that chapter disappear. But if you truly want to become a writer, publish the book exactly as it is." I sincerely told him: I want to become a writer. He then said: "Hold on, your time will come!"


Q

How did literature become a refuge for your mind?

The level of education in Cameroon was very good, so when I arrived in France and entered first grade, my teacher gave me the key to the school library and let me read while others studied. I devoured novels hungrily. For endings I was dissatisfied with, I rewrote them myself. When I thought the heroine could have made different choices, I rewrote the story in my notebook. Gradually, I began recording my thoughts. When I discovered *The Diary of Anaïs Nin*, I finally understood that a diary is also a literary medium..

*Image source: freepic*


As I said in my podcast, Black women's literature has allowed me to name many things. And erotic literature helped me reclaim bodily autonomy after being violated. For me, reading and writing are tools for self-definition. Reading helped me emerge from darkness and gradually accept everything I had experienced, allowing my "I" to be built.


The literary excerpts in my podcast are not decorative. I want to offer listeners two ways to enter my intimate world: either through reality—listening to testimonies from people featured in the podcast—or through fiction—listening to literary excerpts. These two different entry points lead to the same topic.




African societies always tell us that feminism is a matter for white women.



Q

Your work is titled *The (Black) Woman's Intimate Diary*. Why did you put "Black" in parentheses?

The book's title reflects a "situated" perspective. When I talk about my experience as a Black person, it is fundamentally different from the experience of someone born here. My background is unusual; even within the Black community, my viewpoint is both African and European. I was born in Africa but grew up and was educated in Europe. I grew up in an unusual family environment—I was raised by two Black men, and family relationships were not as central as in a typical family. I needed to escape that life.


I am a Black woman, but I was first a confused little girl living in an unusual life pattern, constantly exposed to violence within the family. My first sexual experience was not consensual. The only one who could defend me was myself. From that day on, I chose myself. I am not only Black; I am also Lili, I am also Axelle, I am also a survivor of sexual and educational violence. That is what the parentheses signify.


Q

Why are the words "intimacy" and "Black women" so rarely mentioned together?

In today's society, the collective is more important than the individual. And when the individual is a woman, this is often seen as disorder. To see oneself as an independent individual brings instability to society. This is the criticism we hear when discussing Black women's feminism. People tell us this is what white women do. They make the same criticism toward Asian or Indigenous women. Women have always been seen as a collective in the eyes of the world, so feminist advocacy has a long road ahead.




To some people I am not Black enough; to others, I am not white enough.



Q

You wrote in your book that people thought "you, as an African-descendant woman, are too advanced"...

To some people I am not Black enough; to others, I am not white enough. From the beginning, I have been caught in public discourse. African-descendant women face far too many issues... like the nickname "toubab" ("toubab," commonly used in West Africa to refer to anyone with white skin, regardless of nationality). In Africa, it is difficult for women to establish intimate relationships with men. Given my previous family experiences, it took me a long time to adapt to intimate relationships with African-descendant men. But no one ever cares about these issues. When I reconnected with my biological father, I slowly began to adapt to this relationship, because finally there was someone who wanted me to exist in this world, finally someone who loved me.


Q

You have several birth dates. What does birth mean to you?

I was born only once, yet I have three different birth dates. One is the official date, the one written on documents. But this date does not align with the love story behind the day my mother conceived me. These numbers accompany us throughout life and cannot be erased. My second birth date refers to the day I actually came into this world. The last one might be the day I learned I had a biological father.




Birth is not merely a biological event; behind birth, there can also be love.



Q

What does intimacy mean to you?

Specifically, I am interested in how each woman embodies herself within her gender. I believe in a feminism of liberation from within outward. Everything you do is connected to who you are, and therefore to your mother and the mothers before her. I carry all of them; passing on their desires is a revolutionary act for me. Everything I have done has been a response to the fates of my mother and my ancestors.


Intimacy is not an accessory. Everything changes, everything is permeable to external influences—nothing is sealed off. This fluidity is not a danger; on the contrary, it is the purest fiber of humanity.


In another interview, Axelle Jah Njiké spoke about her work *The Girl on the Sofa*, which addresses a harsh and real issue: domestic violence against minors, and her connection to Black feminism and/or African-descendant literature as a means of healing trauma.


Q

Some people may not have heard of this documentary podcast. Could you introduce it?

*The Girl on the Sofa* is a series within the podcast *Intime et Politique* (Intimate and Political) by Nouvelles Écoutes—a well-known French podcast platform. I conceived the storyline and content of this podcast, which deals with sexual violence against minors within families. Through six testimonies—from a girl, several African-descendant women, and myself—I explore the highly taboo issues of childhood and adolescent abuse and rape within Black communities.


Q

Is "The Girl on the Sofa" referring to you? Why do you feel the need to explore the personal and the political?

I host this podcast as a girl, a woman, a citizen, and a mother who has experienced sexual and educational violence since childhood. But I also do it as a Black woman. I want us not to forget, in conversations around sexual violence, all the intersecting layers of oppression. Sexual violence against children and adolescents is rampant in nearly every society, across every dimension. Black families are no exception. In my view, this violence reveals much about the vulnerability of children—especially within our (Black) families as a system, the family as a general social structure, and the status of children in society.


I want to address the extreme vulnerability of children, a vulnerability that makes them treated as property of their parents and the adults around them. I want to clarify that sexual violence against children is not a private or individual issue, but one that strikes at the heart of our social organization. I believe in this regard, the personal and the political are deeply intertwined.

Q

Why did you choose the documentary format as a platform to amplify the voices of survivors of intrafamilial sexual violence, further highlighting the uniqueness of your story?

The reality is, according to WHO data, one in five girls and one in thirteen boys worldwide experience childhood sexual violence. These figures amount to a catastrophe. My story only represents a fraction of this reality, but my experience can serve as a crucial starting point. From my story and the stories we've collected, it's clear that these experiences of sexual violence affect the people we meet, the people you meet, and the listeners of this podcast. We have a broad audience. I cannot limit myself to my own story, because it is about so much more. We are examining a system, a social structure—how sexual violence within families violates children's bodies, especially those of young girls, and how it shapes a child's entire life.


Q

Why must this be done now?

Since we are now able to talk about violence against adult women, it's time to bring visibility to the violence suffered by children. It's time to stop avoiding the issue. Most rapes and assaults occur within families, not in the external world as we often imagine. The younger the victim, the easier it is for the perpetrator to gain access. In 70% of cases where the child is under six years old, the abuse is classified as incest.


Today, discussions about domestic and sexual violence against adult women are actively circulating in our societies—thanks in large part to the #metoo movement. Yet, violence against children and the stories of their suffering remain deeply denied. As someone deeply concerned with this issue, I am deeply frustrated. I don't understand why there is a double standard. What are we afraid of that prevents us from addressing this?


Q

Might some survivors choose never to bring their abusers to justice, (within the logic of anti-incarceration frameworks) never to speak their truth in court?

From the information we've gathered, some survivors indeed say they see no need to go through the judicial process. They may choose other forms of redress, such as permanently cutting off contact with the abuser within family life and ensuring everyone around them understands the reason. Their silence is a warning. If they discover a child in the family is being abused, regardless of the nature of the abuse, they will take action. Everyone protects themselves as best they can. They know that the judicial process can be extremely difficult. Many today also believe that legal proceedings are far from a solution, because the cost we often have to pay is enormous. Relying solely on the justice system to address sexual violence is nowhere near enough. First and foremost, we need to transform our society, rather than waiting for tragedies to occur before attempting to repair the damage. The data we have comes only from survivors who are willing to speak out. There are many more who have not spoken, so we cannot access the true scale of the problem.


In fact, the number of survivors is far greater than we imagine. Moreover, in reality, if all survivors sought legal recourse for redress, our legal system would be unable to handle such an influx. In short, as a collective within this society, we must ask ourselves the "real question": How did we get to this point? If our society's justice system is unable to hear each and every one of us, how can the society we live in even be called a society? Anti-incarceration approaches, restorative justice, and incarceration all have their justifications, but we must not lose sight of the real issue. The real issue is: How do these things happen within our families and societies? What are the root causes behind these problems?

We must realize that all the data we currently have represents only the tip of the iceberg! Each of us is affected by sexual violence. This has happened or is happening to our friends, colleagues, and family members. Survivors of sexual violence are there—they are among us. They are the coworkers we go to parties with, our closest friends, our lovers, our neighbors. The prevalence of sexual violence survivors is shockingly widespread, and this widespread nature is terrifying. It demands deep reflection, and it concerns all of us. Some people tend to believe that only one in 10,000 girls experiences sexual violence, because when such cases make front-page headlines, they are treated as the hottest topics, leading people to see them as exceptional, rare events. This perception is infuriating.


In fact, what we want to convey is that sexual violence happens every day and is everywhere—it happens in rural areas, in schools, in the apartment upstairs. We don't need to wait for sensational cases to pay attention to sexual violence. Many people around us are experiencing it right now, and it is far from uncommon. These stories deserve at least as much attention as front-page headlines. Over the past three to four years, as these issues have unfolded, people have become more willing to engage with topics of violence, especially sexual violence. Just this year, four podcasts have begun discussing this topic: Charlotte Bienaimé's podcast she founded on Arte Radio, Charlotte Pudlowski's *Ou peut-être une nuit* ("Perhaps One Night"), my own *The Girl on the Sofa*, and Clémence Allezard's documentary podcast on France Culture, *Les Violées: Une histoire opprimée* ("The Raped: An Oppressed Story"). Without prior coordination or even knowing each other, we have all, in recent years, deeply recognized the necessity of discussing this issue. And without the events of the past four or five years in our societies—especially the #metoo movement against sexual assault in the United States—we would not have witnessed such widespread dissemination of these narratives.



Q

What obstacles have you encountered along the way?

Being the writer, subject, and audience of a podcast all at once is a challenge. Even though I am well-practiced in using the first person to discuss politics with a determination to amplify marginalized voices—just as I did in my first podcast, *Myself, My Sex, and I*—what I'm doing now is something different. For me, intimacy is a bridge for communication with others. On the issue of sexual violence, making visible the experiences of Black women means exchanging our connections and the countless ways we are linked. This time, I want to do so through the theme of childhood sexual violence. My aim is not to target the Black community and create stigmatization, nor to claim ownership over an issue that concerns all of us. I want to broaden the scope of this issue by including those who are often forgotten or rarely considered in mainstream narratives. For me, when we talk about these issues, we must talk about Black people and young African-descended survivors—and we must do so in the right way.
