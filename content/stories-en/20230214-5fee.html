"Not a single moment will go by without every other universe screaming for your attention."



The film "Everything Everywhere All at Once" (Everything Everywhere All at Once 2022) unfolds from the perspective of Evelyn (played by Michelle Yeoh), a Chinese-American mother. Evelyn and her husband Waymond eloped to the United States when they were young and had a daughter named Joy. Now in middle age, Evelyn faces heavy financial burdens and a daughter who constantly defies her—just as her family life hangs by a thread, her husband Waymond suddenly transforms into an agent from the Alpha Universe, asking Evelyn to use multiverse-jumping abilities to help him defeat the ultimate villain. But to Evelyn's shock, the ultimate villain turns out to be her own daughter Joy…


Image source: A24films


In the film, Evelyn and her husband eloped to the U.S. during the second wave of Chinese immigration in the 1990s. During that decade, the first wave of immigration had shown many Chinese people the "opportunity" and "American Dream," with the primary goal of migration being to earn money. However, the "American Dream" was not easy to achieve. In 1990s America, many immigrants remained at the bottom of society—like Evelyn and her husband. They run a laundromat business, the whole family crammed into a loft above the shop, and they face an investigation and allegations from the U.S. Internal Revenue Service.


Beyond economic hardship, some immigrant families also experience intergenerational and cultural conflicts. The tension between Evelyn and her daughter Joy, a second-generation immigrant, is one such example. If the film's overt plotline is Evelyn fighting evil forces across the multiverse to save the world, its underlying narrative is Evelyn's journey as a first-generation Chinese-American woman, whose personal identity and sense of self are shattered and rebuilt through collisions with the world around her. On this point, the two writer-directors focus most intently on the emotional entanglement between Evelyn and her daughter Joy—a mother-daughter relationship typical of East Asian families.



East Asian mother-daughter relationships: the love and pain between two women


Evelyn and Joy's emotional story is not complicated, yet it gives a unique substance to an otherwise familiar Marvel-style hero narrative framework. Judging from audience reactions after the film's release—especially comments from Asian American communities and Chinese viewers—the story between Evelyn and Joy is the part that resonates most emotionally. Meanwhile, critiques calling the film flashy, hollow, and ultimately reduced to visual spectacle clearly overlook its nuanced exploration of the love-hate emotional conflict between an East Asian mother and daughter living in America.



Within the first fifteen minutes of the film, the surface-level conflict between Evelyn and her daughter Joy is already evident—her mother does not accept Joy's identity as a lesbian, and she does not want Joy to come out in front of her grandfather. But as the plot unfolds, we discover this is not their only conflict: Evelyn, having gained the ability to jump across universes, realizes that the main villain she must confront is her own daughter. In the Alpha Universe, Evelyn's desperate desire for her daughter to succeed ultimately drives Joy to madness, transforming her into Jobu, who jumps across universes simply to destroy everything—an apparent distorted mirror of the current universe.


It must be said that the different versions of Evelyn across universes, with their expectations and high-pressure tactics for turning their daughters into "prodigies," naturally correspond to the widely discussed image of the East Asian "tiger mom" in recent media. The term "tiger mom" was coined by Amy Chua (Amy Lynn Chua 2011) in her book "Battle Hymn of the Tiger Mother." This concept is primarily popular among Chinese Americans and refers to a type of parent commonly found in East Asian, South Asian, and Southeast Asian countries who pursue perfection in raising their children, demanding strict discipline and making personal sacrifices in exchange for their children's high achievement. While such parenting styles may not be uncommon in East Asian families, where children bear the pressure of their parents' hopes to "raise dragons from sons and phoenixes from daughters," and follow the Confucian teaching of "filial piety," when this family atmosphere collides with American society's emphasis on individual respect and liberal values, the resulting controversies and conflicts become increasingly intense.


Image source: Amazon


East Asian mother Evelyn's expectations of her daughter are clearly visible. From the many clashes between mother and daughter, we see that the mother wants her daughter to be excellently aligned with mainstream social standards, to be conventionally attractive in appearance, and to have a heterosexual sexual orientation. The daughter, however, scorns or even resents these demands. In her mind, simply existing is not only acceptable but optimal—"As a mother, accepting and loving me as I am right now should be enough. Why must you attach so many conditions to your love? And why are all these conditions labeled as 'it's for your own good'?" In the film, when Joy is about to run away from home, Evelyn calls her back. When the audience expects the mother to finally break this silent resistance, Evelyn simply asks, "Why are you getting fatter?" This line provokes laughter from viewers, yet it also strikes a painful nerve for every East Asian daughter watching—when you long for unconditional love, you are instead met with subtle control and attacks. The mother's control and criticism are so small yet omnipresent that the daughter cannot resist, and over time, resentment grows.


Evelyn, as a traditional East Asian mother, also faces her own profound dilemma when confronting her American-born daughter: how should she deal with Joy's lesbian identity? When she knows full well that the family patriarch—her father—will never accept it, how can she find her own footing between the traditional East Asian patriarchal authority represented by her father and the liberal gender culture represented by her daughter? In the film, Evelyn finally gathers the courage to tell her father not to judge her daughter Joy by the same standards he used on her. Everything, in the end, returns to a starting point—this moment could be understood as one daughter's redemption of another.



Simone de Beauvoir (Simone de Beavoir 1964) articulated the mother-daughter relationship decades ago, arguing that in a male-dominated social structure, relationships between women can be understood as relationships between "others" outside the subject. In this sense, the mother-daughter relationship is also a relationship between two "others." American scholar Carol Gilligan (Carol Gilligan 1991) proposed the theory of the "crisis of connection" to explain the emotional entanglement in mother-daughter relationships during adolescence and its critical importance to female development. As girls enter adulthood, they face a "crisis of connection." When they truly experience their relationship with their mother, they also experience the patriarchal culture's devaluation of women—such as the idea that women cannot be seen as independent individuals, but must exist only through roles like "wife," "mother," or "daughter." Gilligan describes this immense cultural pressure as the "patriarchal wall." The mother-daughter relationship is not merely an interpersonal bond between one mother and one daughter, but is co-constructed by society, history, and culture. In "Everything Everywhere All at Once," this wall takes the form of Evelyn's father, "Gong Gong," who stands as a "patriarchal wall" between Evelyn and her daughter, and who also determines why Evelyn has become the mother she is today.



Bagels, the multiverse, and nihilism


The concept of success shaped by East Asian patriarchal systems leads both Evelyn and Joy—the East Asian daughters—into an existential crisis. In "Everything Everywhere All at Once," the two directors use the theme of the multiverse to draw viewers into a debate on existentialism versus nihilism, set within the intersecting contexts of East Asian identity, immigration, and patriarchy.


The film's multiverse premise is that every life experience and every decision a person makes spawns another life—a different universe. Like a line of dominoes collapsing irreversibly, Evelyn's primary universe branches into billions of others. For the first time, Evelyn—whose life falls completely short of any success standard—sees countless versions of herself, all the possibilities her life could have taken: a Hollywood martial arts star walking the red carpet in glittering jewels, a celebrated opera performer admired by her father, a star chef in a restaurant… She becomes entangled in the mess of a life deemed a failure. Suddenly, all the small frustrations in life become glaringly clear: she came to America for love with her husband, and the two young people once dreamed of building a great life in a foreign land. But now, her American Dream is consumed by endless tax paperwork, her husband is useless, and even her carefully raised daughter Joy is someone she cannot proudly present to her father. Her daughter Joy, stubborn and single-minded, just like Evelyn once was when she ran away with her lover, has achieved nothing in life except dating a girlfriend. She fits none of the definitions of success. If life could be lived again, if different choices had been made, would reality be better than this? A life of failure is always filled with regret and resentment.


Image source: Douban Movies


Like any rookie protagonist in a Hollywood film, after hitting rock bottom, Evelyn decides this time she will prove herself and finally change her "loser" life: mastering multiverse-jumping abilities, chasing down her daughter Jobu, and stopping her to save the entire multiverse. But as she pursues Jobu, she gradually comes to understand Jobu's motivation for destroying the world…


In fact, after Jobu freely traversed billions of universes, the more she understood the various universes and the versions of herself and her mother within them, the more she discovered that although the world is impermanent. Yes, after being driven mad by her mother, Jobu might have thought that other versions of herself in other universes could live happily. Perhaps she saw that all versions of herself across all universes were failures, all unhappy and joyless, which led her to say "All feels like shits." She wandered through the galaxy of life, looking at photos of herself as a child one by one, school test papers one by one, childhood toys, every moment with her mother... yet everything in life was heading towards a bad ending. So every decision in Jobu's life actually didn't matter, none of it was important, because everything ultimately leads to nothingness, all feels like shits. She placed everything in life on a bagel, an utterly ordinary bagel. Life is like a bagel, absurd to the extreme.

Nietzsche believed that the true "self" is "not something that exists there to be found or discovered, but something that must be created" (Li Yinhe, 2001). Becoming oneself is a process of seeking and creating oneself, not a pre-established state. Nietzsche also noted that although "our body is merely a social structure," shaped by historically contingent social norms, the meaning of one's existence lies precisely in defying all pre-set norms and truths, and forging one's own path: "No one can build a bridge for you across the river of life you must cross—only you can do it yourself" (Nietzsche, *The Educator Schopenhauer*). Unfortunately, under the immense tyranny of love driven by the expectation to "raise a daughter to become a dragon," Jobu collapses into the extreme of passive nihilism—the emptiness (*emptiness*) of Schopenhauer: life is nothing but suffering, and existence itself is meaningless (Zhou Li, 2003; Du Liyan, 2011). "A person without will or strength will never give things meaning, because he does not believe there is meaning in anything." Jobu embodies the "decline and downfall of spiritual power," a "pessimistic... nihilism" (Note: Nietzsche, *The Will to Power*, p. 295).



Therefore, Jobu's plan to hurl all the universes, including herself, into the black hole at the center of the bagel is not merely an act of world destruction, but also an act of self-destruction.


If Evelyn, upon first encountering the multiverse, still carried a faint hope for the diverse possibilities of life, then by now, having witnessed the multiverse through Jobu and Evelyn's eyes, she has resigned herself: *Nothing matters*. All their struggles, as failures, *nothing matters*—everything ultimately surges toward the bagel's black-hole-like center, turning into nothingness. No matter how much they struggled before, they seem trapped within the framework of the "failed East Asian daughter." The plot of a mother approaching and trying to save her fallen daughter is indeed clichéd, like many mainstream films, as if the daughter's problems must always be solved by the mother. Yet this film does not entirely fall into that trap. Here, perhaps because Evelyn herself was once an unsatisfactory East Asian daughter, she does not naturally "rescue" her daughter, but instead is persuaded and moved by her daughter's sense of nihilism, as if this very nihilism is the bond between East Asian women.

Besides serving as a vehicle for absurdist nihilism, the bagel is also interpreted as symbolizing the female cervix: the shape of the bagel resembles the cervix, its center and the universe's black hole evoke the boundless womb of the mother, and Jobu's hand gesture resembles the vagina. The core of the bagel's nihilism is tightly linked to the expectations and control of parents in the original family. From the moment a child drops from the mother's womb through the cervix, though separated from the maternal body, a permanent connection and entanglement with the mother begins.


Image source: Douban Movies


Evelyn is now in the role of mother, but she was once a daughter—and a failed one. After becoming a mother, she inevitably imposes the same discipline, disguised as expectations or "I'm doing this for your own good," onto Joy/Jobu. Thus, Evelyn and Joy in *Everything Everywhere All at Once* express the same kind of failed East Asian daughter—they are each other's past and future. For Evelyn/Joy, the moment the doctor said "It's a girl" at birth—or being born a lesbian—perhaps from that very beginning, everything was already wrong. This connection and entanglement is so pervasive that Evelyn and Jobu/Joy across the multiverse have never escaped it, and it pushes both into the abyss of nihilism—a self-destruction rooted in the wish that they had never existed, never been born: *If I had never been born, would everything have been better?*


Image source: Douban Movies


At this moment, nihilism triumphs over existence. The film's iconic scene, celebrated by countless viewers—the dialogue between two rocks—elevates this sense of nihilism to its peak. Whether intentional or not, the two rocks stand silently on a desolate mountain peak, evoking a certain Eastern aesthetic of "pure white snow cleansing the earth." All previous splendor is ultimately washed away by snow; two people, tormented to death by life, are merely silent rocks in the vast world; all the chaos of the universe returns to an utterly ordinary bagel, surging toward its black-hole-like center, toward the unknown end of the multiverse—*nothing matters*, absurd.

*Everything Everywhere All at Once*: Hope, Love, and Return


However, the two directors do not remain trapped in the dream of nihilism. In the film's final section, the overall narrative shifts toward a harmonious and heartwarming happy ending. This ending may be seen as clichéd, or it may be viewed as optimistic and inspiring.


This happy ending is primarily due to Evelyn's own awakening from nihilistic despair. Combined with the film's narrative focus on Evelyn's inner voice, *Everything Everywhere All at Once* can be called a feminist film. Nevertheless, the roles and influences of Evelyn's husband Waymond and daughter Joy in Evelyn's life journey are also key factors that guide her from narrow limitations toward perceptual awakening.


Evelyn's husband Waymond could be described as a perfect character. He is gentle, kind, optimistic, and inclusive, always able to see the good in people and things. Even as an Asian man at the margins of mainstream white society, he still loves life, remains content, upholds the values of "love and kindness" in his interactions, and deeply loves Evelyn and his family. In the face of Evelyn's years of criticism, blame, and indifference—even when she initiates divorce—he only seeks​a better way to coexist, never abandoning Evelyn.



In Hollywood films dominated by white masculinity, Asian male characters are generally considered by scholars to be emasculated and pathologized. On the other hand, within the context of East Asian patriarchy, male characters are typically portrayed as masculine and always the saviors of female characters (Yen Ling Shek, 2007). As a Hollywood production influenced by Hong Kong cinema of the 1980s–90s, *Everything Everywhere All at Once* presents Waymond as a martial arts figure embodying Eastern masculinity, yet also as a non-masculine martial artist rarely seen in Hong Kong films.​ The author tends to believe that although Evelyn is deeply influenced by Waymond, she is not saved by him. In any case, as a man, Waymond could never fully understand the existential crises experienced by Evelyn and Joy. He is more like a mediator between Evelyn and her family and the outside world, or a source of inspiration—an external factor catalyzing Evelyn's internal growth and transformation. As Evelyn gradually succumbs to the force of nihilism, Waymond's words suddenly awaken her: "Do you think I'm weak? I always see the good side of things—not because I'm naive, but because it's necessary and essential. That's my way of surviving." "Even though you've broken my heart over and over, I still want you to know that if there's a next life, I'd still choose to be with you—filing taxes, running a laundromat." By this point, CEO Waymond, fully aware of the experiences he and Evelyn have shared across different universes, still willingly abandons better possible lives to choose a mundane, ordinary life with Evelyn. It is precisely this pure and moving love that reawakens Evelyn, making her suddenly aware of the small beauties in everyday family life. Though these moments of beauty are tiny, when countless small instances of family happiness accumulate, they shimmer with the light of meaning in existence. Only then does she realize that the love she once ignored has always been there, and truly understands that the essence of life lies in recognizing: "You are worthy of love, and there is always something worth loving."

Although many film critics believe the movie should have remained at the peak of its nihilistic expression, and that the later happy ending and reconciliation fall into cliché, it is undeniable that this happy ending does not feel forced, but rather carries an awkward emotional effect. A major reason is that the film does not, in pursuit of a happy resolution, deny the emotional entanglement between East Asian mothers and daughters or the crisis of immigrant identity. On the contrary, while chasing her daughter Jobu/Joy, Evelyn continuously engages in self-reflection, attempting to understand her daughter's emotions. After experiencing the vortex of nihilism with her daughter, Evelyn—long estranged from her own daughterhood—re-experiences her daughter's existential crisis and realizes that much of the root problem lies within herself. She truly understands that love is important, but loving in the right way is even more so. The tyranny of love turns love into oppression, suffocating those closest to us. She also realizes her shared connection with Joy as fellow East Asian daughters—they should not be opposed to each other, but should join hands to resist the patriarchal discipline imposed on daughters in East Asian cultures.


In the film, Evelyn finally gathers the courage to tell her father not to view her daughter Joy through her own eyes, saying that everything will eventually return to the starting point—a moment that could be seen as one daughter's redemption of another. Her enormous embrace toward her daughter in the main universe, and her fearless pursuit of her "world-weary" daughter to the brink of life and death in the rock universe, represent her resistance against all structural constraints, embodying the idea of overcoming hardness with softness. This is the tenderness of the two directors and screenwriters (which has also drawn criticism for being "not revolutionary enough")—in universes where all matter, emotion, and social relations cease to exist, a mother's love for her daughter remains the reason the story continues and the universes keep turning. Admittedly, this resistance is highly idealized. How many mothers in real life can fully accept their daughters? How many daughters can, without holding past emotional oppression from their mothers against them, accept their mothers' repeated attempts to draw near? And how many fathers can unconditionally understand and support their wives and daughters like Waymond? Thus, Everything Everywhere All at Once expresses the hopes and aspirations of all East Asians, mothers, and daughters. Perhaps after watching the film, people may gain new understandings of how to live and how to relate to their families, and find greater courage to love.
