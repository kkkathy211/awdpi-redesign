Abstract

Aggression among girls is often hidden within intimate relationships in more indirect forms, and occurs more frequently during the sensitive and vulnerable stage of adolescence. This form of attack, produced by distorted social conditioning, poses a significant danger to both the bully and the victim. The unique indirectness and concealment of this "alternative aggression" also make school intervention and parental response more difficult to achieve. In this context, acknowledging the prevalence of violence among women and changing public perception of this "culture of aggression" will help create a more friendly and healthy environment for female development.




"Girls realize that aggressive behavior is encouraged as a way to gain power. They have never before faced such immense pressure to interact with others in ways that harm their own healthy development."

—— [US] Rachel Simmons


When we talk about "violence against women" (VAW), do we already assume by default that the perpetrator is male? A research report from Beijing Social Sciences [1] points out that according to the definition provided in the United Nations' Declaration on the Elimination of Violence against Women, any gender-based violence against women can be termed "gender-based violence"; in other words, violence by women against women should also fall within the scope of VAW. Yet in reality, research on violence among women is extremely scarce.


[1]  Li Pengcheng, "Violence Among Women: A Neglected Form of Gender-Based Violence," published in Beijing Social Sciences, June 2016.



Violence by women against women includes not only direct physical conflict but also indirect attacks such as rumors, isolation, silent treatment, and digital-era cyberbullying—among which the latter is more prevalent. Compared to men's way of "resolving issues with fists," bullying among girls is often indirect and concealed, and thus frequently overlooked. In a sense, this reinforces societal stereotypes about women. However, we must deeply recognize that this unique, epidemic-like form of aggression is rooted in society's unreasonable expectations and assumptions about women, and is cruelly destructive to both perpetrators and victims.


The World Health Organization, in its research on violence against women, divides a woman's life into five stages: before birth, infancy, girlhood, adolescence, adulthood, and old age. The aforementioned bullying behaviors are especially prevalent among girls during girlhood and adolescence, particularly within their intimate relationships and school cultures.



Rachel Simmons, after years of fieldwork in American elementary and secondary schools—giving lessons to girls, having in-depth conversations with them and their parents, and interviewing school administrators, teachers, and counselors—accumulated materials and experiences that culminated in her book Odd Girl Out: The Hidden Culture of Aggression in Girls, which explores the causes of hidden aggression among girls and the harm this distorted social culture inflicts on them.


The "Perfect Girl's" Intimate Relationships


Intimate relationships among women are inherently complex. Scholar Carol Gilligan points out that relationships and emotions hold primary importance in women's lives, making them more intensely and sensitively reactive to loss than men. The unique qualities of female intimacy also make more covert forms of bullying possible. Simmons also emphasizes that the level of intimacy among women is far higher than that among men. Female friendships can sometimes rival, or even surpass, familial bonds in closeness. Yet this very trait can also become a powerful point of attack: as the closest people, they know precisely how to hurt others accurately and fatally. This form of aggression is difficult to identify, and the victim's suffering intensifies with the original depth of intimacy.


In 1992, a study revealed the unique ways girls attack one another, offering society a mirror into non-traditional expressions of anger among girls. Subsequently, a psychology research team at the University of Minnesota classified girls' aggressive behaviors into three types based on this study: relational aggression, indirect aggression, and social aggression. Rachel Simmons collectively refers to these behaviors as "alternative aggression."



Women are "not allowed" to engage in open conflict. "In this culture," Simmons writes in her book, "culture disempowers girls by defining 'niceness': no aggression, no anger, no direct confrontation." As a result, girls are often forced to resort to indirect, non-physical means of attack. For example, in the chapter "Honesty Hurts," when asked "Do you tell someone when you're angry or upset with them?" the vast majority of girls express fear of confronting conflict directly, believing that "honesty hurts, so I'd rather lie."


The gap between intimacy and danger is razor-thin. Under societal stereotype expectations, the "perfect girl" should have "perfect relationships." When girls aspire to the halo of being a "perfect girl" or a "good girl," they are unknowingly deprived of the opportunity to face conflict and learn to resolve issues in intimate relationships with maturity. When girls decide to "punish" their friends without showing "hostility," they often hide behind sweet masks and inflict silent harm. On the other side, when bullied girls face daily cruel torment, fear of being alone often leads them to willingly maintain an abusive friendship.



Within this distorted culture of the "perfect girl," bullies may be completely unaware of their cruelty, while victims may accept unhealthy friendships with resignation. The unique closeness among girls should have been their initiation into understanding complex intimate relationships; but when the right to express anger is stigmatized and unexamined relationships themselves become the goal, girls cannot properly handle friendship issues through normal emotional exchange and self-expression—ultimately leading to tragedies within intimate relationships.


Distorted "Rites of Passage"


In Simmons' view, our society holds a cruel belief: bullying among girls is a "rite of passage" they must learn to become adults, a growth process every girl must go through. This belief causes immense suffering for the children involved, while parents and teachers remain oblivious. The "rite of passage" theory hides several disturbing assumptions: first, because many girls have engaged in "alternative aggression," it must be innate; second, girls need to learn to interact this way as preparation for adult female life; third, since meanness among girls is widespread and supposedly beneficial, it must be their natural attribute within the social structure, thus should be tolerated and mentally prepared for; and finally, abuse among girls isn't really abuse at all. This viewpoint and its underlying assumptions leave girls struggling precariously in their school environments, as if by merely depoliticizing the issue in discourse, we could relieve the despair and pain it causes them.



Growth does not necessarily mean being reborn from adversity or fighting back from despair; a distorted "rite of passage" may only teach girls to perceive and exploit human cruelty earlier. Girls who have suffered school bullying, even if they eventually leave the painful environment, still carry vivid memories of confusion, anxiety, humiliation, and the intense desire for revenge—memories that resurface from time to time. This pain never truly fades with time. It is precisely for this reason that in the second half of her book, Simmons devotes four chapters to discussing how parents, teachers, and adult school administrators can provide proper guidance and support to girls in their developmental stage, attempting to offer children a true rite of passage.


Conclusion


Simmons advocates empowering girls with the ability to negotiate and resolve conflicts. Since conflict arises from fear of losing relationships, girls should be taught that making friends is a choice, not a mandatory command. They all have the right to choose relationships that make them feel comfortable. And if friendship is a choice, then both intimacy and conflict are normal phenomena. Simmons believes that if girls no longer live in fear of losing relationships and believe that true friendship can withstand the test of conflict, they will no longer engage in hidden aggressive behaviors like "stabbing each other in the back."


"I hope," Simmons writes at the end of her book, "that they—and every woman who has ever been the lonely girl—can regain their strength and boldly speak their minds, whispering to themselves: 'What I regret most is staying silent. What was there to be afraid of?'"



Returning to gender-based violence against women, it is not difficult to see that, beyond friendships among women, in intimate relationships where domestic violence occurs, women also have the right to choose relationships that make them feel comfortable. Both friendships and intimate relationships require effort, but this effort should never mean endless compromise. Women need to cut their losses in time when facing harmful relationships and bravely step away from the manipulation of violent relationships.
