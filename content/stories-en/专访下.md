On a winter afternoon, London's sky had quietly darkened, and the scattered pedestrians made the intricate streets appear somewhat desolate. Yet, inside the office of Hestia Life Beyond Crisis—the city's largest domestic violence shelter and referral center—the phone continued to ring intermittently.

  

Zhong Ruiying has long grown accustomed to this rhythm—answering calls, recording information, assessing risks, and contacting shelter resources—each step demanding precision and speed. The women on the other end of the line, seeking help, are often on the verge of collapse, speaking in urgent tones about the recent trauma of violence they've endured.

  

Every call tests the operator's communication skills, patience, and familiarity with procedures. For Zhong Ruiying, this job is about more than just answering phones—it is about sustaining a social network for survivors of sexual violence.

  

This issue's "Her Perspective" column will continue to tell <strong>Zhong Ruiying's </strong>story. In our previous interview, she told AWDPI that the feminism she practices is an ever-expanding, endless journey. <strong>When a feminist steps from the study into real life, to what extent, and in what ways, can her "feminist" declaration be put into practice?</strong>

  

Zhong Ruiying

* Master's student in Interdisciplinary Gender Studies at the University of Cambridge
    
* Research focuses on gender-based violence, cultural heritage, and collective trauma
    

  

  

(The following is Zhong Ruiying's first-person account, with slight edits)

  



<strong>Domestic violence survivors trapped within the system</strong>

  

The institutional development of domestic violence governance in the UK can be traced back to the women's movements of the 1970s and 1980s. From the 1990s onward, the government gradually established cross-departmental cooperation mechanisms—such as joint efforts by the Home Office, police, and social work systems—and pushed for domestic violence to be recognized as a criminal offense, exemplified by the 2004 Domestic Violence Act.

  

In organizations like Hestia Life Beyond Crisis, this multi-agency collaboration model is still visible. It is not entirely government-run; many services are delivered jointly by communities and social organizations. However, in actual practice, I have also noticed some problems with this model.

  

<strong>The most obvious issue is relatively low referral efficiency. </strong>Because multiple agencies are involved, survivors often have to be referred repeatedly between different departments, making the entire process extremely lengthy. In the cases I've helped with, few women have been able to enter a shelter smoothly on the same day.

  



  

Additionally, different regions and institutions have inconsistent understandings and implementation standards regarding domestic violence, resulting in significant disparities in the quality of support survivors receive. Some survivors' special needs are also difficult to meet—such as those with mobility impairments or mental health issues. If a shelter believes it cannot provide appropriate support, it usually has no choice but to refuse admission. <strong>Shelter resources that can accommodate people facing multiple overlapping challenges are inherently very limited.</strong>

  

The situation for LGBTQ+ individuals is similarly complex. Many shelters in the UK are single-gender, which often excludes transgender people. Yet transgender individuals are more likely to face multiple overlapping challenges, making it even harder for them to access domestic violence support under existing institutional and social norms.

  

Although the system emphasizes multi-agency cooperation, information sharing between departments is actually insufficient, causing the entire support system to resemble fragmented pieces. Many survivors complain to me <strong>that they have repeatedly recounted their experiences to different agencies, yet each time they are forced to start from the beginning.</strong>

  



<strong>"This is not domestic violence—it's attempted murder"</strong>



  

In contrast, the situation in China presents different characteristics. Domestic violence only entered public discourse in the 1990s, with transnational NGO collaboration and international funding driving related research and legislative exploration.

  

However, individuals advocating on domestic violence issues in China often need to operate through state-affiliated channels, <strong>which has led the domestic violence support system to exhibit strong administrative and legal orientations.</strong>

  

Currently, domestic violence in China is primarily addressed through legal and case-processing channels. It is often treated as a judicial matter handled by public security and courts, <strong>rather than being fully recognized as a public issue requiring a coordinated societal response. </strong><strong>The space for social organizations to operate is relatively limited.</strong>

  

This tendency easily reduces domestic violence to <strong>"a family dispute." </strong>When extreme cases occur, many members of the public emphasize <strong>"This is not domestic violence, but attempted murder." </strong>This statement actually reflects how, in mainstream understanding, "domestic violence" itself has not yet been taken seriously as a form of social violence. The result is that only when a case escalates to extreme violence and fully enters the criminal justice system does it receive sufficient attention, while domestic violence as a long-standing social issue lacks systematic discussion and response.

  

Viewing domestic violence as a social issue involves not only whether laws are comprehensive, but also whether social services, public education, medical systems, social work systems, community organizations, and the judiciary can form coordinated linkages. From prevention to intervention and then to recovery, a complete support network is needed.

  



  

But in China, once social-level support mechanisms begin to emerge, they are often quickly absorbed into the administrative system and subsequently marginalized. This leads the public discourse to unconsciously avoid the concept of "domestic violence" itself, focusing only on individual criminal cases. Over time, <strong>the deep-rooted causes behind domestic violence—such as long-term controlling relationships, gender inequality, and lack of social support—are continuously downplayed, leaving only isolated criminal cases.</strong>

  



<strong>Only when the system undergoes fundamental change</strong>

<strong>can survivors truly receive help</strong>



  

One day, I received a call from a woman seeking help. I carefully documented her situation and discussed it with the team, but ultimately determined she did not meet the shelter's admission criteria. Following protocol, I had to call her back to deliver the result and provide contact information for other organizations.

  

But at that moment, she suddenly broke down. Crying, she said she had just been referred from the very number I had given her, and had been passed back and forth between different agencies, exhausted and drained, so she unleashed all her anger on me.

  

That call deeply affected me. Although I was just an operator without decision-making power, I still felt extremely frustrated. Afterwards, my manager comforted me and reminded me to <strong>learn to distinguish between the caller's emotions and my own, and not to become completely absorbed in them.</strong>

  



  

At that moment, I first truly realized that even the UK's relatively well-developed support system for domestic violence survivors still has irreparable gaps. And although I am only a small part of the system, I might unknowingly become a link that disappoints women on the margins.

  

This incident happened during my sophomore year and became a turning point in my thinking. I gradually realized <strong>that if the system itself does not change, the power an individual can exert is actually very limited. </strong>At the same time, I began to understand that social work cannot be sustained by sympathy alone. Over-involvement in emotions not only depletes oneself but may also leave survivors feeling even more hopeless. True professional competence lies in finding a balance between empathy and boundaries.

  

Later, through observation and practice in different countries, I gradually discovered that whether in the UK or China, although domestic violence presents different appearances, they share similar dilemmas—it is often not truly understood as a structural issue, sometimes reduced to a procedural problem, sometimes seen as a private family matter, and more often only taken seriously when it becomes a criminal case, while deeper causes such as gender systems, economic structures, and lack of social support are overlooked.

  

It is precisely for this reason that I gradually shifted my focus toward knowledge production. In my view, change often begins with redefining the problem itself. Research can not only reveal the structural contradictions behind gender-based violence but also break the narrative that reduces violence to isolated incidents, helping people re-understand these issues from social and historical perspectives. At the same time, cross-cultural academic dialogue can allow experiences from different countries to illuminate one another, rather than remaining trapped in isolated paths.

  



<strong>Change happens through small but persistent perseverance</strong>



  

In the process of delving deeper into these issues, it is easy to see the world's injustices and even feel powerless. But I always believe <strong>that choosing to keep moving forward, after clearly seeing the problems, is itself a powerful act. Many changes often slowly emerge precisely through such seemingly small but continuous perseverance.</strong>

  

For young students currently studying feminism, I have a few keywords I'd like to share:

  

First is <strong>empathy. </strong>In the process of learning and engaging with these issues, we constantly encounter people in vastly different circumstances. Their experiences, emotions, and even choices may be far removed from our own. But it is precisely this difference that reminds us to truly listen, rather than rush to judge.

  

Second, maintain <strong>flexibility and openness</strong>. Gender studies is inherently a field that is constantly discussed, revised, and even dismantled and rebuilt; rarely does any single theory explain all issues. I've always believed that, rather than rushing to find definitive answers, it's more important to allow oneself to move between different perspectives and to keep thinking amid uncertainty. Many new insights often emerge precisely in such an open state.

  

Third, is reflection on <strong>one's own position</strong>. None of us speaks, conducts research, or engages in activism from a completely neutral standpoint. Our upbringing, social identities, and contexts shape how we understand the world. I constantly remind myself to ask: whose experiences am I amplifying through my perspective? And whose voices might I be overlooking? This ongoing self-reflection is, in fact, a crucial step toward understanding others and social structures.
