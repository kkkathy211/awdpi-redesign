#This Issue's Interview


Molly Murphy MacGregor

Executive Director and Co-Founder of NWHA, she has over 40 years of experience working in the fields of gender equality and women's history, and has participated in strategic planning with the U.S. government and related agencies to promote recognition of women's historical contributions. Her work in multicultural women's history has been widely recognized, earning awards from organizations such as the American Educational Research Association, the U.S. Department of Education, and the National Association for Multicultural Education.

#About NWHA

NWHA (National Women's History Alliance) was founded in 1980 in Santa Rosa, California, by Molly Murphy MacGregor, Mary Ruthsdotter, Maria Cuevas, Paula Hammett, and Bette Morgan, with the goal of promoting women's historical achievements.


NWHP created a working coalition and successfully lobbied the U.S. Congress to designate March as National Women's History Month. To this day, celebrations take place across the United States every March. To better support research on women's history, NWHA collaborates with women's organizations across the country to ensure that the remarkable contributions of women to American history are remembered and celebrated.



Everyone knows that March 8 is International Women's Day, and many also know that this holiday originated from early 20th-century women's labor movements in the United States. But in the U.S., beyond celebrating this women's holiday on March 8 each year, the entire month of March is also designated as "Women's History Month." During these 31 days, the government and civil society emphasize in various ways the significant historical contributions women have made in political, economic, cultural, scientific, and technological fields, and use this occasion to advance work on women's rights protection.


The establishment of "Women's History Month" is closely tied to an organization called the "National Women's History Project" (NWHP). In 1980, the "National Women's History Project" (NWHP) was established in California and successfully lobbied Congress in 1987 to designate March as "Women's History Week." In 1980, the "National Women's History Project" (NWHP) was renamed the "National Women's History Alliance," and for the past 43 years has continued its mission "to recognize and celebrate the diverse and significant historical achievements of women by providing information, educational materials, and programs."


Over the past 43 years, the women's rights movement has experienced several waves of thought, taking on new forms under changing social landscapes; old problems may still lack concrete solutions, while new issues continue to emerge. During Women's History Month 2023, Avoice conducted an in-depth interview with Molly MacGregor, co-founder of the U.S. National Women's History Alliance. Over several hours, Molly, who has served as the Alliance's Executive Director for 43 years, spoke eloquently—not only recalling revolutionary events from the last century, but also offering unique insights into the challenges facing women of all races in the United States and around the world today.


(Note: The following Q&A is from Avoice's interview with Molly MacGregor, with responses edited and condensed for clarity)



I. From "Women's History Week" to "Women's History Month"

Avoice: How did you become a co-founder of the National Women's History Project and the National Women's History Alliance? Under what circumstances was this nonprofit organization established?


Molly MacGregor: I was a high school history teacher, and one day a student asked me, "What is the women's movement?" That year was a landmark year for women's history (Editor's note: 1972)—Shirley Chisholm ran for President of the United States, and Barbara Jordan became the first Black woman elected to the Texas state legislature. It truly was an extraordinary year. I was only 23 at the time, and despite having been a history major with excellent academic performance, I couldn't answer the student's question. So I divided my class into groups to research different topics, including the women's movement.



Later, as a member of the education task force of the local Sonoma County Commission on the Status of Women, I went to our county schools. I asked them to make the week containing March 8—International Women's Day—a focus. I believed that if they could place Women's History Week on the school calendar, it would be a great opportunity to explore topics related to the women's movement and women's history.. For this, we invited women experts from the community to come to the schools and share their life stories. In 1978, our first "Women's History Week" was born in Sonoma County. It was a huge success. The following year, we held a march, bringing the success we had achieved in Sonoma County to a national conference.


In February 1979, I received a phone call from the White House. The President's assistant told me that President Carter would issue a presidential message for "Women's History Week" in 1980, and Congress quickly approved it. For the next six years, we had "National Women's History Week," the week containing March 8. In 1987, Congress finally declared March as "National Women's History Month." The National Women's History Project and all its allies and advocates thus joined this journey together.

Avoice: We noticed you mentioned many things about government, schools, and organizations. The shift from "National Women's History Week" to "National Women's History Month" was actually a huge leap. We wonder which organizations played an important role in the development of the National Women's History Alliance, and how they provided support?


Molly MacGregor: Starting in 1980 with President Carter, government involvement was a tremendous driving force, because when the government announced the congressional resolution, every government agency had to celebrate Women's History Week. Our congressional representatives and senators might not have known women's history, but their staff considered it important enough.


Moreover, we were sometimes surprised by the military's response—we would occasionally receive copies of letters from commanders. We had an amazing coalition: we had one of the most progressive women in the House, who introduced the congressional resolution; and we also had one of the most conservative members in the U.S. Senate, and our project was the only thing they jointly sponsored. But for us, this was perfect, because we wanted people to know we were not talking about any single ideology. We are not about liberals or conservatives, but about everyone. This is everyone's history—your mother's history, your ancestors' history. We wanted to create that connection..


Later, we also received support internationally from Canada and Australia. Canada established a similar Women's History Week, taking us as a model.



II. Why Is "Women's History" Important?

Avoice: Why make "Women's History" the theme for an entire month?


Molly MacGregor: Most women's organizations didn't know any women's history either. Women historians had been working hard to publish biographies of women and produce extraordinary new versions of historical narratives, but often their mentors were men, which was frustrating because the traditional view was that studying women's lives wouldn't lead to academic success. But when the academic community came together and truly began pushing research on women's history, it inspired all women's organizations.


Once National Women's History Month was established, especially with a specific annual theme, these organizations would come together, often alongside government agencies, to celebrate Women's History Month. Themes are truly important—for example, in 2023, we celebrated women who tell stories, especially stories about "us," opening new pathways for journalists and playwrights.

 

After the successful implementation of themed activities, many male historians became our greatest supporters and colleagues, saying they wanted to write women back into history and tell the complexity of the American story. Ideological differences coexisting with gender inclusivity is interesting and novel. So it's not that men want to suppress women's history—it's just that they don't know enough about it yet.

Avoice: Collections of biographies of outstanding women have been well received. How does your team select the women to feature?


Molly MacGregor: Initially, we gathered any books related to women from various publishers, did a preliminary review, and then selected the best ones to include in our resource directory. As interest in women's history grew, the number of published books increased exponentially, so our directory is now 32 pages long. I don't have exact statistics from the Library of Congress, but if you counted the holdings on women's history or related fields from 1980 to today, I could say it's at least 50 times what it was decades ago.



We must ensure that the books we select discuss women from every cultural group—from Asian women to Black women, to Indigenous women, to Hispanic women, to white women—representing information from the entire spectrum. We strive to ensure the authenticity of the content and include as many citations as possible, because it represents multicultural perspectives. Interestingly, when we first started working, when we first began writing women's history and doing research, when we read those biographies, you'd think these women had no mothers, because all the biographies only mentioned the women's fathers. But today's approach to teaching history is very different—it emphasizes including women, celebrating their importance as mothers, caregivers, and role models. Such a dramatic change is almost unbelievable for many young women.



III. "The Patriarchy of 2000" and "Woke Culture"

Avoice: Do you think the current social environment or the advocacy environment for women's rights is important, or do you believe they still need further development?

 

Molly McGregor: We always need more development. Remember, we are up against at least 2,000 years of patriarchy, so we will always need more progress. But I think in certain areas—such as women's history, people's history, and the history of poetic and cultural expression—there has been a stimulation of dialogue. We are increasingly realizing that opposing sides can move forward together, and to move forward together, we must listen to each other. We are facing an entire "woke culture," and we take pride in our "wokeness," because it means I have become aware of things I didn't know before.

 

So in the context of public opinion, when people say something with sexist implications, instead of confronting them, we should create space for them to ask different questions and then learn to see issues in a new way. Learning women's history is one of the opportunities to expand our worldview and values. For example, women who run for prime minister or president, even if they don't succeed and face significant controversy due to their gender, create massive shocks across the international political arena when they do run. From the fight for suffrage to women's marches, we want this country to recognize who women are: here are full citizens with formal rights and voices demanding civil rights..

 

My brother and I are only two years apart, and it took him two years to fully understand what I was doing. After that, we became partners. Because he realized that, as a man, he had been accustomed to a fixed lifestyle—being the breadwinner—while women, without choice, were expected to take on the responsibility of caring for the family. Women's history shows that men and women can actually transcend gender bias and become partners, working together to contribute to social equality, and this also encourages other men to understand the importance of teamwork..

Avoice: Many people criticize that this movement has gone too far, becoming too intense, to the point of "canceling" individuals without proper investigation or due process. Do you think this is excessive, or is it a necessary part of the developmental process?

 

Molly McGregor: I'll start from the beginning of what's called the second wave of feminism, which is my generation. People always have factions. At that time, some people felt embarrassed by women's actions, thinking these women were too radical, while radical women thought others were too conservative. It comes back again to the issue of listening and trying to collaborate. I don't think it's excessive to demand that people be recognized as full citizens.

 


The United States still does not have a constitutional amendment guaranteeing women equal opportunity. It's 2023, and we still don't have an Equal Rights Amendment. August 26 is the anniversary of American women winning the right to vote, and all our women's organizations are petitioning the government to make August 26 a national holiday. People always say we already have enough federal holidays, but compared to most countries, we actually have fewer, and lack meaningful ways of celebration. Mother's Day only recognizes women who are mothers, ignoring other groups of caregivers. So what we need is a national holiday on Women's Equality Day to celebrate and acknowledge that women across the nation are full citizens. One way to achieve this is by building coalitions, talking to as many people as possible, and collaborating with as many women's organizations as possible. Of course, the prerequisite is doing this with good intentions. There's no need to be so harsh or arrogant—we need civility and kindness.

 

We once had only one computer, because we could only afford one, so we worked 24-hour shifts, changing shifts in the middle of the night to keep the computer running. This was before email even existed. So I'm counting on you, all of you, to pick up this torch and keep fighting for your generation. You have the skills, access to information, and connectivity that we didn't have when we started the National Women's History Project..



Four: Trapped Minority Women in the United States and

"China Is Not Our Enemy"

Avoice: What are the biggest difficulties or challenges faced by minority women in the United States when they experience domestic violence?

 

Molly McGregor: The United States is a racist country, filled with racism and racial bias. This white supremacy affects the way people treat any woman, especially those who are abused. Domestic violence is everywhere. This is not only a problem for minority women, immigrant women, or refugee women—it's a problem for all women. One of our board members manages a team focused on domestic work, and due to the overwhelming number of women seeking help, they've had to increase their work budget by 400%.

 

Many minority women, or immigrant women, lack legal documentation, which means they lack a foundation of rights—that's often their primary challenge. The U.S. immigration system is extremely harsh for immigrants. They go through unimaginable processes just to gain the chance to live in the U.S. and obtain legal status, so they are deeply afraid of the police. When they face difficulties or danger, they don't dare to report it or speak the truth, fearing it will bring even greater trouble upon themselves.

 

Then there's the language barrier. Take my home state of California, for example—the state with the highest proportion of minorities. In just one school district, 37 different languages are spoken. This means that language barriers alone make it extremely difficult for local minority women to access help through the same channels available to white women. Moreover, when a minority woman suffering from domestic violence needs help, police may not necessarily come to her aid due to her background and origin.

 

In fact, we can see that these women are suffering from invisible imprisonment. They are trapped in challenges and constraints that are hard to escape. It's not only immigrant or refugee women—many women across the United States are enduring such imprisonment. Our organization is based in California, a state that has often led the nation in driving social change. Despite this, our organization has still faced significant criticism, with many people believing we've done too much in protecting women's survival and rights. But in fact, it wasn't until the 1980s that the U.S. established its first shelters specifically for women suffering from domestic violence. Before that, people denied the existence of domestic violence, pretending these things weren't happening. It has only been 50 years since people began to confront the issue and establish women's shelters. We still have a lot of work to do, a lot to fight for. What inspires us is that more and more grassroots organizations are joining the work of protecting minority women's rights.

 

Additionally, We are delighted to see that the newly elected mayor of Oakland, Jean Quan, is a Chinese American woman. She is a role model whose life story has inspired many minority women. We hope more Asian women can see her position and achievements, so they realize they can be stronger..

 

Currently, the U.S. government is attempting to reform the immigration management system to help cross-cultural groups understand and integrate into local communities. For foreign women who long for legal status, without legal and community protection, their lives will be controlled by their husbands—a very difficult problem. We should strive to learn different languages and communicate with minority women in their native languages, helping them feel safe, and then transfer them into a sound women's protection system to help them escape the pain caused by domestic violence.

Avoice: What social barriers do you think American women's groups, especially immigrant women, will be able to overcome in the near future?

 

Molly McGregor: The first thing American women are striving for is earning a decent wage. Some women are already leading and organizing farmworker movements to fight for higher pay. Though this process is extremely difficult. Again, take California: the cost of living is very high, yet many women are forced to survive on minimum wage, especially women of color, who often have to work three jobs just to barely make ends meet and buy food and necessities for their families.

 

The U.S. government has taken note of this and launched nationwide investigations. In recent years, the government has been promoting democratic management and high-quality job standards, and there is hope that a resolution to raise the minimum wage will be passed. To support the passage of this resolution, we are traveling around lobbying, encouraging more citizens—especially women—to participate in public discussions and decision-making, helping women fight for better living conditions..


Avoice: There is another event we would like to hear your opinion on. Under the current circumstances, feminist organizations in the United States are calling for setting aside geopolitical biases to jointly address global challenges. For example, during a recent event hosted by the conservative think tank Heritage Foundation, a member of the U.S. grassroots women's organization PINK CODE shouted at the scene: "China is not our enemy—climate crisis is!" What are your thoughts on this incident?

 

Molly McGregor: I fully support this view, whether it's China or any other country, we must remember that the people of these countries are not our enemies—they are our friends, potential allies, and human beings just like us. I absolutely support this idea, as it is a fundamental basis for many things—for instance, in addressing climate change, many countries are taking action, with women actively participating and uniting collective strength to find better solutions.

 

For women's organizations around the world, our goal should not be to create something entirely new, but to enable people to integrate and access existing resources. Our goal is to connect all these different women's organizations, helping them get to know each other and support one another. That's why we renamed ourselves the Women's History Alliance: we aim not only to serve as an information hub for women's history, but also as an information hub for women's historical groups..
