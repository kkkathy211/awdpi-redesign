Introduction



The Women Against Violence Europe (WAVE) network, as a pan-European network of women's specialist services, expresses its solidarity with our members in Romania and with all women and girls in Romania, in the face of deeply concerning developments that seriously threaten their fundamental rights and safety. Following our 27th conference held in Romania on 22–23 September 2025, we wish to voice our concerns about institutional changes that risk undermining decades of progress.


The Romanian government plans to merge the National Agency for Equal Opportunities between Women and Men (ANES) with the National Agency against Trafficking in Persons (ANITP), a move that requires careful scrutiny regarding its potential impact on women's rights and safety. This institutional restructuring raises critical questions: How will respective responsibilities be maintained? How will mechanisms dedicated specifically to gender equality and violence prevention be safeguarded?


Learning from Georgia: Part of a broader European trend



Romania's current situation reflects a wider trend across Europe—the systematic "dismantling" of women's rights under various pretexts, including claims of efficiency, anti-migration narratives, and rhetoric promoting traditional family values. These movements have led to weakened reproductive rights, weakened protections against domestic violence, defunding of gender equality mechanisms, and the silencing of women and feminist voices. The case of Georgia shows that seemingly administrative reforms can trigger a chain reaction that fundamentally undermines the institutional foundations of women's rights.


In 2024, Georgia's ruling party, the Georgian Dream Party, passed the Law on Transparency of Foreign Influence, requiring media and non-governmental organizations receiving over 20% of their funding from abroad to register as "entities pursuing the interests of foreign powers." Subsequently, gender equality mechanisms were systematically dismantled: in April 2024, the Georgian parliament overrode the president's veto to abolish gender quotas on electoral lists; in February 2025, legislation was adopted replacing the terms "gender" and "gender identity" in all Georgian laws, and renaming the Law on Gender Equality as the Law on Equality, with the ruling party claiming "gender" is a product of foreign influence. By March 2025, the parliamentary Committee on Gender Equality, as well as gender equality committees at the autonomous republic and municipal levels, were all abolished.


These measures are part of a systematic campaign targeting civil society, women's rights organizations, and democratic institutions. This sequence of events shows how administrative reforms can rapidly evolve into systemic dismantling. So-called "institutional reforms" ultimately result in the complete erasure of gender equality frameworks.


The risk in Romania




While Romania's situation differs from Georgia's, the pattern is similar: weakening specialized gender equality mechanisms through institutional changes such as mergers, abolitions, or downgrades—changes that rarely occur in isolation. Although the government claims the reforms aim to increase efficiency, concern lies not only in the sequence of measures but also in their cumulative impact on the institutional framework for women's rights and those who uphold it.


This restructuring has lacked genuine public consultation or civil society participation, raising serious questions about how Romania will continue to fulfill its obligations to women and girls. Although the government has held some meetings with civil society and academia, WAVE's members in Romania report that the process has been merely formal, with their input excluded from the proposed legislation. Such a significant measure should not be decided behind closed doors, but must fully incorporate the expertise and perspectives of women's NGOs and academic experts actively working in the field.


WAVE is particularly concerned that this merger coincides with two other legislative initiatives: one proposing to reduce the legal abortion limit from 14 weeks to 8 weeks and to define an 8-week fetus as a "person"; and another seeking to narrow the legal definition of what constitutes domestic violence. The combined effect of these measures could severely impact women's autonomy, safety, and equality.


Legal obligations and international consequences



Romania has binding obligations under the Istanbul Convention, the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW), EU Directive 2024/1385 (requiring transposition into national law by June 2027), and the EU Victims' Rights Directive. ANES and ANITP serve critical functions under these frameworks as national rapporteurs and focal points. The proposed merger could significantly affect Romania's compliance, including:


* triggering infringement procedures from the EU for violating gender equality and violence prevention directives;

* hindering Romania's accession to the OECD, which requires adherence to the Recommendation on Gender Equality in Public Life and other gender standards;

* potential loss of EU funding (as ANES's gender equality compliance is a precondition for fund disbursement);

* weakened capacity to cooperate with international partners such as Europol and Interpol, as the Ministry of Labour may fail to meet required security protocols.


WAVE fears that, at a time when violence against women in Romania remains extremely severe, prioritizing short-term cost savings may inadvertently endanger women's safety. The data is alarming: as of August 2025, 32 femicides have been recorded in Romania—one woman killed per week on average. According to the European Institute for Gender Equality (EIGE), in 2022 alone, police recorded 67 intimate partner femicides and 34,776 reports of intimate partner violence victims. Romania ranks fourth in the EU for intimate partner violence, with 45.5% of women reporting psychological abuse and 25.4% reporting physical violence by a partner. Dismantling specialized agencies will shift costs onto an already overburdened healthcare and justice system, women's specialist services, and ultimately onto victims—women and children. The institutional framework necessary to ensure Romania fulfills its legal obligations to women must be prioritized.


The necessity of specialized responses



WAVE has long advocated that state institutions address violence against women and domestic violence through gender-sensitive approaches and provide adequate funding for women's specialist services delivered by civil society organizations, ensuring women and girls can access critical support.


Institutional reforms that eliminate specialized gender bodies may trigger a domino effect, leading to diluted responsibilities and generalized support. General victim services are important, but they cannot replace the specialized expertise and feminist frameworks provided by women's specialist services, including trauma-informed care addressing coercive control, economic abuse, sexual violence, and the intergenerational impacts of patriarchal violence. Likewise, anti-trafficking efforts require specialized mechanisms with security protocols and cross-border cooperation. The proposed merger risks creating a one-size-fits-all model that fails to meet the specific needs of survivors of gender-based violence.


It has been proven that weakening gender equality institutions and women's services has fatal consequences. Restrictions on reproductive health services threaten women's health and autonomy; weakened domestic violence protections allow abuse to continue. These issues are matters of life and death. Romania has a moral and legal obligation to ensure comprehensive support services, robust institutional mechanisms, accessible justice, and a society where women live free from violence. Funding women's specialist services is not a luxury—it is a legal requirement.


Our call

Women's rights are not optional, expendable, or negotiable. We urge the Romanian government to prioritize women's safety in all decision-making and to genuinely fulfill its international legal obligations.


WAVE stands with our members and women's organizations in Romania, who are raising critical concerns about these developments. We call on the Romanian government to truly listen to and incorporate the expertise, experience, and concerns of Romanian women's rights organizations, feminist civil society, and specialist service providers—these organizations are not mere "stakeholders," but indispensable partners whose knowledge and voices must play a substantive role in policy reforms affecting women's safety and rights.


We urge the Romanian government to ensure institutional stability and clarity during any transition. The state's core responsibilities—coordinating gender equality, preventing violence against women and girls, and preventing femicide—require strong, well-resourced, and clearly mandated institutions. Institutional uncertainty and the weakening of specialized mechanisms not only undermine Romania's commitments but directly endanger the safety and rights of women and girls.


WAVE will continue to support our members in Romania and amplify their voices as they defend women's rights in their country. Their agency, expertise, and leadership must be recognized and respected by decision-makers. We stand ready to work with them, European institutions, and the international community to ensure Romania fulfills its obligations to women and girls.


Feminist solidarity is our strength. Collective action is our strategy. Women's liberation is our goal.


— Women Against Violence Europe (WAVE)
