In this interview, we are honored to have invited Professor Li Saihong. When she was young, Li traveled to the West as a visiting scholar, earned her PhD at the University of Copenhagen in Denmark, and then continued her studies in the UK, where she has spent many years deeply engaged in the fields of translation and linguistics, achieving remarkable accomplishments. Currently retired from her role as Director of the Centre for Translation and Intercultural Studies at the University of Stirling in the UK, her memories stretch like railway tracks—long and continuous. Reflecting on over two decades of life overseas, she believes the most crucial word that has run through her journey is "independence." "From the very first day I arrived, I knew the most important thing was to be independent—economically independent—and to have something I truly loved, whether it was academic work or something else."


Living in a foreign country, women who wish to be safe and free to be themselves must endure pressures from all directions, including violence hidden under the guise of love. Beyond her academic and professional work, Professor Li has paid special attention to the living conditions of overseas Chinese women, particularly those suffering from domestic violence, and has actively engaged in related public welfare initiatives. In this interview, Professor Li shared her observations and reflections on the issue of domestic violence among overseas Chinese women, combining personal experience with a broad socio-historical-cultural perspective. She proposed possible solutions and offered advice on how Chinese women can navigate cultural differences and achieve both spiritual and material independence.



Brief Overview: Domestic Violence in the UK



The UK is an immigrant country. After the Opium Wars and World War II, large numbers of Chinese people—mostly from southern China—migrated to the UK, making a living by opening restaurants and other means. That era feels distant now, and information about domestic violence among overseas Chinese communities from that time is scarce. Professor Li shared a case she encountered: "The man had a tendency toward domestic violence, while the woman held a traditional belief—'If he hits me, it's over once it's done. He was just drunk, so I'll let it go.'" When people around her urged her not to forgive so easily, saying, "You can't keep letting this happen—once, then again, it will only get worse," she still hesitated. In fact, similar situations continue today, both overseas and in China. Bound by the notion that "family shame should not be made public," many female victims still find it extremely difficult to seek or accept outside help. Professor Li emphasized: "In such cases, we need to do more public awareness work and offer more support, so they don't feel they have to endure it alone." Unfortunately, at that time, there were few organizations or groups within the Chinese community capable of offering support and assistance. Unfamiliar cultural and linguistic environments, along with an inadequate social security system, left these "Nalas" who wanted to leave with nowhere to go.


With the development of the times and the economy, the main immigrant groups have changed. Increasing numbers of young people choose to change their nationality after studying abroad, pursuing different lifestyles. Compared to decades ago, the educational level of Chinese immigrants, the availability of social security measures, and public awareness of domestic violence have all improved to some extent., For example, Professor Li's university displays anti-domestic violence slogans in multiple locations. However, domestic violence remains a serious issue within Chinese immigrant communities, and legal and policy protections overseas still have many gaps. Professor Li shared a recent case she learned about: "Both parties were Chinese. The man had a history of domestic violence, and the woman wanted a divorce. But under Scottish law, they must live apart for two years before they can legally divorce."



Hope: Joint Efforts from Multiple Sectors


Achieving "love without violence" truly requires joint efforts from multiple sectors. Professor Li highly affirmed the important role played by international non-governmental organizations (NGOs) in supporting and assisting Chinese women affected by domestic violence. Reflecting on domestic violence cases from decades ago, she said: "I think the Avoice organization is excellent—it at least provides a community where women can talk to others or raise awareness through various activities." To offer more comprehensive services and stronger protection for women in need, Professor Li offered the following two suggestions to international NGOs like Avoice:



  • Provide Diversified Community Services

We should deeply consider the specific difficulties faced by those seeking help and provide diverse, targeted services, including but not limited to language training and translation, legal aid, psychological counseling, financial support, and access to expert resources and information. Such support is especially crucial for residents from other countries. Professor Li said: "Even I, after living abroad for over twenty years, don't fully understand every aspect of Western culture or certain divorce laws. If an NGO could bring together experts and volunteers from various fields, it would be extremely helpful." Additionally, differences within the Chinese women's community—such as varying levels of education—should also be taken into account. Professor Li particularly noted that for women with little formal education, informal group activities such as watching movies or having dinner together might be more effective in raising self-protection awareness and providing emotional support.


  • Promote Collaboration Among Multiple Stakeholders, including cooperation between different countries, different types of social organizations, and dispersed communities.

First, exchanges between NGOs from different countries are very important, as they can help overcome the limitations of single cultural perspectives. For example, "something not considered in the UK, or not thought of in China, or another point missed in Australia—such exchanges could be quite useful." Regarding cooperation between different types of social organizations, Professor Li explained: "There are many Confucius Institutes and women's associations abroad. If Confucius Institutes collaborate with local women's associations to host events, it not only expands their social networks but also increases understanding of relevant situations and protective measures both domestically and internationally." Additionally, Professor Li encourages more talented, young individuals to join international NGOs. For instance, she would suggest her PhD students volunteer in related public welfare activities, exploring potential development paths in this field from their own professional perspectives.



Advice: Adapt to Life, While Maintaining the Self


Professor Li observed an interesting phenomenon: in many aspects such as diet and clothing, women generally show stronger adaptability than men (this point will not be elaborated here). Immigration authorities in the West have also noticed this, which is why their programs designed to help immigrant groups adapt to new environments typically start with women and children, offering training and community services, then hoping these women will subtly influence the men in their families.


"I think women are relatively more capable of understanding and integrating into society, but integration sometimes requires a certain 'degree'—that is, you adapt to life without losing yourself, and remain independent in being who you are. Deep down, women must learn self-respect, self-reliance, self-strengthening, and self-love. This is very important. Without this internal mindset, it's hard to find that 'degree.' But if you have it, you'll naturally pay attention—what are Western living habits? What are Chinese habits? How can I better integrate into society and better grasp this 'degree'?" 



Professor Li said women can fully leverage their adaptability to learn how to live independently overseas, which requires a strong sensitivity and deeper understanding of cultural differences between China and the West. For example, British homes usually have back gardens. While this may sound like a stereotype, Chinese women—especially female students new to the UK—can use the tidiness of a landlord's back garden to assess their lifestyle when unfamiliar with the owner, thereby avoiding potentially dangerous individuals and better protecting themselves.


Professor Li Saihong's own life journey has already set an outstanding example for many female students studying abroad. Today, she is providing support and guidance to Avoice's language division, working hand in hand with Avoice to further improve the quality of its services, more effectively prevent violence against women, more concretely protect women's basic rights, and jointly create a safe environment where overseas Chinese women can be themselves—no matter who they are, whom they love, which country they come from, and going forward, regardless of their gender.
