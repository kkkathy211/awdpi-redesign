G V S .

The Global Volunteering Service (GVS) is a volunteer organization initiated by Beijing Foreign Studies University, dedicated to incubating global volunteer projects and cultivating youth global leadership. As the operational team of the alliance, Globalink Studio is fully committed to advancing this mission.


GVS × AWDPI





A new language is the starting point of a new story.


British psycholinguist Frank Smith wrote in his book *To Think* (To Think) that "One language lets you into the corridor of a lifetime, but two languages open every door along the way." Language is a communication system that conveys "sisterhood," a powerful tool for women to resist social injustice, and an essential instrument for women to venture abroad and explore the unknown.



Language is also a bridge connecting different ethnicities, nations, and regions. Students from Beijing Foreign Studies University are building a bridge between China and the international community in their unique way during this era of constant tension between openness and conservatism, and intertwined opportunities and challenges.


As an international nonprofit organization dedicated to protecting and empowering overseas Asian women, AWDPI has recruited over a hundred volunteers overseas, among whom are many students from Beijing Foreign Studies University:

Chen Xingru, a graduate of the Russian language program, was one of the early core leaders of AWDPI and led the team in establishing a comprehensive Chinese-Russian bilingual database of support resources for Asian women in Russia.


Wu Tiantian, a graduate of the International Economics and Trade program, currently serves as the head of the U.S. Office, leading the team in continuously improving the assistance process to reach survivors of violence more quickly.


Chen Wenjing, a graduate of the French program, provides aid to Chinese women in French-speaking countries who suffer from domestic and sexual violence by translating relevant anti-domestic violence legislation.


Oulu Luhao, an undergraduate student majoring in Korean, learned about the social challenges faced by women in South Korea through language study, and firmly believes that continuous education is key to fostering women's independence.


Yang Yiyun, a graduate of the English program, is currently responsible for drafting the outline of an anti-domestic violence textbook, hoping to help resource-limited women learn how to protect themselves by providing free educational materials.

 After joining AWDPI, these five BFSU alumni began five different stories—using four different languages—to break gender bias and assist survivors of domestic and sexual violence.





01


"Mutual gains and emotional connections are precisely the meaning of public welfare"


« Chen Xingru, BFSU Bachelor's, Stanford University Master's, currently working in the finance industry »


I joined the AWDPI project in 2021, and was one of the early core leaders, primarily responsible for work related to the Russian region.


In the initial phase, I worked in the General Management Division; later, I focused on the Russian region, including contacting local Russian aid organizations and Chinese community groups, establishing cooperative relationships, and collaborating with translation and academic teams to process relevant materials. My core tasks in each region were similar, and I essentially started from scratch each time.


In our work at AWDPI, the biggest challenge we faced was establishing cooperation with local organizations. Many local Russian organizations and Chinese community groups initially showed resistance to collaboration. Some believed cooperation lacked tangible benefits, while others were concerned about the sensitivity of the domestic violence issue. Particularly puzzling was that some organizations that originally actively helped vulnerable groups were skeptical of women's protection projects, even claiming without research that domestic violence did not exist locally.



In practice, although we were unable to establish a complete assistance closed-loop, we successfully built a systematic aid database covering shelters, legal support, and psychological counseling resources. This process was far more complicated than imagined, but despite numerous difficulties, we achieved some substantial results. We developed a Chinese-Russian bilingual search system to make it easier for help-seekers from different backgrounds to use. What we ultimately completed was not only a practical database of Russian support resources, but also a detailed research report on the current state of anti-domestic violence efforts in Russia. These hard-won achievements laid a solid foundation for the organization's future work.


My studies at BFSU provided crucial support for our participation in overseas women's protection projects. Solid language skills were the foundational tools for cross-border work. An international perspective enabled us to keenly recognize the needs of vulnerable overseas groups, while the interdisciplinary curriculum provided effective support for handling complex cross-border assistance cases. I remember that several of us BFSU Russian majors often stayed up late, repeatedly refining our translations to ensure accuracy of professional terminology. We had to sift through massive amounts of information, then organize it into a clear and accessible framework. Sometimes, confirming the contact information of a single shelter could require over a dozen phone calls.


Our research found that the existing domestic violence legal framework in Russia has serious shortcomings, and the number of domestic violence cases significantly increased during the pandemic. However, notably, since the 1990s, local civil society has spontaneously developed a relatively mature network of aid organizations and shelters. We conducted in-depth exchanges and cooperation with 11 representative professional aid institutions.


Particularly noteworthy is that the situation of the local Chinese women community remains far from optimistic, with many women in vulnerable or victimized conditions. Moreover, support resources specifically for Chinese women are extremely scarce, victims' awareness of their rights is often weak, and effective social support networks are few and far between. I believe that to improve the situation, we need not only to strengthen the aid institutional system but also to cultivate women's awareness of domestic violence prevention. We must enhance cooperation with more local organizations and build a collaborative network.


This project witnessed my growth in multiple aspects. Most importantly, it deepened my understanding of the essence of public welfare. I realized that public welfare is not a one-way act of giving. In the process of helping others, the positive feedback from those we assist allows me to truly experience the realization of my own value. This mutual gain and emotional connection is precisely where its meaning lies.


02


"Love, non-violence, and warmth"


« Wu Tiantian, BFSU Bachelor's in International Economics and Trade, NYU Master's in Public Policy, currently working in the finance industry »


I joined AWDPI in 2020. When I joined, the organization was newly established, so I was among the first batch of volunteers. Currently, I am the head of the U.S. Office.


When I first joined, the U.S. Office had not yet been established. I worked on several projects with the head of the Partnership Development Department and other team members. After graduating from BFSU, I went to the U.S. for graduate studies and became the head of the U.S. Office.


Each country has its own national conditions; the U.S. anti-domestic violence institutions, legal system, social culture, and political policies are different from those in China. Therefore, our U.S. Office operates independently, with a structure similar to the domestic one, but we must follow U.S. laws to protect and assist Chinese women who have experienced sexual assault and domestic violence.

The individuals in the cases we handle are generally overseas Chinese women under the age of 50. Many of them are women who recently married in the U.S. or university students in intimate relationships. Through my work, I've realized that the demand for assistance among women is very high. As a Chinese woman, simply adapting to the foreign environment is already exhausting, let alone becoming familiar with local laws—many don't even fully understand the laws in their own country. In such circumstances, women experiencing violence are often at a loss.


Over the years, I've become much more outgoing. I constantly need to connect with different people and organizations, introduce our organization to others, and ask about potential collaborations, continuously expanding our influence. At the same time, because I've been with the organization for a long time, I've maintained good relationships with many members, and made some like-minded friends. We sometimes organize team-building activities, and everyone has a great time together.



Currently, the U.S. Office is still in its early development stage, and we are working hard to build it into a more mature team. I feel that what I'm doing is very meaningful. It not only helps women who have suffered violence but also spreads knowledge about anti-domestic violence and breaks down public misconceptions.


In the future, I hope AWDPI can become more professional and develop a full-time team. Additionally, I hope we can attract more partners and donors, so we can have more sustainable and stable funding to support our operations. I also hope more Chinese and Asian women in the U.S. will learn about our organization. If they suffer violence, they can reach us in time, and we can quickly provide them with support.


I think I can summarize my experience at AWDPI in three words: Love, non-violence, and warmth.


03


"Distant lands and countless people are all connected to me"


« Chen Wenjing, BFSU Bachelor's in French, currently a graduate student in International Development Management at the London School of Economics and Political Science »


Because of my initial dream of becoming a journalist, I chose to major in French. Years of study led me to pay attention to the injustices faced by Chinese women. My overseas education background also made me realize, that once women find themselves in such difficult situations abroad, even their confidence to seek help can be affected. To be able to support them, I chose to join AWDPI.


There are mainly two things I have worked on, one is translating domestic violence laws and cases from French-speaking countries into Chinese, and the other is reviewing materials written by fellow team members.


Compared to everyday language, legal language is more precise and rigorous. Our translation work doesn't require elaborate wording, but rather the most straightforward and concise language to convey the meaning of the text.


Some specialized content is quite challenging for me, so to improve translation accuracy, I consult a large amount of reference material. We've also compiled a dedicated internal team document where everyone contributes difficult terminology, complex sentence structures, and useful reference files encountered during translation. When we hit a bottleneck in translation, we can refer to this shared document.


Through translation, I've learned about domestic violence cases in other countries, the status of anti-domestic violence laws, and many real-life cases. This experience has influenced my career direction and inspired deeper reflection on domestic violence cases both domestically and internationally. I've found that in combating domestic violence, foreign countries offer more methodologies and greater room for exploration compared to China, and I also believe that clear definitions are crucial for advancing anti-domestic violence work.



Although we each do seemingly small tasks, we must still do them. Because fundamentally, what we care about is people themselves. Only when the survivor is no longer entangled by external identity issues can her personal feelings, needs, and solutions return to the center of consideration.


If I were to summarize my takeaways in one sentence, it would be "Infinite distant places and countless people are all related to me." To me, phrases like "creating a better future" sound too idealistic. I think doing what I can in daily life is enough.


04


"Every girl must grow strong, and then protect each other."


Ou Lü Hao, undergraduate student majoring in Korean at BFSU


I joined AWDPI this spring. I've always been interested in feminism and women's protection. When I was in elementary school, I came across Emma Watson's UN speech "He for She" and various equality advocacy statements by well-known international figures. "We each must grow strong, unite, and protect each other." This idea took root in my heart and has deeply influenced me ever since.



At BFSU, where there are many female students and an open environment, I've felt a strong atmosphere of mutual care among girls, and at the same time, we engage in more healthy competition. In my spare time, I've learned a lot about gender equality movements in South Korea, which has deepened my interest in this area. I joined AWDPI both to develop my own abilities and to contribute, through my efforts, to women's protection initiatives and society.


I am currently a WeChat official account editor in the Communications Department, and have participated in multiple layout and editing tasks. This work has strengthened my skills in new media design, editing, and operations. Our working environment is very warm—everyone can speak freely, and the overall atmosphere is friendly.


AWDPI places great importance on volunteers' professional development and frequently organizes activities. I've attended several online lectures. Whenever someone encounters difficulties and asks in the group chat, there's always someone who responds promptly. Team members constantly help each other solve problems. I feel that everyone is driven by internal motivation, and there are no hierarchical power dynamics. Moreover, the organization includes not only women but also men, which I believe greatly benefits our development and the advancement of gender equality.


For me, the two key aspects of gender equality are, first, ensuring women's personal safety; and second, guaranteeing women's equal right to education. Through public education, women are more likely to develop independent consciousness. Even if a woman is born into a relatively "feudal" family, an independent-minded woman will not see herself as inferior. Additionally, I believe that achieving gender equality requires more women to speak up. Only through education can women better understand how to advocate for themselves and their communities.


05


"The road ahead is long, and our mission is heavy."


Yang Yiyun, BA in English from BFSU, MA in China Studies from Yenching Academy at Peking University, currently an ICF Life Coach


I joined AWDPI during this year's spring recruitment. Before joining, I had been conducting research on intimate partner violence and had long been interested in related practical work, which was my main motivation. I had heard of this organization before, and after completing my thesis this year, I decided to support our team.


In the field of anti-domestic violence, although much knowledge has already been produced, I believe it is more important to transform theory into public awareness in order to drive policy change. That's why I've always wanted to do work that builds a bridge between academia and the public.


I've been interested in gender issues since my undergraduate years. BFSU is a place with a strong feminist atmosphere and progressive campus culture. While majoring in English, I also studied gender sociology, gradually forming a deep connection with the field of gender studies.


The 2019 Peking University Bao Li case shocked and deeply saddened me. This incident profoundly moved me and led me to focus on non-physical violence and power dynamics in intimate relationships. Later, I chose this topic for my thesis. As my research deepened, I became increasingly aware of its significance to me and to contemporary society.



If you ask me when I first started paying attention to gender and women, perhaps it was very early. As a child, I often questioned: Why are there things girls can't do while boys can? I've always been a bit rebellious by nature—not a quiet, obedient, or docile girl—and this brought me a lot of judgment from others. Some people around me thought I was wild or overly assertive. These criticisms caused me much confusion, and that confusion pushed me to search for answers. I wanted to understand myself, and that's how I began studying gender-related issues.


Gender is a field that demands the integration of knowledge and action. You don't just learn the theories—you must constantly reflect, observe, and practice in your daily life. Practice and theory nourish each other. At the same time, the more I study, the more I realize that gender issues are present in every corner of life and are relevant to everyone.


Currently, I am in the Curriculum Development Department, mainly responsible for drafting the outline for anti-domestic violence educational materials. In my work, I can feel the professionalism of my teammates, and through like-minded exchanges, I gain resonance, inspiration, and empowerment. I'm truly grateful to be part of such an organization.


I'm also currently working in the field of mental health as a personal growth coach. I hope to support women's personal growth through coaching—especially in helping them identify and confront the trauma and false beliefs already present in their lives, reclaim their authentic selves, and build their own subjectivity. I see this as a life mission. No matter what difficulties arise, I am determined to keep going, step into the field, stabilize myself first, then aim higher.


Moving forward, I look forward to working with my teammates, focusing on doing my current tasks well, and helping more people. The road to ending domestic violence is long, and our mission is heavy.
