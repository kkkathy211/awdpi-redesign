Abstract

Previous research has largely focused on discrimination against Asian Americans in general, with little attention paid to discrimination specifically targeting Asian American women. This paper first details Asian immigration and the enactment of the Page Act, then argues that the Act heavily relied on American societal stereotypes of Asian women and the prejudice associating them with prostitution. These stereotypes and biases have led to the current hypersexualization of Asian American women, including verbal and physical violence. One root cause of the abuse suffered by Asian American women lies in postwar-era racism in the Americas and the military dominance of Western nations.


Finally, this paper emphasizes the necessity of dispelling Western misconceptions about Asian American women through solidarity with transnational feminist organizations and increased diverse media representation of Asian American women. Overall, this paper reveals that discrimination against Asian American women has persisted throughout history and concludes that such discrimination has had a destructive impact on the identity development of Asian American women.


Keywords: hypersexualization; Asian American women; anti-Asian discrimination; Asian American identity


01


Introduction


The current status of Asian American women in the United States is closely tied to the history of Asian immigration. The first wave of Asian immigration began in the early 19th century, and most of these early Asian immigrants were male. This immigration wave was driven by two main factors: (1) fleeing economic and political instability in their home countries; and (2) the California Gold Rush.


According to data from the Library of Congress, by 1849, a total of 25,000 Chinese men had arrived in California.[1]These Chinese laborers took on roles as miners, railroad builders, or other labor-intensive workers, while demanding very low wages.[1]This directly led to competition in many labor sectors, as the newly arriving Chinese immigrants accepted lower pay, sparking the first wave of hostility from American workers toward Chinese people. This marked the beginning of the "Yellow Peril," during which Asians were portrayed as foreign threats bringing economic competition and immoral behavior to America.[2].


02


Historical Causes of the Social Status of Asian American Women


2.1 The Page Act




Following the rise of anti-Asian sentiment, the Page Act of 1875 specifically targeted Asian American women (AAW) immigrants and explicitly prohibited the "importation of women for the purpose of prostitution."[3]U.S. censuses from 1870 and 1880 showed that the proportion of women in the Chinese population dropped from 6.4% to 4.6%. [4]This demonstrates the significant effectiveness of the Page Act in regulating Asian American women's immigration and restricting family formation in the United States.


The first page of the Chinese Exclusion Act

Image source: Wikipedia


The rationale behind the Page Act was not only to control immigration but also stemmed from fears about the spread of an "Oriental prostitution culture" into American society. As early as the 1820s, before the first wave of Asian immigration, there already existed sexually charged hostility toward Asians. These anti-Asian prejudices emphasized prostitution and polygamy within Chinese communities. [5]The American public associated Chinese women with the sex industry, which helped federalize the Page Act; the Act was one of the first federal policies entirely based on stereotypes of a marginalized group. After the Page Act was passed, most Americans assumed that regardless of whether a woman was in a polygamous relationship, Chinese people would regard her as a prostitute.[5]Although prostitution did exist within Asian American communities, research also shows that Chinese women constituted a relatively small proportion of the total Western prostitute population.[6].


Moreover, the interrogation methods used to identify Chinese prostitutes depended on the subjective opinions of immigration officials. Questions included: "Have you ever lived in a brothel in Hong Kong, Macao, or mainland China? Have you ever engaged in prostitution in any of these places? Are you a chaste woman?" [5]All women entering through Angel Island were questioned based on their gender and race. Furthermore, even if they answered "no" to all these questions, they could still be denied entry. Consul Bailey argued that the Chinese were "extremely cunning."[7]Through these interrogation methods, the nature of the Page Act shifted from a law originally intended to regulate prostitution to one specifically targeting all Asian American women immigrants. Additionally, John Mosby, a U.S. immigration consul in Hong Kong, noted: "To me, investigating the character of [Chinese] women immigrants and issuing them passports is futile... because in San Francisco, these passports are deemed invalid based solely on the presumption that 'all Chinese women are prostitutes.'" [8]Mosby's record reveals the true nature behind the Page Act and American society's attitudes toward Asian women. In this context, the Page Act intensified stereotypes of Asian women. Without examining actual data or evidence, many Americans feared that these so-called "Asian prostitution" phenomena would destroy America's traditional ideals of marriage, firmly believing in the stereotypes constructed by Western society, which subsequently led to various forms of discrimination against Asian American women, including the tendency to hypersexualize this group.


In U.S. immigration history, Asians were not the only group to migrate to America in large numbers. However, they were the first group to be specifically targeted by national policies and laws such as the Page Act. The Act was part of an 1882 U.S. immigration policy that banned Chinese laborers from entering the country. This outcome was not accidental but a result of racism in the Americas. This form of American racism is the primary reason behind the abuse of Asians in American society. It has led to Asians being perceived as outsiders or aliens within the American social hierarchy from the 19th century to the present day.


03


The Hypersexualization of Asian American Women


Throughout the long history of American immigration, Asian American women have been targeted due to their association with prostitution. However, this association has not disappeared in contemporary society; instead, it has evolved into microaggressions, hypersexualization, and physical violence against Asian American women. Moreover, applying the concept of intersectionality, Asian American women often face overlapping oppression based on both race and gender, resulting in compounded racialized gender discrimination. [9]The next section will present three case studies on the hypersexualization of Asian American women.


3.1 ThisIs2016




In a 2021 case study, a research team led by Sameena Azhar collected data from tweets under the hashtag #ThisIs2016, where Asian American and Pacific Islander women shared their experiences of discrimination based on race and gender. Two main conclusions emerged from this dataset: (1) Asian American and Pacific Islander women are perceived as exotic and hypersexualized; and (2) Asian American and Pacific Islander women are expected to be passive. Based on this study, many Asian American women shared experiences of being described as "exotic," and such conversations sometimes still use extremely sensationalized phrases toward Asians, such as "porcelain doll" and "Oriental girl." [10].


This indicates the continuation of stereotypes that originated during the Page Act era and highlights the verbal violence and microaggressions currently faced by Asian American women. Although this may not apply to all women within the Asian American community, the large volume of tweets proves that this is a widespread form of violence experienced by women in this group.

3.2 The Atlanta Massacre




On March 16, 2021, a shooting in the Atlanta metropolitan area, Georgia, USA, resulted in eight deaths. Six of the victims were Asian American women. The 21-year-old perpetrator claimed to suffer from "sex addiction" and viewed these Asian American women as sources of sexual temptation. [11]In 2021, prior to the Atlanta tragedy, Asians had been accused of spreading the coronavirus, sparking a new wave of anti-Asian sentiment. Some groups referred to the pandemic as the "China virus," blaming Asians. The Atlanta shooting was a turning point, revealing anti-Asian hatred and highlighting the extreme willingness of anti-Asian extremists to take action. Moreover, the fact that the shooting occurred at a spa and the victims were Asian American women once again indicates that certain groups continue to associate Asian American women with the sex industry. Such associations make Asian American women more vulnerable to physical violence, especially during the current global COVID-19 pandemic.

3.3 Case Study #3: Pornhub Popular Search Results




In the "2019 Pornhub Year in Review Report," popular search terms reflected a fetishistic fascination with Asian women: "Japan" was the top search term, "Korea" ranked fifth, and "Asian" ranked sixth. [12]Asian American women are excessively exoticized and linked to "sexual temptation." In many cases, this intense sexualization leads to dehumanization and objectification. The "objectification" of Asian American women originated in 19th-century stereotypes. Through depictions of the "feminized Orient," Asian American women are portrayed as submissive and passive figures. From "geisha" to "exotic doll," this racialized sexuality exposes Asian American women to risks of violence, as certain groups view them as tools to satisfy sexual desires and believe they possess inherently submissive traits. [10].

3.4 Why Target Asian Women?




After reviewing the three case studies above, this section will explore why Asian American women are hypersexualized. From the Page Act and racialism in the Americas to dehumanization and the hypersexualization of Asian American women, where did it all begin? The origins of this discrimination can be traced back to Western imperialism during World War II and proxy wars. The presence of U.S. military forces in Asia during World War II, the Vietnam War, and the Korean War led to the notion of a "feminized Orient" submitting to dominant white soldiers. [13]After World War II, the U.S. collaborated with the South Korean government to establish military camp towns for soldier entertainment. In 1965, 85% of surveyed American soldiers who had served in Asian countries reported having had sexual relations with Asian sex workers. [14]During this period, extramarital affairs between soldiers and Asian women were common, leading to the formation of stereotypes about women in Asian countries. Finally, the contradiction of this postwar prostitution system lies in the fact that although it was systematically designed to meet the needs of U.S. military personnel, it ultimately resulted in Asian women being targeted.


04


Reimagining the Future of Asian American Women


Asian American women face stereotypes and injustices rooted in racial and gender biases, making it necessary to deconstruct these prejudices and stereotypes. In recent research, a website called "Big Bad Chinese Mother" (BBCM) has emerged online, aiming to subvert common assumptions about Asian American women through humor. [15]As a paradigmatic example of cyberfeminism, the BBCM website uses humor and satire to dismantle assumptions established by imperialism and patriarchy. Additionally, the site's creators added tags similar to those used by Asian pornographic websites, so users searching for such sites are directly redirected to BBCM. [15]Such creative approaches not only help Asian Americans overcome the problem of hypersexualization; they also empower other Asian American women to resist all forms of discrimination and help construct a new, distinct identity for Asian American women. [15].


In recent years, researchers have begun proposing the idea of transnational feminism in the postmodern era. However, there exists a disconnect between Asian American women and many global feminist groups. Some Asian American activists believe that while Asian American women may experience gender-based violence like all other women, the discrimination they face also carries distinct racialized characteristics. Therefore, due to differences in the nature of discrimination, they find it difficult to act in unison with mainstream feminist activists. Nevertheless, despite these challenges, solidarity among transnational feminist organizations would only benefit Asian women's rights activists. Despite cultural divisions, the ultimate goal remains dismantling the colonial hierarchies left behind by Western imperialism. Thus, Asian American women should not focus on differences within gender, but rather view gender as a container encompassing all differences. [16].


Moreover, another emerging issue facing the future of Asian American women is the lack of female representation to break rigid stereotypes. Media portrayals are a key factor shaping public perceptions of groups. Furthermore, Asian American women in media often conform to Western stereotypes of passivity and sexualization. The underrepresentation of Asian Americans in Hollywood limits the ability to challenge these biases and assumptions. To enhance representation of Asian women, establishing strong role models who can break through Western imperialist depictions of Asian women is key to establishing a new identity.


05


Conclusion


In summary, discrimination against Asian women originated in anti-Asian immigration sentiment. Subsequently, the historical association of Asian American women with prostitution and the sex industry led to the passage of the Page Act, which federalized social prejudices and exacerbated broader misunderstandings about Asian American women. In today's society, the hypersexualization of Asian American women has been confirmed in multiple case studies. First, under the #ThisIs2016 hashtag, a significant number of Asian American women shared experiences of verbal sexualization due to their race. Second, the recent Atlanta shootings revealed violence against Asian American women, indicating that rising racial tensions have made them targets of abusive attacks. Finally, the most searched terms on Pornhub are "Japan" and "Asian," demonstrating the excessive fetishization of Asian women; the numerous stereotypes targeting Asian women are essentially the result of intersecting gender and racial biases. This article has identified the main factors contributing to the racialized sexism faced by Asian American women today. Future research could benefit from studying specific laws or policies targeting hate crimes. Additionally, researchers should emphasize how these laws and policies influence abusive behaviors, especially physical violence against Asian American women. Future studies could conduct comparative analyses of societal attitudes toward Asian Americans during the Atlanta shootings amid the COVID-19 pandemic, as well as examine the progress and coping strategies Asian American women have developed in response to ongoing abuse against their community. Furthermore, with increasing social media usage, studying stereotypes of Asian American women in media offers a pathway to dismantling these labels. Given the large number of teenagers on social media, positive and diverse portrayals of Asian American women are crucial to mitigating discrimination against them.
