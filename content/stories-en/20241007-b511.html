Introduction:


August brought frequent heatwaves, with lives across the globe enduring the scorching sun—and women's circumstances have been equally unbearable. In India, a young female doctor was brutally murdered at her workplace, triggering nationwide strikes by medical professionals and public outrage. In South Korea, pornographic content created using deepfake technology has plunged countless women into fear; its rapid spread and strong anonymity make timely regulation a daunting challenge. In Afghanistan, three years after the Taliban seized power, women's rights have been severely eroded, with basic rights stripped away and women completely disempowered. Meanwhile, violence against Asians has not ceased—whether within families or in public spaces, the safety of Asian women still demands greater protection. In Arizona, U.S., Chinese-American professor David Zhu was arrested for fatally shooting his wife, with long-standing domestic violence possibly behind the tragedy. In Toronto, Canada, a prominent real estate agent was murdered and her body burned. This month, women around the world have shared the same distress—worry, fear, disappointment, and hardship. Yet some have not given up: regrouping amid anxiety and fear, pressing forward through disappointment, and continuing to fight and raise their voices for women's rights.

A 31-year-old female doctor in India was sexually assaulted and brutally murdered in a hospital, sparking public anger and widespread protests


In August 2024, a 31-year-old female doctor in Kolkata, West Bengal, India, was sexually assaulted and murdered at her workplace. Her body was discovered at RG Kar Medical College, where she worked, during a break from a 36-hour night shift. The autopsy report revealed multiple injuries and severe sexual violence.


The incident triggered nationwide anger and protests, with doctors and the public jointly calling for enhanced safety in medical facilities. Led by medical groups, protests saw many hospitals suspending all non-emergency services, demanding government action to protect healthcare workers. They called for the creation of specific laws to prevent similar violent incidents. Indian Prime Minister Modi also addressed the case in his Independence Day speech, emphasizing the need to confront violence against women.


This case not only sparked public outrage but also exposed safety risks within India's healthcare system. Medical professionals lack effective protection during shifts, have no secure rest areas while on duty, and face sexual harassment from colleagues and patients alike. Twenty-three medical colleges in West Bengal jointly declared that this is not merely a doctors' movement, but a struggle for workplace safety for all women. Meanwhile, the judiciary has intervened in the investigation, criticizing the local government and police for mishandling the case, and ordered the Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI) to take over to ensure a fair inquiry.


Despite India strengthening laws against sexual violence after the 2012 Delhi bus rape case, violence against women has still not been effectively curbed. In 2022, crime rates against women in India rose by 4% compared to the previous year, highlighting the severity of such incidents.

South Korea sees surge in deepfake pornography crimes, causing panic over online sexual violence


In August, South Korean media exposed a large amount of deepfake pornographic content circulating on Telegram. In paid channels, male users upload photos of women and pay to have them turned into pornographic images and videos—most of the photos depict young women, including underage girls. These pornographic materials are widely shared in anonymous Telegram chat rooms, some of which have up to 220,000 members.


From January to July this year, South Korean police reported 297 cases of deepfake-related sexual crimes. In 2021, the first year such data was collected, only 156 such cases were reported for the entire year. News of the surge in deepfake content has caused panic among young women, many of whom have begun deleting selfies and other personal photos from social media, fearing their images could be used to create deepfakes.


Although the South Korean National Assembly passed a law in 2020 banning the creation and distribution of such content, users often bypass government censorship and spread the material on anonymous platforms, where it spreads rapidly while police investigations lag behind. Investigators require reports of child sexual abuse to initiate probes, and gaining access to deepfake chat rooms requires court approval—factors that add a hidden and dangerous dimension to this form of sexual violence.

Three years since Taliban takeover: Afghan women's rights severely damaged


On August 13, Diana Quick, UN Women's Country Representative in Afghanistan, said at a press conference that in the three years since the Taliban took power, Afghan women have been stripped of basic rights and autonomy.


On the same day, UN Women released a report based on multiple rounds of consultations with thousands of Afghan women since August 2021, illustrating trends in the erosion of women's rights in Afghanistan. The report shows Afghan women are losing power severely. Within families, the proportion of women who feel they can influence household decisions dropped by nearly 60% over the past year. At the community level, 98% of surveyed women said they have little or no influence on community decision-making. Additionally, Afghan women face a serious mental health crisis: 68% reported their mental health as "poor" or "very poor," and 8% said they know at least one woman who has attempted suicide.


Over the past three years, UN Women's investments in Afghan women have focused on three key strategies: providing flexible, long-term funding to grassroots women's organizations, designing programs specifically aimed at eliminating discrimination against women and girls, and creating spaces for Afghan women to directly voice their concerns and priorities. Quick stated that UN Women will continue such investments to confront this deepening crisis in women's rights.

Chinese-American professor David Zhu from Arizona State University arrested for allegedly killing his wife


David Zhu (Zhu Hongquan), a Chinese-American professor at Arizona State University, was arrested by police on suspicion of fatally shooting his wife, Susan Yijuan Yan. The incident occurred at a residence in Scottsdale, where police found Yan with gunshot wounds. Though rushed to hospital, she died from her injuries. Zhu has been charged with first-degree murder.


Police stated that Zhu turned himself in immediately after the shooting and called 911, admitting he had shot his wife. At the time, the couple's 19-year-old daughter and 14-year-old son were also at home but were unharmed. Upon arrival, police found Yan lying in the hallway outside the master bedroom and discovered a shotgun and two spent cartridges inside the bedroom. Additionally, another handgun was found in the bedroom, and a rifle was discovered in the closet.


Court documents show that Zhu told police he had intended to fire at the wall, not at his wife. However, investigators found two shotgun wounds on Yan's back, contradicting Zhu's statement. Police suspect Zhu deliberately shot his wife, causing her death.


It is reported that the couple had a long history of domestic violence. After the incident, the children told police that their parents had had several intense arguments and conflicts over the years. A loud argument between the couple shortly before the shooting may have been the direct trigger for the tragedy.


Zhu is a professor in the Department of Management and Entrepreneurship at the W. P. Carey School of Business, Arizona State University, where he has taught since 2009 and served as director of the China DBA program. The university issued a statement expressing sorrow for all affected by this tragedy but did not disclose further details. The case is under further investigation.

Chinese-Canadian woman murdered after going missing; police identify 47-year-old man as murder suspect, possibly fled abroad


Yuk-Ying Anita Mui, a 56-year-old well-known real estate agent in the Toronto area, Canada, went missing after leaving home on August 9. After her family reported her missing, the case drew widespread attention from the local Chinese community.

 

On August 12, police discovered a charred body 200 kilometers north of where Ms. Mei went missing; the body was later confirmed to be hers. Three suspects aged 16 to 17 were charged with weapons possession, fraud, and other offenses, but the charges did not include murder.

 

On August 28, police identified 47-year-old Zhi Xiong Hu (pronounced) as the murder suspect and issued a first-degree murder arrest warrant across Canada. Zhi Xiong Hu may have already left the country, and police are cooperating with the Border Services Agency to apprehend him. According to records, Hu's registered address is in a building in Markham, Ontario. A man in the building said Hu is a general contractor in residential construction and renovation. Police have not yet disclosed the relationship between the suspect and Ms. Mei or the motive for the crime.

San Francisco mayoral election: courting Chinese American voters

is key


San Francisco will hold its mayoral election in November, and candidates are actively courting Chinese American voters, recognizing the influence of the Chinese community. In San Francisco, Chinese Americans make up more than one-fifth of the local population, and Chinese American voters are seen as having the power to sway election outcomes. During two citywide recall elections in 2022 that shocked the city, Chinese Americans were considered one of the driving forces.


There are currently no major Chinese American contenders in the race, but candidates have already begun competing for Chinese American support. They are reaching out to voters through Chinese social media platforms such as WeChat, and some have recruited Chinese community leaders who played key roles in the recall elections to take prominent positions in this year's campaigns. Some are even debating how their Chinese names should appear on ballots and campaign signs.


For example, candidate Farrell has pledged to take a tougher stance on crime, fentanyl abuse, and homelessness in San Francisco. London Breed, the city's current mayor, recently visited China and is running for re-election. During her visit, she called for the resumption of direct flights from China to San Francisco International Airport and promised to bring giant pandas to the San Francisco Zoo. However, many Chinese American voters interviewed said that more changes are needed and that city leaders must do more to ensure community safety.

Cambodian Prime Minister welcomes return of artifacts by the Metropolitan Museum of Art and other institutions  


Cambodian Prime Minister Hun Manet presided over a ceremony on Thursday to welcome the return of 70 precious cultural artifacts from overseas museums and private collectors, stating that the government will continue efforts to bring more artifacts back to Cambodia. The exhibition of returned items is being held at the Peace Palace, the seat of the national government.


According to a statement from the Ministry of Culture and Fine Arts, the 70 Cambodian cultural objects were returned through voluntary repatriation, negotiations, seizures, and legal proceedings, originating from the Lindeman family, Jim Clark, the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York, and other American private collectors. The returned artifacts include significant Hindu and Buddhist masterpieces from the Angkor period (9th to 14th centuries), particularly rare stone sculptures such as mythological warrior figures from the Indian epic Mahabharata, statues of Shiva and Parvati, and the Ardhanarishvara statue from the ancient capital of Koh Ker.


Many of the artifacts were looted during Cambodia's prolonged civil war and the rule of the Khmer Rouge regime. Some were bought and sold by the well-known art dealer Douglas Latchford. Latchford was indicted in 2019 on suspicion of orchestrating the sale of stolen Cambodian antiquities but died the following year, having denied involvement in smuggling.


The U.S. Embassy in Cambodia stated that it has facilitated the return of more than 150 antiquities, including previously repatriated items such as a Hindu Ganesha statue and a 10th-century "Skanda on a peacock" statue. This repatriation effort is seen as the right step toward protecting Cambodia's cultural heritage. Although U.S.-Cambodia relations have been affected by human rights issues, the two countries continue to cooperate in cultural preservation.
