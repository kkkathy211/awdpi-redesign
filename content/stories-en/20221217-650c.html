Avoice Series Psychological Course Introduction:

Avoice's Self-Healing Series Course is designed for survivors who have experienced relationship violence, aiming to help survivors understand their own reactions and confusion, and actively identify internal and external resources. Through this course, we want to convey the following messages:

• It is not your fault 

• No matter what emotions or reactions you have, they are normal 

• You are not alone; you can receive support

• Your experience deserves to be understood, and deserves to be told.


This session lasts approximately 15 minutes. You will learn about:

• Characteristics of relationship violence

• Possible impacts of relational trauma

• How to view trauma


If you feel confused, angry, anxious, guilty, or any other emotions due to past experiences, these feelings are normal—and it is not your fault. Please understand, trauma can be healed. At the moment you enter here, we have already begun a new journey together.


01.

What is trauma?


Each of us has experienced injuries of varying degrees during our growth. These may stem from insufficient care, response, or recognition, or from unmet needs for attention and protection. They may also arise from long-term neglect and abuse, sudden betrayal, unfair treatment, or even psychological manipulation and physical violence. These harms may come from family members, partners, or friends, as well as from workplaces and society. Because everyone's experiences and sensitivities differ, our responses to events and the experiences we have are also different. But no matter what feelings you have, they are your authentic reactions.


Image source: freepik


So, what is trauma? Trauma is mainly divided into developmental trauma and relational trauma. Developmental trauma arises from injuries during the growth process, while relational trauma primarily stems from interpersonal relationships, such as school bullying and gender-based violence. When pain becomes unbearable, causing intrusive thoughts and emotional difficulties, and long-term affects our lives, relationships, and social interactions, the pain can be termed trauma. You do not need to feel guilt or shame about trauma. It is precisely a reminder that you have experienced something painful, and now you need to take better care of yourself.


Relational trauma includes the psychological trauma caused by intimate partner violence. Forms of violence in intimate relationships are numerous, including persistent harassment and stalking, deprivation of freedom, psychological and emotional abuse, sexual violence and physical abuse, economic control, and abusive threats. Intimate partner violence is often hidden, leading victims to question whether their own mistakes caused the situation, making it difficult to recognize the abuser's violent behaviors. A more covert form includes the widely known "gaslighting," which is a type of emotional abuse. It involves not only verbal belittlement and degradation but also emotional blackmail. The abuser might say, "If you don't do as I say or meet my demands, it means you don't love me," which often makes the victim feel extremely inadequate and may lead them to blame themselves even when suffering severe abuse. Economic control is another hard-to-detect form; regardless of whether the victim has an income, the abuser restricts and controls their access to financial resources, requiring them to report even for minor expenses. Threats of abuse mainly convey violent messages through language—for instance, the abuser may say, "If you don't do as I say, I will threaten the lives of you, your family, or your pets," which is also a form of violence. All these forms of violence often share one commonality: controlling behaviors and an unequal power relationship between the two parties.


02.

Myths about intimate partner violence


Survivors of intimate partner violence suffer harm not only within the relationship, but also from societal misunderstandings that repeatedly retraumatize them. Common misconceptions about intimate partner violence include: first, the belief that abusers lash out at their partners because they have bad tempers or high stress, so "if they just changed their temper, everything would be fine." We need to distinguish that there is no necessary link between anger and violent behavior; second, the belief that survivors are weak or flawed, thus inviting violence—however, the survivor's behavior is never an excuse for the abuser's actions, and the abuser must be held accountable; third, framing intimate partner violence as family conflict or emotional discord, reducing it to "private family matters" and downplaying its seriousness—intimate partner violence is a violent crime, not a family dispute; fourth, due to internalized social norms and moral rules such as "family shame should not be made public," survivors are silenced and even blamed.


Image source: freepik


All of these attitudes overlook the most critical point: the power imbalance between intimate partners. Although people of any gender or sexual orientation can experience intimate partner violence, research shows that the majority of survivors are women—proportionally at least three times higher than men—highlighting gender inequality. Under such unequal dynamics, survivors' experiences may be misunderstood or ignored by others, such as questioning why survivors don't just leave. This "victim-blaming" logic often causes secondary trauma.


The phenomenon of gender power imbalance creates a structural dilemma for those involved in gender-based violence. A structural dilemma refers to the situation where, within an unequal relationship, the individuals involved lack relevant support resources, such as economic income and social connections. They may also face threats from the perpetrator, a lack of understanding from other family members, or even stigmatization from societal attitudes. This means survivors are not facing a single issue, but a series of complex circumstances. All of this makes leaving much more difficult. In fact, any effort survivors make is a resistance against systemic oppression—and this is highly commendable. However, leaving such an environment requires help and support from others; it cannot be achieved by the survivor alone. Clearly, intimate partner violence is not merely a private matter. When facing such issues, everyone can take action to show their stance or offer support.


03.

What do you experience when facing trauma?


Image source: freepik


1. Physical and psychological reactions to trauma


When understanding your own experiences or touching on past trauma, you may have a range of reactions and feelings. First, you may experience various emotions such as fear, anger, self-blame, and self-loathing. Emotionally, you may feel out of control or numb. When facing potentially harmful situations, your alertness may become overly sensitive or completely shut down. You may also experience physical or physiological symptoms such as difficulty concentrating, insomnia, nightmares, and bodily pain. You may feel confused due to fragmented memories, feel intense self-blame, and have recurring thoughts like "I should have done this" or "I shouldn't have done that," hoping to change what happened. Second, in interpersonal relationships, you may often feel isolated, forced into silence, because you feel no one can understand you, and you can no longer trust others. We want to emphasize: no matter what happened, it is not your fault—the one who should be condemned is always the abuser. You may have temporarily suppressed or forgotten your own strength, feeling helpless in the face of events, but in fact, most of your current feelings and reactions are ways of trying to protect yourself.

2. Learned helplessness after trauma


Facing relationship violence, you may often feel helpless and experience frustration in other areas of life, feeling you lack the courage and strength to face or change things. Here, we want to introduce the concept of learned helplessness —a condition where a person, after prolonged exposure to uncontrollable situations, develops a cognitive belief that no matter how hard they try, they cannot change the outcome, and thus completely gives up trying. Initially, when facing relationship violence, you might look for causes within yourself—thinking, "If I change, things will get better." This relates to our sense of psychological control: when we cannot control the external environment, we try to control ourselves to change it. But the occurrence of violence is solely related to the abuser. Survivors cannot truly protect themselves from harm, which intensifies feelings of inferiority, self-blame, and guilt. Even when encountering someone they could ask for help or an opportunity to leave, survivors may test it cautiously, fearing that failure could trigger even more severe violence, leading to renewed panic and fear.


If you remain in these negative experiences for a long time, or continue to be troubled by the original relationship without being able to build a new life, new relationships, or a new self, then you need to proactively care for yourself. You need to prepare to leave the person and environment that harm you, because such relationships are more likely to deteriorate than improve. Leaving is an active step toward self-care and self-compassion.

3. Accompanying Your Trauma Responses


When these emotions and reactions repeatedly intrude, you can seek methods of self-soothing and offer yourself greater kindness and patience. The key to healing lies in allowing yourself to fully experience safety within a secure environment, rather than remaining in a constant state of tension and anxiety that prevents relaxation and vigilance. We need to pay more attention to and allow space for our feelings and reactions, actively seeking understanding and support from others. Everyone's experiences are different, and so are the methods that work. Exploring and finding what suits you also takes time. Please remember, only when touching pain within safe environments and relationships—only when safety is first felt—can healing truly occur.


04.

Resilience

The "antidote" to psychological trauma


So, what can we do to help ourselves in the face of trauma? In fact, each of us possesses a certain level of resilience, which can help us navigate difficult situations. Just like exercising muscles, you can intentionally spend time strengthening your resilience. It can be cultivated through four core components: enhancing your sense of connection with others and the world, taking care of your physical health, restoring psychological resilience, and discovering a sense of meaning. For example, you can strengthen your connections with others by joining communities or organizations, because having a network that can share your pain and offer understanding and support is crucial—it helps us combat current anxiety and unease. In daily life, you can care for your physical health through regular exercise and nutritious diets, and increase bodily awareness through practices like yoga, breathing exercises, and mindfulness. Only by first feeling your body and connecting with your inner self can you rediscover who you are, establish your sense of worth, and truly own yourself.


05.

Stepping Out of Intimate Partner Violence


In our article, we use the terms "individual involved" and "survivor." Compared to the term "victim," "Individual involved" and "survivor" are more neutral expressions. Although your experience has caused immense pain, it is not your fault—those who should be held accountable are always the perpetrators. The pain you feel is precisely the cry of your inner self striving to uphold your rights.


When you see this article, it means you have already survived the harm, and you have already taken the first step toward helping yourself, striving to grow anew from suffering. Tell yourself: Even in the midst of hardship, I still have the strength to survive, and I am creating new possibilities. I can actively free myself from difficult situations and seek out resources to address my challenges, ensuring that reality does not simply repeat the past. My efforts and attempts are worthy of recognition. I am amazing.


In the next lesson, we will discuss how to recognize emotions and feelings, and achieve connection with the self—this will also be the starting point of self-healing. Thank you for being here; we'll see you in the next session.
