16 years ago, the United Nations General Assembly adopted a resolution calling for intensified efforts to eliminate all forms of violence against women and requested the UN Secretary-General to establish a database to document the extent, nature, and consequences of violence against women, as well as to examine the impact and effectiveness of policy responses to eliminate such violence. In 2009, this database was officially developed and launched as the "UN Secretary-General's Database on Violence against Women." In 2016, against the backdrop of the adoption of the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, UN Women updated and redesigned the database, renaming it the "Global Database on Violence against Women."


Image source: Global Database on Violence against Women


calling for the establishment of a database on violence against women 16 years later, the COVID-19 pandemic has shaken the world, and violence against women has only increased under pandemic conditions. The World Health Organization has pointed out that the prevalence of intimate partner violence generally rises after disasters. The spread of COVID-19 has intensified crises in gender relations, and at this moment, the surge in domestic violence has become an issue that our society and the entire world cannot ignore.


Abuse is not love, asserting dominance is not love, and ignoring conflict is not love. Behind domestic violence and various forms of violence against women, discrimination and objectification must be recognized, empathy and compassion must be called for, and the current situation must be taken seriously—requiring collective action to actively change it.

How can we understand the violence women endure? Heavy, tangible data is one of the most direct and comprehensive "windows." To date, multiple global databases have documented and verified acts of violence against women. In this issue, Avoice will use big data to tell the story of violence surrounding women worldwide. These data come from authoritative databases, and behind the numbers lie countless women's raw life experiences—countless women struggling desperately yet unable to escape the quagmire.


It is not the numbers that are cold—it is the violence.


As early as 1993, the United Nations defined "violence against women" in General Assembly Resolution 48/104, the Declaration on the Elimination of Violence against Women, as "any act of gender-based violence that results in, or is likely to result in, physical, sexual or psychological harm or suffering to women, including threats of such acts, coercion or arbitrary deprivation of liberty, whether occurring in public or in private life." To this day, acts of violence against women remain countless.……What the media exposes is only the tip of the iceberg. Behind the vast numbers recorded in databases lies an unimaginable dark world and layers of unbearable wounds.


01 Sexual Violence: 1 in 3 Women Globally Have Experienced It


In 2021, the United Nations released the full report "2018 Estimates of the Global Prevalence of Violence against Women," the first-ever global estimate of violence against women during the 2015–2030 Sustainable Development Goals period, based on surveys of 1,266,758 women across 137 countries and regions worldwide (including 41 high-income and 96 middle- and low-income countries and regions). The data clearly confirm that violence against women remains highly prevalent. Today, violence against women aged 15 and above is a global, large-scale public health issue affecting hundreds of millions of women.


Image source: UN Women WeChat Official Account


The report primarily estimates two types of violence: intimate partner violence and non-partner sexual violence. The data show that intimate partner violence—physical, sexual, or psychological violence by husbands or male intimate partners—is the most common form of violence against women globally.


Image source: UN Women WeChat Official Account


According to the report, in 2018, approximately 736 million to 852 million women aged 15 and above (about one-third of women worldwide) had experienced at least one incident of physical and/or sexual violence by a current or former husband or male intimate partner, or sexual violence by a non-partner—such as strangers, acquaintances, friends, peers, teachers, neighbors, family members—or both forms of violence in their lifetime. Additionally, intimate partner violence occurs early: it is estimated that nearly one-quarter of married or partnered girls in the youngest age group (15–19 years) had experienced at least one incident of physical and/or sexual violence by an intimate partner in their lifetime, and 16% of adolescent girls and young women aged 15–24 had experienced intimate partner violence in the past 12 months.


02 Shoving, Slapping: 1 in 5 Women in the EU Have Experienced It


Within the EU, in March 2014, the European Union conducted interviews with 42,000 women across its 28 member states to document their experiences of physical violence, sexual violence, psychological violence, stalking, and sexual harassment, publishing the first EU-wide survey on violence against women: "Violence against Women: An EU-wide Survey."


The survey shows that, regarding non-partners, 1 in 5 women (22%) has experienced physical violence, which takes many forms, including shoving, slapping, being hit with objects, having hair pulled, being kicked or punched, being burned, having the neck choked or strangled, being stabbed or cut, and being slammed head-first into objects.


These terrifying physical violence incidents often occur repeatedly. The data show that among 4,872 respondents who had been shoved, about 19% had experienced six or more incidents of violence; among 3,292 respondents who had been slapped, 20% had experienced six or more incidents.


03 Lack of Accountability: 75% of Men Faced No Consequences


The survey indicates that (intimate) partner violence is widespread: among female respondents who had had a partner, 39% reported experiencing physical and/or sexual violence from an intimate partner. 52% of male respondents admitted to having committed violence against an intimate partner, and among men who had raped women, 75% faced no legal consequences.


As for the motives behind rape, the main reasons were "sexual entitlement"—the belief that men have the right to sexual relations with women regardless of consent (86%), seeking fun/boredom (58%), anger/punishment (43%), and being under the influence of alcohol (24%).


Image source: Souxinyuan


The data also show that among all male respondents, 73% believed men should be tough, 52% supported men using violence to defend their honor, and 72% believed men should be the decision-makers on important family matters. These so-called "masculine norms" are closely linked to violent behaviors.


Furthermore, the survey shows that among men who had committed rape, 67% committed their first rape between ages 20 and 29, and 24% committed their first rape between ages 15 and 19. These data indicate that education on preventing sexual violence and respecting women must begin in adolescence.


See the violence, end the violence, love without violence


Authoritative data on violence and injustice against women extend far beyond this, and the latest data during the COVID-19 pandemic urgently require investigation and research by relevant institutions.


According to the latest 2022 data from UN Women, during the pandemic, in addition to facing a higher likelihood of violence, women also face greater difficulty accessing vaccines, obtaining food, bear increased unpaid care and domestic work, and experience reduced incomes. Behind these changing figures are the tears shed by individual women suffering violence.


Around the world, violence against women persists, media coverage of domestic violence and related issues continues to emerge, and unjust systems against women still exist. How to truly "see" women and provide timely, effective support remains a long and arduous journey.
