 Introduction:

One in five women worldwide lives with some form of disability, and they are two to three times more likely than non-disabled women to experience gender-based violence across all areas, often facing longer-term and more severe forms of such violence—Women with disabilities experience both the same forms of violence as other women, as well as specific forms of violence targeting their disability. Clearly, women with disabilities face numerous barriers when seeking shelter or justice. Yet current laws, policies, and programs on gender-based violence rarely address the specific challenges faced by women with disabilities. In terms of data collection, it is also difficult to obtain accurate and comprehensive statistics disaggregated by both sex and disability.

I

Gender-Based Violence Against Women with Disabilities



 What is Gender-Based Violence?

The World Health Organization defines violence against women as "any gender-based act of violence that results in, or is likely to result in, physical, sexual, or psychological harm or suffering to women, including threats of such acts, coercion or arbitrary deprivation of liberty, whether occurring in public or private life." Gender-based violence takes many forms: physical, emotional, psychological, and economic, and perpetrators may include intimate partners, family members, caregivers, medical and other service providers, law enforcement officers, military personnel, educators, employers, and strangers.


Forms of Violence Experienced by Women with Disabilities

  • Women with disabilities experience the same forms of gender-based violence as non-disabled women,, such as intimate partner violence, domestic violence, sexual violence, and sexual harassment.


  • Women with disabilities also face specific forms of sexual violence due to their disability,, including sexual abuse by medical staff, withholding of medication or assistive devices, intentional failure to provide prescribed care, denial of essential items for eating, toileting, or personal hygiene, controlling sensory equipment, financial control, restricting access to communication devices, "virgin rape" (some individuals believe that having sex with a virgin can cure HIV or other sexually transmitted diseases; they wrongly assume that women with disabilities have low sexual desire and thus possess a purifying power, leading to rape of disabled women), long-term violence in care institutions, and forced institutionalization.


  • Women with disabilities also disproportionately experience other forms of violence faced by women, such as forced medical procedures, especially forced sterilization, contraception, and abortion.


  • Guardianship systems and other formal or informal proxy decision-making mechanisms can strip women with disabilities of their legal rights and autonomy, placing them at greater risk of gender-based violence. Depriving women with disabilities of decision-making power may increase their dependence on abusers and make them more vulnerable to coercive medical interventions.


  • Women with disabilities who belong to (or are perceived to belong to) other marginalized groups—such as migrants, ethnic minorities, Indigenous peoples, LGBT+ individuals, older women, or adolescents—may experience more complex forms of gender-based violence.


Barriers to Ending Violence

  • Women with disabilities may fear leaving or reporting abusers due to emotional, financial, or physical dependence. This dependence stems from an isolated environment caused by low employment rates, lower education levels, and widespread societal discrimination against women with disabilities.


  • Women with disabilities may worry that reporting abuse could lead to retaliation, being placed in institutions, losing assistive devices, or losing custody of their children.


  • Women with disabilities often cannot access emergency shelters or other emergency housing, and are rarely offered alternative suitable accommodations. Worse still, women with disabilities are sometimes directly excluded from lists of those eligible for assistance. Moreover, information about emergency housing is rarely provided in alternative formats accessible to women with disabilities.


  • Shelters may have "no animals allowed" policies, which pose a barrier for women with disabilities who rely on service animals such as guide dogs.


  • Service providers (including social workers and healthcare workers) often lack necessary training to effectively support women with disabilities who have experienced gender-based violence..


  • Service providers may wrongly assume that people with disabilities do not have sex lives or intimate relationships, thus failing to recognize signs of domestic or sexual violence.



 Barriers to Accessing Justice

  • Police stations may lack the necessary facilities to ensure effective communication with people with disabilities, and women with disabilities may also lack the physical means to reach police stations.


  • Information about legal rights is often not available in alternative formats (such as Braille, plain language, or text-to-speech), which may prevent women from fully understanding their rights and filing complaints to the fullest extent permitted by law.


  • Women with disabilities may fear losing custody of their children after reporting domestic violence—courts may apply discriminatory stereotypes, assuming the non-disabled parent is more capable.


  • Police, judicial authorities, and the public may perceive women with disabilities as less credible witnesses than others, and in some cases, women with disabilities who have been deprived of legal capacity may be prohibited from testifying in court.


  • The only or primary witness in cases of violence against women with disabilities is often the woman herself, increasing the difficulty for her to report violence or testify in court.


  • Leniency toward perpetrators of violence against women with disabilities encourages further crimes, as offenders know the justice system is unlikely to take such reports seriously and thus act with impunity.


Pathways for Women with Disabilities to Access Legal Aid

Women with disabilities have the right to seek and receive legal aid after experiencing gender-based violence. To ensure this, women with disabilities must have guaranteed access to legal aid. This means:


  • Law enforcement, judicial authorities, and other actors in the justice system must be trained to recognize and respond to gender-based violence against women with disabilities. In addition, lawyers must receive targeted training to represent women with disabilities.


  • To protect women with disabilities' right to information, details about obtaining protection orders and other legal rights must be provided in multiple formats.


  • States must recognize the legal capacity of women with disabilities and give equal weight to their testimony.


  • Women with disabilities must have opportunities to access necessary legal support to understand and participate in judicial procedures.


  • When women with disabilities bring cases of gender-based violence to court, they must be free from fear of being institutionalized in psychiatric facilities or losing custody of their children..


  • Women with disabilities must be able to access police stations and courts (including witness stands) easily and without barriers.


II

Freedom from Gender-Based Violence

Is a Legitimate Right for All Women with Disabilities



Several international and regional human rights treaties protect a range of fundamental rights for women with disabilities, including the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD) and the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW), which explicitly affirm women with disabilities' right to be free from gender-based violence. These rights include:


  •  Freedom from gender-based violence

  • Life

  •  Liberty and security of person

  •  Equality and non-discrimination

  •  Accessibility

  • Freedom from torture, inhuman or degrading treatment or punishment

  • (For survivors of abuse, violence, and exploitation) the right to physical and psychological recovery and social reintegration

  • Freedom and equality in marriage

  • Freedom from harmful practices

  •  Equal recognition before the law

  •  Adequate standard of living and social protection

  • (In times of danger) personal protection

  •  Independent living and inclusion in the community


Government Obligations Regarding Gender-Based Violence

Under international law, governments have the obligation to respect, protect, and fulfill the rights of women with disabilities to be free from gender-based violence. Governments must:


  • Respect

This obligation requires states—and any state actors—not to commit any acts of gender-based violence. States must enact laws and regulations to prevent gender-based violence, such as ensuring that laws and law enforcement prohibit forced sterilization or abortion without the consent of women with disabilities.


  • Protect

This obligation requires states to exercise due diligence in protecting women with disabilities from private acts of gender-based violence.


  • Fulfill

This obligation requires states to take practical measures to ensure women with disabilities can live free from gender-based violence, such as ensuring access to protective services and training service providers to deliver inclusive disability services.


While states have direct responsibility to eliminate gender-based violence committed by state actors—such as government officials, military personnel, or legal professionals—they must also exercise due diligence in preventing and responding to private acts of gender-based violence. This requires states to address gender-based violence through laws and policies, and also to:


  • Prevent

This requires states to provide adequate, timely, and accessible services for survivors of gender-based violence to prevent further harm.


  • Investigate, prosecute, and punish

This requires States to take allegations of violence against women with disabilities seriously and to take all measures to fully investigate and address acts of violence against them. In addition, States must ensure that penalties imposed are commensurate with the severity of the violence.


  • Remedies

This requires States to adopt a holistic response to experiences of violence, using all available resources to promote the physical, cognitive, and mental health of persons with disabilities who have experienced gender-based violence, and to support their reintegration into society.



Three

Protecting women with disabilities from gender-based violence


  •  Women with disabilities must be able to live free from gender-based violence.


  • Laws, policies, and related programs must recognize and address the specific and multiple forms of gender-based violence faced by women with disabilities. Furthermore, States should involve women with disabilities in the design, implementation, and monitoring of these measures. States should also collect disaggregated data on incidents of violence against women with disabilities to inform the development of more targeted policies and programs.


  • Campaigns against gender-based violence targeting women with disabilities must recognize and challenge the myths, stigma, and stereotypes directed at women with disabilities.


  • To identify and respond to cases of violence against women with disabilities, law enforcement, the judiciary, healthcare providers, and other personnel must receive training.


  • Health care and shelter services must be made available to women with disabilities and be accessible to them. At the same time, contact information for these services must be provided in multiple formats to ensure accessibility for women with disabilities.
