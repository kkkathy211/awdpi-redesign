Preface

On the journey of studying abroad, disability is not always visible. It may be the morning when depression makes it hard to get out of bed, the sudden mental blank during an exam caused by anxiety, or the extra effort—many times that of others—required due to differences in reading, writing, or attention. As the population of international students grows, these visible and invisible physical and mental challenges are becoming increasingly recognized within overseas campuses and workplaces. They include not only physical and sensory impairments, but also highly prevalent mental health conditions (such as depression and anxiety) and neurodiverse conditions (such as attention deficit hyperactivity disorder and dyslexia) among international students.


However, due to cultural influences, many Asian students often carry a heavy burden of stigma when facing physical or mental challenges. They fear being seen as "not good enough," worry about "being a burden to the school," or "shaming the family," and thus hesitate to acknowledge their situation or seek help. We have created this handbook to help you shed this burden. Requesting reasonable accommodations is never a privilege—it is about securing equal opportunities to participate in learning, work, and life. We also hope to support your transformation: from silently enduring and passively receiving help, to actively understanding and claiming your rights, learning to identify discrimination, and having the confidence to confront it.


Disability issues are inherently complex and diverse, involving various types of impairments, legal systems, and institutional arrangements, making it impossible for any single handbook to cover everything comprehensively. Therefore, this handbook focuses on common study destinations for Asian international students, primarily following the US disability rights framework, centered on the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 (ADA) and Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973, with key comparisons to the UK's Equality Act 2010. The latter applies to England, Scotland, and Wales, though legal jurisdictions and complaint bodies differ by region. The UK sections in this handbook are based on the laws of England and Wales. This focus does not mean excluding readers in other countries or regions. Core principles of disability protection—such as the prohibition of discrimination, the duty to provide reasonable accommodations, and protection against retaliation—as well as the basic framework of documentation and complaints, are common across many rule-of-law jurisdictions. However, specific agency names, application procedures, and their interplay with visa status may vary. Therefore, if you are studying in Canada, Australia, or the EU, the advocacy strategies and pathways outlined in this handbook are still valuable references. But before taking specific actions, please verify them with your university's Disability Office / Student Support services and local legal resources.


This handbook will sequentially introduce: definitions and main types of disability and disability-based discrimination; how to identify disability discrimination in daily life; on- and off-campus responses and remedies; and potential risks and coping strategies during the advocacy process. A list of UK and US on- and off-campus resources is also included at the end. No matter where you are in your journey, you can turn directly to the chapter most relevant to your situation.

Definition and Types of Disability and Disability-Based Discrimination


1.1

Definition of Persons with Disabilities and Major Types of Disabilities



According to the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, persons with disabilities include those who have long-term physical, mental, intellectual, or sensory impairments which, in interaction with various barriers, may hinder their full and effective participation in society on an equal basis with others.


In addition, national laws often provide more detailed definitions. For example, the ADA includes three categories of individuals as persons with disabilities: those with a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities; those with a record of such an impairment; and those who are regarded as having such an impairment. "Major life activities" include tasks such as caring for oneself, walking, learning, reading, thinking, and working, as well as the functioning of major bodily systems such as the immune, neurological, and brain systems. The UK's Equality Act 2010 defines disability as a physical or mental impairment that has a substantial and long-term adverse effect on a person's ability to carry out normal day-to-day activities.


>>Click to expand<<



Disabilities are classified in various ways, including by medical pathology, legal criteria, or visibility, and specific classifications depend on the laws and regulations of the individual country. For example, the U.S. Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (hereinafter "IDEA") lists 14 types of disabilities (with "developmental delay" applicable only to young children, see table below). It should be noted that IDEA primarily applies to public education in the United States from preschool through 12th grade and does not directly extend to higher education. For international students studying at U.S. colleges and universities, the primary applicable laws are the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) and Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973. These two laws use a functional definition rather than listing specific categories. However, the above classification under IDEA still serves as a useful reference for readers to understand common types of disabilities. Additionally, according to the implementing regulations issued by the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (hereinafter "EEOC"), the following conditions are typically considered to constitute a disability: amputation, autism, bipolar disorder, blindness, cancer, cerebral palsy, deafness, diabetes, epilepsy, HIV or AIDS, intellectual disability, major depressive disorder, mobility impairments requiring wheelchair use, multiple sclerosis, muscular dystrophy, obsessive-compulsive disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder, and schizophrenia. This list is intended to simplify identification; conditions not listed are not necessarily unprotected. Conditions such as ADHD and dyslexia may still qualify as disabilities, provided that in individual cases they can be shown to substantially limit major life activities.


Furthermore, the UK's Equality Act 2010 also adopts a similar functional definition, but its Schedule 1 and associated regulations directly list certain conditions as "automatically deemed disabilities," including: cancer (including pre-cancerous conditions), HIV infection, multiple sclerosis, and blindness or visual impairments certified by a registered ophthalmologist. Severe disfigurement is also generally considered a disability, and progressive conditions (such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's) are protected from the point at which they begin to affect daily activities. Compared to the EEOC's list, the UK's "automatic recognition" scope is narrower; other conditions (such as diabetes, depression, and ADHD) still require functional assessment for recognition.

1.2

Definition of Disability-Based Discrimination


According to the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, disability-based discrimination refers to any distinction, exclusion, or restriction based on disability which has the purpose or effect of impairing or nullifying the recognition, enjoyment, or exercise of human rights and fundamental freedoms on an equal basis with others in political, economic, social, cultural, civil, or any other field. Disability-based discrimination includes all forms of discrimination, such as the denial of reasonable accommodation.

How to Exercise Your Rights and Respond to Discrimination—On Campus


3.1

Proactive Protection: Applying for Accommodations



According to the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, reasonable accommodation means necessary and appropriate modification and adjustments, not imposing a disproportionate or undue burden, where needed in a particular case, to ensure that persons with disabilities can enjoy or exercise on an equal basis with others all human rights and fundamental freedoms.


In addition, countries often have more detailed provisions in their domestic laws. For example, the UK's Equality Act 2010 establishes a "duty to make reasonable adjustments," which requires institutions to take reasonable steps to avoid placing disabled individuals at a substantial disadvantage when their rules, criteria or practices, physical features, or lack of auxiliary aids put them at such a disadvantage (e.g., by modifying rules, removing physical barriers, or providing assistive devices), and the disabled person must not be required to bear any related costs. The U.S. Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) defines reasonable accommodations as including primarily modifying existing facilities used by employees to make them accessible and usable for people with disabilities, and adjusting work schedules and procedures—such as job restructuring, part-time work or modified work hours, reassignment to vacant positions, purchasing or modifying equipment, adjusting exams, training materials or policies, and providing qualified readers or interpreters.


Accommodations typically only apply to academic activities after approval and are not retroactive. This means the purpose of applying for accommodations is to ensure future academic performance, not to revise past results. Therefore, even if your current situation seems manageable, it is best to complete registration as early as possible to secure eligibility—being prepared but not needing it is far better than seeking remedies after the fact. If, after an exam or assignment submission, you request changes to prior grades, retakes, or resubmissions based on health conditions or failure to apply for accommodations, schools generally have the right to deny such requests under this principle.

(1) Medical Documentation and Disclosure


Medical documentation is the foundation for applying for reasonable accommodations. Disability Service Offices (DSOs) at universities are not medical institutions; they must rely on the medical documentation you provide to determine your eligibility. A document that can be directly accepted by DSOs in the UK and US is typically issued by a qualified professional (a doctor or psychologist) and must include the following three key elements:


a. Clear diagnosis (Diagnosis): It is best to include specific medical terminology or codes (such as DSM-5 or ICD-10 codes), rather than vague descriptions.


b. Functional limitations (Functional Limitations): This is the part most valued by DSOs. The doctor must not only state what condition you have, but also explain specifically how it hinders your academic activities.

  • Incorrect example: "The student feels anxious."

  • Correct example: "Due to generalized anxiety disorder, the student experiences mental freezing and panic attacks in high-pressure, timed exam environments, preventing them from demonstrating their true knowledge level."


c. Recommended accommodations (Recommendations): The supports the doctor clinically believes you need. For example: "Recommend 50% extended exam time," "Recommend a separate, low-distraction exam room."

  • Privacy protection note: Under laws such as the U.S. Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA), the UK Data Protection Act 2018, and the UK General Data Protection Regulation (UK GDPR), you are not required to disclose specific diagnoses or medical details to your professors. Your original medical documentation is submitted only to the DSO, which keeps it confidential; professors have no right to view it.

  • Cross-border diagnostic recognition issues: Many international students arrive abroad with diagnoses from hospitals in their home countries, only to be told they do not meet local requirements. To avoid having to undergo costly re-evaluations abroad (which can cost thousands of dollars), please pay close attention to the following details:

⩥ Translation and notarization:

    ∆ DIY translations not accepted: Schools typically do not accept self-translated medical records.

    ∆ Certified translation: You must provide a copy of the original document plus a certified translation. It is recommended to use an official notary office in China that handles foreign affairs for translation and notarization, or to use a certified translator abroad (e.g., from the American Translators Association or the Chartered Institute of Linguists in the UK).


⩥ Attention to timeliness:

    ∆ Mental health conditions (e.g., depression, anxiety): Due to the fluctuating nature of these conditions, schools usually require diagnostic documentation from within the past 6 months to 1 year.

    ∆ Learning disabilities or neurodiversity conditions (e.g., ADHD, dyslexia): Schools typically require comprehensive assessment reports from within the past 3 to 5 years.


⩥ Avoiding the risk of re-evaluation:

    ∆ UK and US universities have very high standards for diagnosing learning disabilities such as ADHD, often requiring a lengthy "psycho-educational evaluation report" of over ten pages, including IQ tests and cognitive assessments.

    ∆ Recommendation: If your documentation from your home country is outdated or lacks detail, it is strongly advised to obtain a new, comprehensive evaluation report in English from a qualified tertiary hospital or specialist institution in China before departure, one that meets UK/US standards. Otherwise, upon arrival, you may be required to undergo re-evaluation locally—a process that is not only time-consuming (with potential waitlists of several months) but also expensive, and may not be covered by insurance.


(2) Registration and intake


After preparing your materials, you must proactively contact your university's Disability Service Office (DSO) for an intake interview to determine a personalized list of accommodations (e.g., 1.5x exam time, separate exam room, note-taker, assignment deadline extensions, etc.).


a. Submit application: Complete the application form via the DSO's online system on the university website and upload your medical documentation.


b. Intake interview: This is a one-on-one meeting with your DSO advisor. The advisor will review your medical records but also values your personal account. Do not merely state your diagnosis; instead, clearly describe the functional limitations and the specific accommodations you are requesting. For example, instead of saying "I have depression," say "Due to morning lethargy caused by depression, I struggle to attend 8 a.m. classes—can I be given priority for afternoon classes?" or "Medication side effects cause hand tremors, making writing very slow—can I use a computer during exams?"


c. Finalize plan: After the interview, the advisor will approve an official list of accommodations.


(3) Notification and implementation


a. Obtain accommodation letter: The DSO system will generate an official letter listing all approved accommodations (e.g., Exam: 50% extended time), but it will not include your specific diagnosis—only the operational requirements.


b. Send email and confirm: It is recommended to send an email confirming your accommodations during the first week of each semester. Accommodations are typically not retroactive; presenting the accommodation letter after final exams usually means it is too late to make adjustments. For accommodations requiring professor cooperation (e.g., extended exam time), after sending the email, use office hours to confirm implementation details in person or via Zoom. For example: Where do I take my extended-time exam? At the university's exam center or through a separate arrangement by the professor?

3.2

When rights are obstructed: On-campus remedies


When disability rights are obstructed (e.g., a professor refuses to implement accommodations listed in the accommodation letter on grounds of academic standards; classmates exclude a disabled student from group work due to slow communication or the need for sign language interpretation, resulting in the student failing the group assignment; university administration delays approval, causing the student to miss the exam adjustment window), consider the following steps:



(1) Stage One: Preserve evidence, written confirmation


When a professor verbally refuses your accommodation request, the first step is to confirm and document it.


Immediately after the conversation, send an email converting the verbal refusal into a written paper trail. This will be crucial evidence if you later need to file a complaint.


Sample email templates are provided below:

Dear Professor [Name],


Thank you for meeting with me earlier today to discuss my DSO-approved accommodations. I truly appreciate the time you took to explain the course structure and your expectations.


I'm writing to ensure I have a clear understanding of our discussion. My takeaway was that you have concerns regarding the implementation of [Specific Accommodation], as you feel it might fundamentally alter the nature of the [e.g., timed assessment / participation].


Could you please confirm if this accurately reflects your view? I would like to consult with my DSO advisor to see if there are alternative tools or creative solutions that could support my medical needs without compromising the essential academic integrity of your course.


I value your expertise and hope we can find a path forward that works for both the department and the learning objectives.


Best regards,

[Your Name]

Note: In some U.S. states, recording conversations without consent is illegal. It is best to use email confirmation as evidence to avoid legal complications that could put you at a disadvantage.


(2) Stage Two: Intervention by DSO or DOS


This is the core of informal resolution. The Disability Service Office (DSO) holds administrative authority and compliance oversight. You do not need to debate the law with the professor yourself—let the DSO intervene.


Additionally, U.S. universities typically designate the Dean of Students Office (DOS), or its affiliated Student Care and Outreach / Case Management unit, as the first point of intervention and coordination. While naming may vary across institutions, the core function is to provide non-clinical resource coordination and administrative support through case managers. For example, Stanford University's DOS explicitly lists "responsive care and case management services" as one of its core responsibilities.


a. Contact your DSO advisor or DOS case manager


Forward the professor's refusal email and attach your request, emphasizing that the professor's refusal occurred without engaging in the interactive process and without offering alternative solutions.


b. Official intervention method (The Compliance Check)


The DSO advisor or DOS case manager will typically contact the professor directly to clarify that since the accommodation has been officially approved by the university, the professor must provide a valid justification for non-compliance—such as the accommodation fundamentally altering the nature of the course (fundamental alteration). Otherwise, the university faces compliance and legal risks.


(3) Stage Three: Tripartite meeting


If the professor continues to refuse after official intervention, a tripartite meeting involving the student, professor, and DSO advisor (or DOS representative) is usually arranged. For example:

  • Student: Present functional limitations, not demands for privilege. For example: "I process auditory information slowly and need recordings for review."

  • Professor: Express academic concerns. For example: "Recordings could leak exclusive case analysis content."

  • DSO / DOS (mediator / decision-maker): Propose a mutually acceptable solution. For example: "If the student signs a Lecture Recording Agreement, promising to use recordings only for personal review, never to share them, and to destroy them after finals, would you accept this, Professor?"


(4) Stage Four: Ombuds—Alternative path


If intervention proves ineffective, or you feel the professor is rejecting you due to personal bias rather than academic standards, communication has reached an impasse, emotions are running high on both sides, or you fear retaliation after filing a complaint and would prefer someone to mediate behind the scenes, you may seek assistance from an ombudsperson on campus. As a neutral third party, the ombudsperson does not represent the university or the student, but stands for fair process. However, since ombudspersons have no enforcement power, they cannot order a professor to change a grade—they can only make recommendations.


Departments such as DSO are administrative bodies representing the university, and all communications and decisions made with them are formally documented ("on the record") and become part of the university's official records. They have the authority to require professors to implement legally mandated reasonable accommodations, with the baseline goal of ensuring the university complies with federal law and avoids litigation risk. In contrast, communication with an ombudsperson is informal and confidential ("off the record"). Ombudspersons operate independently of departmental interests, lack coercive authority, and can only exert influence through neutral advice and by highlighting compliance risks.


It should be noted that on-campus ombudspersons are a common feature at U.S. universities, but are typically not present at UK institutions, where independent review is primarily handled by external bodies. In the UK, if you encounter similar situations, you should first seek help from your Students' Union Advice Service. External avenues are covered in Part 5 of this handbook.

When informal interactions break down, or when you experience serious discrimination or harassment, students may consider initiating a formal internal administrative remedy process.


(1) Complaint and Reporting


  • Formal Complaint Channel (Formal Complaint)

    This is the strongest option. Once a complaint is submitted, the university will launch a comprehensive investigation, and the final outcome is binding. It can support individual remedies such as grade changes, re-examinations, or removal of disciplinary records. Under the principle of due process, the respondent usually has the right to know the complainant's identity and the content of the complaint.

  • Anonymous Reporting Mechanism (Anonymous Reporting)

    This option is weaker and is typically used only to provide leads. Because the respondent has the right to confront their accuser, anonymous reports generally cannot change your personal grades or situation, and at most may prompt the university to initiate general corrective actions.

  • Third-Party Proxy Complaint (Third-Party Reporting)

    You may authorize a third party—such as a Students' Union disability representative or student rights advisor—to submit the complaint materials on your behalf. This is suitable for students who, due to psychological trauma, are unable or unwilling to participate directly in the investigation.


(2) Investigation and Resolution Process


After a formal complaint is submitted, the university will initiate a quasi-judicial internal administrative investigation.


a. First Stage: Initial Assessment


The investigator determines whether the allegations fall under the jurisdiction of the ADA or Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973. If so, the case will be formally opened and the respondent will be notified.


For international students, an important protective mechanism during the investigation is interim measures.


The investigation process typically lasts 60 to 90 days. International students may apply for interim measures during this period. For example: having an incomplete course marked as "Incomplete" instead of "F" until the investigation concludes, to avoid jeopardizing visa status; or requesting a class transfer to avoid contact with the involved faculty member.


b. Second Stage: Fact-Finding


The investigator conducts separate interviews with the student, professor, and any witnesses, and may review emails, assignment feedback, and syllabi. Some universities may hold a hearing, during which both parties can cross-examine each other (through advisors).


c. Third Stage: Standard of Evidence


Campus appeals use the preponderance of the evidence standard: if the likelihood of discrimination or harassment occurring exceeds 50%, the student's claim is considered substantiated.

(1) U.S. Section: Medical Reduced Course Load (Medical RCL)


In the U.S., F-1 students who are unable to maintain full-time enrollment due to temporary physical illness or mental health conditions may apply for a Medical Reduced Course Load (Medical RCL). With prior approval from a Designated School Official (DSO—not the same office as the disability services office), students may reduce their course credits; if necessary, they may even enroll in zero credits for a specific term. During the approved period, students may maintain valid F-1 status, and their SEVIS records typically remain active. A maximum of 12 months of Medical RCL may be used cumulatively per degree level (e.g., during a master's program).

[Note] It should be noted that the U.S. Department of Homeland Security issued a final rule on July 17, 2026, which was scheduled to take effect on September 15, 2026, eliminating the "Duration of Status" (D/S) policy for F-1 students and replacing it with a fixed "Admit Until Date" on the I-94, with a maximum stay of four years. Subsequently, university associations and labor unions filed lawsuits, and on September 14, 2026, the U.S. District Court for the District of Massachusetts issued a nationwide preliminary injunction halting the rule's implementation pending litigation. As a result, the D/S policy currently remains in effect. This ruling is a preliminary remedy during litigation, not a final judgment. The case is ongoing, and the government may appeal. Whether and when the rule will take effect remains uncertain. Please closely monitor the latest developments. If the rule eventually takes effect, it will have three direct impacts on students seeking reduced course load or leave of absence due to illness.


First, if medical reasons prevent completion of the program within the "Admit Until Date" on the I-94, prior approval from the DSO and SEVIS updates alone will no longer be sufficient. Students must file Form I-539 (Application to Extend/Change Nonimmigrant Status) with U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) before the I-94 expires. "Documented illness or medical condition" is a recognized reason under the rule, but final approval rests with USCIS.


Second, unlawful presence begins to accrue the day after the "Admit Until Date" on the I-94 expires, without requiring a formal determination.


Third, the grace period for departure after program or Optional Practical Training (OPT) completion is reduced from 60 days to 30 days. Students who entered under the D/S policy before September 15, 2026, and were still in the U.S. on that date are eligible for a transitional arrangement: they may remain until the later of the program end date on their I-20 or the expiration of their OPT/STEM OPT employment authorization, but no later than November 14, 2030. During this period, the 60-day grace period still applies. However, upon re-entry after departure or after USCIS-approved extension, they will be subject to the new rules. Students considering reduced course load or leave of absence due to illness must confirm the current status of this rule with their international student office, verify their I-94 expiration date, and determine whether a separate extension application is required.

Students must submit medical documentation issued by a licensed U.S. professional: a Medical Doctor (M.D.), Doctor of Osteopathic Medicine (D.O.), or a Licensed Clinical Psychologist. Documentation from general counselors, therapists, nurses, or traditional Chinese medicine practitioners typically does not meet the Medical RCL requirements unless the provider also holds one of the above-mentioned licenses.

  • Application Process:

    1. Contact your school's international student office.

    Contact ISSS, OISS, or the Visa Team before dropping courses to confirm the school's Medical RCL application form, required medical documentation format, and submission deadlines.


    2. Obtain qualifying medical documentation.

    The medical documentation should confirm the student's physical or mental health condition and support the recommendation to reduce course load or temporarily suspend studies.


    3. Submit application materials to the Designated School Official.

    Students must submit the Medical RCL application form and current medical documentation as required by the school.


    4. Wait for the Designated School Official to complete approval and update SEVIS.

    The Designated School Official must enter the authorization reason, authorization date, and next term start date into SEVIS before the student actually reduces their course load.


    5. Drop courses only after approval is granted.

    Only after receiving explicit approval from the Designated School Official may students reduce their course load below full-time status or enroll in zero credits as permitted.


    6. Reapply each new term.

    Even if the medical condition persists, students are typically required to submit updated medical documentation and obtain renewed authorization from the Designated School Official for each new semester, quarter, or academic term.


(2) UK Section: Authorized Absence and Suspension of Studies


UK universities, as licensed student sponsors, are required to monitor the attendance and academic engagement of student visa holders. When students are unable to study normally due to health reasons, there are typically two options:


  • Option A: Authorized Absence

    • Applicable when: short-term illness, and absence will not affect timely program completion or exceed the current visa validity.

    • Application method: Submit a Fit Note, medical letter, or other medical documentation to your academic department or international student office as required by the university.

    • Immigration impact: If the university approves the absence and continues sponsorship, the student visa is generally unaffected, and the student may remain in the UK.


  • Option B: Suspension of Studies

    • Applicable when: illness is severe and requires a prolonged break from studies, such as one semester or one academic year. The university may continue sponsorship for suspensions of up to 60 days, provided the student can still complete the program within the existing visa validity upon return. For suspensions exceeding 60 days, the university is generally expected to withdraw sponsorship; however, in exceptional cases such as serious illness or injury, and if the student can still complete the program within the current visa period, the university may exercise discretion to maintain sponsorship.

    • Immigration impact: Once sponsorship is withdrawn, the university must report this to UKVI. UKVI may cancel the student's remaining leave and notify the student of the termination date via a Cancellation Notice. Students do not automatically have a 60-day grace period; the date specified in the UKVI notice must be followed. Students are generally required to leave the UK as instructed. Upon recovery and return to studies, the university will issue a new CAS, and the student must reapply for a student visa.

How to Exercise Your Rights and Respond to Discrimination—Internship and Job Search Edition


When applying for internships or full-time positions, job seekers and workers with disabilities are also protected by local anti-discrimination laws. These protections typically cover recruitment, hiring, employment management, promotion, and termination.


4.1

Workplace Disability Rights and Scope of Legal Protection



(1) Competence Principle

Applicants must possess the skills, experience, and qualifications required for the position and be able to perform the essential functions of the role with reasonable accommodation. The law does not require employers to lower fundamental job requirements, but prohibits employers from imposing barriers unrelated to the job due to disability.


(2) Principle of Reasonable Accommodation

Employers should adjust recruitment processes, work arrangements, or the work environment according to actual needs, unless such measures would cause what the law defines as undue hardship.


(3) Privacy and Non-Disclosure Rights

Prior to receiving a job offer, employers are strictly prohibited from inquiring about an applicant's health status or disability details. Applicants have the right to choose when, where, and to whom they disclose such information.


>>Click to collapse<<



(1) Scope of Application


Employer size: Federal anti-discrimination laws (such as Title I of the Americans with Disabilities Act, ADA) apply to private employers, state/local governments, employment agencies, and labor unions with 15 or more employees. (Note: Most companies capable of sponsoring H-1B visas meet this threshold.)


Covered stages: Full-process coverage, including recruitment, interviews, pre-employment medical exams, internships, full-time employment, and promotions/terminations. Specifically, for interns:

  • Paid interns: As long as they receive compensation (regardless of whether during CPT or OPT), they are legally considered bona fide employees under U.S. federal law and are fully protected under Title I of the ADA.

  • Unpaid interns: If the internship is purely unpaid and conducted for academic credit (CPT), due to the lack of substantial remuneration, they are generally not covered under federal ADA Title I. However, be sure to monitor local state laws. To fill gaps in federal law, an increasing number of states have enacted specific legislation explicitly granting unpaid interns the same anti-discrimination rights and eligibility to request reasonable accommodation as regular employees. State and local workplace anti-discrimination laws are often stricter and more detailed than federal laws.


(2) Protection Mechanisms


First, essential function protection: Employers cannot reject you because you are unable to perform non-essential or marginal functions. For example, if a law firm's paralegal position requires "ability to carry 20-pound file boxes," and you cannot carry them due to using a wheelchair, but can perform core duties such as legal research and document drafting, the employer must either exempt you from carrying or arrange for someone else to do so, and cannot reject your application on that basis.


Second, the interactive process: This refers to an ongoing, two-way communication and negotiation between employee and employer. Once you make a request for reasonable accommodation, the employer is obligated to initiate this interactive process. For large law firms or leading tech companies, it is typically very difficult for employers to refuse to provide assistive tools such as screen-reading software on grounds of excessive cost.


Third, pre-employment medical inquiry prohibition: Before issuing a conditional job offer, human resources departments are strictly prohibited from asking about disability status, frequency of past sick leave, or other health-related questions. For example, questions such as "Do you have a disability?" or "How many sick days did you take last year?" are considered illegal inquiries.

(1) Scope of Application

  • No size limit: Unlike the U.S., the UK's Equality Act 2010 applies to all employers.

  • Protected workers: Includes all employees, workers, contractors, interns, and job applicants.

  • Disability definition: Legal protection applies if a physical or mental impairment has a substantial and long-term (already lasting or expected to last 12 months or more) adverse effect on the person's ability to carry out normal day-to-day activities.


(2) Protection Mechanisms

  • Duty to make reasonable adjustments: Employers are required to take reasonable steps to prevent disabled individuals from being placed at a substantial disadvantage in recruitment or employment, including:

a. Adjusting policies or working methods: For example, allowing flexible working hours or remote work.

    b. Improving the physical environment: For example, installing ramps or improving lighting.      

        c. Providing auxiliary equipment or services: For example, specialized keyboards or sign language interpreters.

  • Access to Work scheme: Access to Work is a UK government employment support program that can fund specialist equipment, assistive software, support workers, and additional travel costs arising from disability. This scheme does not replace the employer's duty to make reasonable adjustments, nor does it guarantee full cost coverage.


Individuals legally residing and working in England, Scotland, or Wales who are engaged in or about to start eligible paid work may apply for this support. Those holding a Graduate Route or Skilled Worker Visa may qualify, but eligibility depends on the nature of the work and individual circumstances. Ordinary unpaid internships and volunteer work are typically not covered.

Reasonable Accommodation in Recruitment and Employment



4.3

Disclosure Strategy and Timing


Legally, job applicants or employees are under no obligation to voluntarily disclose a disability to an employer. However, disclosure of necessary information is advisable if the condition may pose a substantial risk to personal or others' workplace safety, or if reasonable accommodation is needed. If you can safely perform the essential functions of the job without any adjustments, disclosure is generally optional.



4.4

Intersection of International Student Status and Disability Rights


Disability rights are not affected in principle by nationality or international student status, but when exercising these rights, visa and immigration requirements must still be taken into account.


Note:

1. In large multinational companies, reasonable accommodation is typically handled by the Employee Relations/DEI team, while visa matters are managed by the Immigration/Legal team. These two systems are usually not connected. Do not refrain from seeking accommodation from the DEI team out of visa-related concerns.

2. Always send an email to HR to create a written record before making any adjustments to working hours.

3. If you face complex H-1B working hour adjustments, consult an immigration lawyer experienced in employment law to maximize your rights. Company immigration lawyers represent the company's interests, not yours.



 NO.5 

How to Exercise Rights and Respond to Discrimination—Other Channels


5.1

Administrative Complaints


When internal grievance procedures at school or work fail to resolve the issue, or when you believe the internal investigation was biased, you have the right to file a complaint with external government regulatory bodies or non-governmental organizations.

  • Key Advantages:

  • Zero cost: Most administrative complaints do not charge any filing fees.

  • Neutrality: Investigators are appointed by the government and have no conflict of interest with the school/employer.

  • Enforcement power: After confirming discrimination, U.S. federal agencies can require institutions to sign corrective action agreements and, if necessary, initiate procedures to withdraw federal funding; UK ombudsman bodies primarily issue recommendations, which, while not legally binding, are typically followed by institutions.



The U.S. administrative complaint system has strong enforcement characteristics. If a school violates the ADA or Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973, it is effectively challenging federal law.


(1) Office for Civil Rights (OCR), U.S. Department of Education


Applies to all educational institutions receiving federal funding—this includes nearly all public and private universities. OCR investigates whether schools have violated Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 or Title II of the ADA. Complaints must be filed within 180 days of the discriminatory act. If you first pursue internal procedures, you must file with OCR within 60 days of the conclusion of the internal process. OCR does not represent you in seeking large compensatory damages, but it can compel the school to sign a Resolution Agreement—for example, revising unreasonable leave policies, mandating faculty training, or reinstating your enrollment.


(2) Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC)—For Internships and Employment


Applies to employment discrimination against employers (including universities when acting as employers, such as for RA/TA positions). Before suing an employer, you must first file a Charge of Discrimination with the EEOC. The EEOC will conduct an investigation or mediation. If mediation fails, the EEOC will issue a "Right to Sue" letter, which is required before you can proceed to court.


(3) Department of Justice (DOJ)


Primarily handles cases involving public universities or state government agencies violating Title II of the ADA, or cases involving broad public interest—such as an entire campus having non-compliant buildings. DOJ-involved cases often carry precedent-setting significance.


>>Click to collapse<<



The UK system places greater emphasis on dispute resolution and independent adjudication. If students are dissatisfied with the outcome of internal university investigations, they may file an external appeal with an independent body after exhausting internal procedures. The responsible agency varies by region: in England and Wales, appeals are handled by the Office of the Independent Adjudicator for Higher Education (OIA); in Scotland, by the Scottish Public Services Ombudsman (SPSO), which serves as the final stage for complaints at Scottish universities; in Northern Ireland, by the Northern Ireland Public Services Ombudsman (NIPSO) under the Public Services Ombudsman Act (Northern Ireland) 2016. None of these bodies re-evaluate academic judgments—such as grades—but instead review whether institutions followed their own rules and whether procedures were fair. The following discussion on higher education appeals will use the OIA as an example.


(1) Office of the Independent Adjudicator for Higher Education (OIA)—Final Arbiter in Higher Education


Applies to higher education providers in England and Wales that are legally enrolled in the scheme—most universities are included. You must first complete all internal university complaint procedures and obtain a "Completion of Procedures" (COP) letter. You must submit your appeal to the OIA within 12 months of receiving the COP letter. The OIA may issue recommendations. Although technically non-binding, universities almost always comply. Common outcomes include re-marking of assignments, uncapped resit opportunities, and compensation for distress and inconvenience (typically £250–£5,000).


(2) ACAS (Advisory, Conciliation and Arbitration Service)—Mandatory Step for Workplace Disputes


Applies to discrimination disputes against employers (including internships). Before filing a claim at an Employment Tribunal, the law requires you to first contact ACAS for Early Conciliation. ACAS mediators will communicate between you and your employer to attempt a settlement (Settlement Agreement) and avoid litigation. ACAS covers England, Scotland, and Wales; in Northern Ireland, the equivalent body is the Labour Relations Agency (LRA), with cases heard by the Industrial Tribunal.


(3) Equality and Human Rights Commission (EHRC)


Although it does not directly handle all individual complaints, EHRC may provide legal funding or directly intervene in cases of strategic significance (Strategic Litigation).

5.2

Using social media and public opinion for rights protection


This is a high-risk, high-reward non-traditional path for rights protection, and should only be initiated when all formal channels (on-campus DSO, appeals, external OCR/OIA) have failed and you have clearly suffered unjust treatment.



Universities, especially private elite institutions, highly value their reputation. When internal bureaucratic systems engage in mutual buck-passing, external public attention often breaks the deadlock. Adopting a strategy of public pressure is not about emotional venting, but about increasing your negotiation leverage.



>>Click to collapse<<



(1) State only facts


  • Wrong: "This professor is a disabled-discriminating pervert who deliberately failed me!" (Opinions/insults are easily actionable for defamation)

  • Correct: "Despite holding an approved accommodation letter from DSO, Professor X refused to provide extended exam time on date Y, stating 'no special treatment will be given.' This prevented me from completing the exam." (This states verifiable, objective facts)


(2) Pay attention to anonymity protection


As an international student, it is recommended to post via anonymous confession pages (Confession Pages) or burner accounts, and avoid disclosing your student ID or real name unless you are fully prepared for an open, named campaign.


(3) De-identify evidence


When posting email screenshots, always redact the other party's personal phone number, private address, and other private information to avoid accusations of doxxing. It is acceptable to retain their work email and job title, as these relate to official duties.

5.3

Taking legal action



Before formally filing a lawsuit, you need a professional legal assessment. Do not attempt to face the school or employer's legal team alone. Below are pathways to seek help, listed from lowest to highest cost:


(1) Pro bono resources

Although wait times may be long, these services are completely free and staffed by experienced professionals.

  • Law school legal clinics:

    Many law schools (e.g., Harvard, Yale, UCL) operate clinics supervised by professors and run by senior law students. These are free and deeply familiar with the internal logic of education systems. Note: Avoid clinics at your own university (due to potential conflicts of interest); instead, seek clinics at other universities in the same city.

  • Disability rights advocacy organizations:

    • US: Each state has a federally funded Protection and Advocacy (P&A) system (e.g., Disability Rights California), which has statutory investigative authority and specializes in ADA cases.

    • UK: Disability Law Service or Law Centres, which provide free legal advice specifically for low-income individuals.


(2) Contingency fee arrangements and lawyer referrals


If pro bono resources are unavailable, you may seek private lawyers, but should control costs. You can search the website of local bar associations (e.g., New York City Bar Association); many offer low-cost 30-minute consultations (typically $25–$50) for initial case evaluation.


Fee structure options:

  • a. Hourly rate: Extremely expensive; avoid if possible.

  • b. Contingency fee (No Win, No Fee): "No payment unless you win." The lawyer covers upfront costs and takes 30%–40% of any compensation if successful. Lawyers are only likely to accept such cases if there is clear potential for financial damages.

  • c. UK-specific: Conditional Fee Agreement (CFA). You pay no legal fees if you lose, but may be liable for the other side's legal costs (insurance can mitigate this risk).


(3) Preparation before consultation


To maximize the effectiveness of free consultations, prepare the following materials:


a. Chronology of events: List all incidents in reverse chronological order (date – event – evidence). (Example: 2026.01.15 – Sent email requesting extension – Professor replied with refusal – Attachment 1)

b. Key evidence: Print out the 3–5 most critical emails, doctor's notes, transcripts/dismissal letters. Do not hand the lawyer a disorganized pile of documents.

c. Clear demands: What exactly do you want? (Compensation? Reinstatement? An apology?)


>>Click to collapse<<



Legal litigation should be a last resort. Under common law systems, lawsuits often mean a complete breakdown of relationships, are time-consuming, costly, and public. Only consider this path when you have suffered significant financial loss, physical or mental harm, or severe injustice, and all internal/administrative remedies have failed.


(1) Pre-litigation stage: Letter of demand (The Demand Letter / Letter Before Action)


Your lawyer will send a strongly worded letter to the school/employer, listing evidence of their legal violations and specifying settlement demands—e.g., compensation, grade correction, public apology. Many institutions, to avoid costly litigation and reputational damage, will have their legal department initiate out-of-court settlements upon receiving such a letter.


(2) Formal filing and service


If the other party refuses to settle, your lawyer will formally file a complaint (Complaint / Claim Form) with the court. Jurisdiction and statute of limitations vary by country and type of discrimination. Below are examples from the US and UK:

It is particularly important to note that for ongoing acts of discrimination (Continuing acts of discrimination), the statute of limitations typically begins from the date of the most recent discriminatory act. Additionally, in practice, courts may extend the limitation period due to the continuing nature of the discrimination or specific factors such as the claimant's status as a minor. Given the high legal complexity of calculating limitation periods, the precise deadline for filing in any specific case must be carefully assessed by a qualified lawyer.


(3) Discovery/disclosure phase


Both parties must exchange all relevant evidence. The school has the right to demand your full medical records, private diaries, and even social media chat logs. You must be mentally prepared for complete exposure of your privacy. You also have the right to demand internal emails between faculty members, such as those mocking you. Many schools choose to settle at this stage to avoid scandal.


(4) Trial and judgment


Very few cases reach this stage. A judge or jury delivers the final verdict.

 NO.6 

Risks involved in exercising rights and responding to discrimination, and corresponding coping strategies


6.1

Potential risks and coping strategies



6.2

Evidence collection guide


  1. Regardless of whether you decide to file a formal complaint, developing good documentation habits is the best shield for self-protection.

  2. Keep medical records: Retain all original diagnosis documents and doctors' accommodation recommendation letters.

  3. Maintain a communication log:

    (1) Email archiving: Create a dedicated folder to store all email exchanges with DSO, professors, and counselors, especially those containing refusals or evasions.

    (2) Document verbal conversations: If a professor refuses to respond in writing and only agrees to a face-to-face meeting, immediately send a follow-up email afterward: "Dear Professor, thank you for today's meeting. To avoid misunderstandings, I would like to confirm that our discussion concluded with… (list the refusal). Please correct me if I have misunderstood."

  4. Record details of violations: For environmental barriers (e.g., broken elevators) or verbal harassment, document the exact time, location, witnesses present, and content of the conversation. If possible, take photos or make recordings, but be mindful of local laws regarding recording.


6.3

Comprehensive recommendations


  • Safety first: In cases of intense conflict or harassment, prioritize personal safety, avoid direct confrontation, and file formal complaints afterward through proper channels.

  • Leverage third parties: When communication with a professor reaches a deadlock, do not try to persuade them alone. DSO advisors are familiar with institutional procedures and can intervene to shift the issue from personal conflict to a systemic matter.

  • Prioritize mental health: Rights advocacy is a long-term battle that can be extremely draining. Seek psychological counseling support simultaneously to avoid secondary trauma caused by the advocacy process itself.



As globalization continues to advance, Asian international students pursuing education abroad face not only cross-cultural challenges but also the dual burden of mental and physical health issues and systemic barriers. For students with disabilities, cultural stigma around illness and a lack of institutional accessibility often create invisible barriers that are harder to overcome than overt discrimination.


Disability is not an individual misfortune or defect, but a manifestation of human diversity. In major study-abroad countries like the US and UK, accessing reasonable accommodations and barrier-free support is not merely essential for academic and professional success—it is a civil right guaranteed by law, not a charitable favor bestowed by schools or employers. Asian international students can transform passive endurance into active rights protection by breaking the silence, practicing self-advocacy, and fully utilizing legal tools and on- and off-campus resources.


We hope this handbook can provide essential support and guidance to Asian international students, bridge information gaps, eliminate stigma, and ensure no one feels alone when facing mental or physical challenges. No matter where or when, remember: the starting line for pursuing equality is your rightful entitlement. Each of us should bravely speak up—through every application for reasonable accommodation and every act of resistance against injustice—not only to defend our own dignity but also to tear down barriers for those who come after us, collectively building a more inclusive and accessible international society.
