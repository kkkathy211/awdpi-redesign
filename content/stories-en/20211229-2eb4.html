Part 01. Introduction



November 25 is the International Day for the Elimination of Violence against Women, established by the United Nations, also known as "International Day for the Elimination of Domestic Violence."

 

On November 25, 1960, political activists Patria, Minerva, and María Teresa Mirabal from the Dominican Republic were brutally persecuted. In July 1981, to honor the sacrifice of the Mirabal sisters, the first Latin American Feminist Congress declared November 25 as a day against violence each year. On November 3, 1999, the United Nations General Assembly officially designated November 25 as the International Day for the Elimination of Violence against Women, calling on governments, international organizations, and non-governmental organizations to hold related activities on this day to raise public awareness of this issue.

 

To this day, gender-based violence persists across physical, psychological, and social dimensions, and domestic violence incidents occur frequently around the world. Especially during the pandemic, domestic violence victims have been forced to stay in confined spaces with their abusers for extended periods, and limited communication with the outside world has made it harder to seek help, causing the problem of domestic violence to intensify..

 

In previous Avoice public education posts, we introduced the origins of the International Day against Domestic Violence, reviewed historical anti-domestic violence campaigns, defined domestic violence, discussed victims' responses, analyzed domestic violence from a psychological perspective, and examined the situation during the pandemic. This issue covers additional aspects of domestic violence, including victims beyond marital relationships, the role of bystanders, and an outlook on current urgent issues in the field of domestic violence and the topic of gender equality.




Part 02. Main Body



(I)

Other Forms of Domestic Violence Beyond Marital Relationships


Domestic violence can take various forms, including physical violence, emotional violence, sexual violence, and economic control, and anyone can become a victim of domestic violence.

 

Previous articles discussed domestic violence between married partners. In addition, this article turns attention to other victims of domestic violence—children and the elderly within families, unmarried heterosexual or same-sex cohabiting partners, and even tenants living under the same roof. (Note: Definitions of domestic violence vary across countries and regions; this will be explained in detail later.)

 

First, children and the elderly may also become victims of physical violence, psychological violence, economic control, and even sexual violence within the family. Therefore, in a significant number of countries and regions around the world, violence against children and the elderly within the family is also categorized as domestic violence.

 

The issue of children experiencing violence within the family cannot be overlooked. Research shows that domestic violence severely harms children's physical and mental development, increasing their likelihood of developing psychological and physical illnesses. Traumatic experiences can leave these children feeling insecure, emotionally unstable, and depressed. Moreover, domestic violence negatively affects children's emotional regulation, social skills, behavior patterns, and cognitive development. Many children become more aggressive, more prone to anxiety, and less capable of problem-solving compared to their peers. In addition, children are also highly vulnerable to becoming indirect victims of domestic violence—even if children are not directly targeted by violence, when they witness domestic violence, they may attempt to intervene, thereby exposing themselves to serious injury or even death. Psychologically, children who witness domestic violence often blame themselves, living in endless guilt and fear.

 

Besides children, elderly family members may also become both perpetrators and victims of domestic violence. Such incidents similarly cause varying degrees of physical and psychological harm to victims and should receive sufficient attention from public discourse and the judicial system. Victims should calmly recognize their situation and actively use social resources and legal avenues to protect themselves and uphold their basic human rights.

 

As previously mentioned, definitions of domestic violence vary across countries and regions. For example, in Connecticut, USA, domestic violence is not limited to legally defined family relationships; violent incidents between unmarried cohabiting same-sex or heterosexual partners, or even roommates living under the same roof, are also classified as domestic violence. Among these, domestic violence between same-sex partners is often overlooked by mainstream discourse. In fact, domestic violence not only occurs in same-sex relationships, but the prevalence is not lower than in heterosexual relationships. According to the 2011 National Intimate Partner and Sexual Violence Survey published by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 44% of lesbian women, 61% of bisexual women, and 35% of heterosexual women experienced at least one incident of domestic violence in their lifetime; the survey also showed that 26% of gay men, 37% of bisexual men, and 29% of heterosexual men had been victims of domestic violence. A 1997 study pointed out additional discrimination and fear experienced by LGBTQ+ individuals in domestic violence situations, including potential neglect by police and social service agencies, lack of peer support among same-sex partners, fear of stigma within the LGBTQ+ community, and threats of forced outing.



(II)


 What Can Bystanders/Ordinary People Do?



3.1 If You Are a Witness


3.1.1

Call the police. Because domestic violence is often private and occurs in secluded spaces, it is rarely witnessed directly. If you do witness it, please help by calling the police.

 

3.1.2 

Additionally, as a neighbor, your actions can have a limited deterrent effect on the abuser. For example, if you hear domestic violence happening in a neighboring home, you can walk over and knock on the door, asking, "Is everything okay?" This may make the abuser aware that someone has noticed their behavior, potentially causing them to stop or reduce their actions.


3.2 If You Are a Family Member or Friend of the Victim


In the past decade, family members offering effective support to victims has not been optimistic. Most victims who sought help from family did not receive strong support and may have even suffered secondary harm.


from their own family members. So, how can family and friends help the victim?



Support Methods

 

Reasons

 

 

Listening to the victim's narrative to assess whether she is safe. When necessary, assist the victim in calling the police.

 

 

Sometimes, domestic violence victims only share minor details of their lives without realizing they are in danger or fully understanding the risks of domestic violence.

 

 

Do not subjectively judge or discriminate against the victim because of the violence they have endured, and never blame the victim. Comments such as "Why are you so weak?" "Can't you handle such a small matter?" or "It's your own fault for being too soft" constitute secondary harm to the victim.


 

Many victims, for various reasons, do not immediately end their marriage or cohabitation. It is important to offer full understanding. Arbitrary judgments or criticism will only make victims defensive, gradually distancing themselves from you, and possibly cutting off this channel of help.

 

 

Educate the victim about domestic violence and related laws and regulations. Help them recognize the nature and seriousness of the issue, and encourage the victim to self-empower..

 

Some victims do not realize the severity of domestic violence after experiencing it—for example, believing the abuser won't repeat the violence after apologizing, thinking the abuser will gradually change through their own influence, or assuming violent acts won't have devastating consequences. Ultimately, this stems from a lack of awareness about domestic violence, overlooking its cyclical nature, harmfulness, and even lethality. Additionally, understanding local laws helps victims fully use legal tools to protect themselves and uphold their rights, such as evidence collection in domestic violence cases and applying for restraining orders.

 

 

Calm the emotional state of the victim and provide positive, encouraging responses. Or suggest the victim seek psychological counseling to rekindle hope in life and gradually heal psychological trauma.

 

In domestic violence cases, perpetrators often impose psychological pressure on victims through insults, defamation, threats, and intimidation, and by belittling the victim or labeling them, attempting to shift responsibility for the abuse onto the victim. This aims to erode the victim's confidence and self-esteem and control their behavior. In this process, victims often fall into a state of diminished self-worth, feeling "worthless" or "completely useless." Therefore, helping victims recognize the psychological pressure and manipulation they are under, and assisting them in rebuilding self-identity, regaining confidence in life, and restoring hope, is equally crucial.

 

 

If the survivor chooses to leave the abuser, provide them with temporary shelter and other necessary support when conditions allow.

 



*Encourage or assist the survivor in seeking help from relevant organizations, such as legal aid agencies, psychological counseling services, and domestic violence emergency assistance organizations. 


3.3 Other Situations


3.3.1 

If you possess relevant knowledge, are concerned about domestic violence, and have the time, financial means, and other conditions, you may join public welfare organizations as a short-term or long-term volunteer, doing what you can and contributing your share of strength.

 

3.3.2 

Likewise, as a survivor of domestic violence, you may, according to your personal wishes, choose to speak out publicly. Your voice can help raise awareness of this dark corner, and it may also encourage those currently suffering from domestic violence to recognize the truth, bravely escape, and start a new life.

Looking Ahead—What Elements Need Urgent Improvement


4.1 Providing Better Policy Support for Relevant Volunteers


Volunteers and professionals working in the field of domestic violence, such as lawyers and psychological counselors, need financial resources just like anyone else. However, because work in this field is less tied to material benefits, without sufficient policy support, organizations focusing on gender-based violence will face even greater difficulties. This predicament exists in most regions of the world and urgently needs to be addressed. Beyond financial hardship, the psychological stress endured by domestic violence support workers and the shrinking operational space for related organizations also require further policy optimization and support.


4.2 Promote the improvement and optimization of legal systems and judicial procedures.

Provide applicants with more convenient and comprehensive support in areas such as case filing, litigation, and evidence submission.

 

Moreover, many survivors are not fully aware of procedures like evidence collection and litigation, making assistance from judicial institutions and nonprofit organizations especially crucial.



(v)

Looking Ahead—Perspectives on Gender Equality


Violence against women is deeply intertwined with gender equality. In today's world, gender equality has sparked widespread discussion globally.


First, women need to have a clear and rational understanding of gender relations, avoiding the trivialization or glorification of unreasonable or discriminatory practices. In December 2015, renowned actresses Emma Watson, Angelina Jolie, and Kristen Stewart participated in an anti-domestic violence photo campaign—depicting themselves with bruised faces, scratch marks, and expressions of numbness, fear, and despair. The campaign aimed to call for greater societal attention to the heinous act of domestic violence. However, two years earlier, a trend known as the "domestic violence makeup" briefly went viral online. In these photos, young women used dark eyeshadow and deep-colored lipstick to simulate the appearance of having been beaten, proudly claiming it looked beautiful and cute. This phenomenon was severely criticized by official channels and public opinion. Because behind such mockery lies disregard and indifference toward fellow victims, a terrifying normalization of gender-based violence, and a violation of one's own dignity. Undoubtedly, such phenomena are deeply ironic in the context of gender equality, and society at large is jointly calling for resistance against such vulgar behaviors.


Furthermore, the roles women play in the workplace and at home remain highly controversial. In many parts of the world, women still commonly face various constraints in job hunting, promotions, and wage distribution. As a result, advocates are calling for improved maternity leave policies to provide women with greater space and choices. Regardless of whether they choose to have children, some industries still tend to favor male candidates due to various considerations. Due to limitations in maternity leave policies, men's career advancement is often less affected by parenthood than women's. Worse still, some women are directly fired during pregnancy.


Additionally, in many households, the burden of childbearing, child-rearing, and managing domestic chores overwhelmingly falls on women, while their partners and society at large remain invisible—commonly mocked as "widowed parenting." This domestic burden affects some women's career development and income. Yet, material income significantly impacts women's status: a 2010 U.S. study on domestic violence found that narrowing the gender income gap can reduce the likelihood of domestic violence occurring. One reason is that higher wages empower women with greater bargaining power and more external options.


Moreover, the naturalization of the "male gaze" remains an obstacle on the path to gender equality. "Male gaze" is a term from sociology and philosophy, referring to how, in a patriarchal society, women are the seen—the second sex—while men, as the primary viewers, enjoy the pleasure of voyeurism through various media and channels. In China's traditional power and division of labor models, women are in an absolutely subordinate position, while men hold fame, profit, and power, gazing down at women from a top-down perspective. Social norms have solidified and normalized this relationship of gazing and being gazed upon. Feminist theory suggests that social and cultural customs shaped by biological sex lead to differentiated expectations for men and women in values, institutional norms, and other domains. On this basis, whenever women exhibit any "deviant behavior," they are "naturally" subjected to scrutiny and external and internal suppression. However, in today's highly industrialized modern society, traditional division of labor systems have already changed. More and more women emphasize self-empowerment and reject the naturalization of the "male gaze." Yet, in contemporary society, the shadow of the male gaze still lingers in public discourse on beauty, evaluations of women, and various cultural works. Thus, this transformation will still take time.


Finally, equality among same-sex individuals is also worth attention. Whether in same-sex partnerships or relationships, the essence of equality is no different from heterosexual equality—it ultimately comes down to mutual respect between individuals. Regardless of gender, people need to build psychological awareness from the ground up, avoiding violence as a means to solve problems or vent emotions, and instead respecting the fundamental rights of others as human beings. Moreover, the prejudice and discrimination women face do not come solely from men, but also partly from women themselves. This is known as misogyny—a term medically defined as hatred or strong prejudice against women, including phenomena such as sexism and the devaluation of women—which exists among both men and women. Therefore, in advancing gender equality, overcoming and eliminating ingrained gender biases in people's minds is particularly important.



(vi)

Conclusion


Behind all forms of domestic violence lies the issue of power and control. The more civilized a society is, the lower its tolerance for domestic violence. As a survivor, one should bravely face the reality, distance oneself from harm, rekindle hope, and achieve self-empowerment; as a bystander, one should actively offer help and provide feasible support in various ways. In short, domestic violence is a social issue—only through joint efforts by the entire society can we gradually move toward a more equal, inclusive, and civilized modern era.
