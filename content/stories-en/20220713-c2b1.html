Introduction

     Since late May, nearly 250,000 Afghans have been forced to flee their homes, of whom approximately 80% are women and children (UNHCR, August 16). By mid-August 2021, the situation in Afghanistan had become clear: the Taliban had seized the presidential palace and Kabul had fallen. Desperate civilians tried every possible means to escape the land. On August 19, news spread rapidly across global media headlines that Afghan women, during the U.S. military evacuation, threw their babies over barbed wire, pleading with British soldiers to take them away. In fact, as early as the end of 2018, the Trump administration in the United States had begun peace talks with the Taliban over the Afghan war, reaching an agreement in February 2020. The U.S. committed to withdrawing its troops from Afghanistan by May 2021, while the Taliban promised to ensure Afghanistan would not become a breeding ground for extremism threatening U.S. allies. After Trump lost the 2021 U.S. presidential election and Biden took office, the U.S. troop withdrawal was delayed by several months. 

     With the change of regime, the domestic situation in Afghanistan became turbulent. The Taliban stated that it hoped for a "peaceful" transfer of power and promised to respect the rights of Afghan women. At their first press conference after seizing power, spokesperson Suhail Shaheen publicly declared that Afghan women would not be deprived of employment or education opportunities under Taliban rule, and that women could continue working, but must wear veils. In other words, the Taliban guaranteed that Afghan women's rights would be respected "within the framework of Islam."

Figure 1. Afghan women wearing burqas   

Image source: BBC News


     On August 19 local time, the Taliban announced the establishment of the "Islamic Emirate of Afghanistan." The Taliban spokesperson intended to send a message to the world through the press conference on the 15th: today's Taliban is no longer the Taliban of 20 years ago. He claimed that the current Taliban does not want war or conflict, but instead desires to establish an "inclusive" Islamic government. During the Taliban's rule from 1996 to 2001, women were banned from education and employment, required to wear the full-body black veil known as the "burqa" in public, and allowed to leave home only when accompanied by a male relative (Initium Media, 2021). Violations of these rules led to religious police being granted the power, in the name of Islam, to publicly humiliate and punish women. Afghan women thus lived in endless fear for years. The 9/11 attacks, which shocked the world, became a turning point; the Taliban regime quickly collapsed under strong U.S. military strikes. Now that the Taliban has regained power, many Afghan women and the international community, closely watching the situation, cannot help but fear a repeat of the Taliban's oppression of women's rights through extreme interpretations of Islamic doctrine.

     On September 2, 2021, according to Al Jazeera (2021) news reports, dozens of Afghan women protested on the streets of Herat (a city in northwestern Afghanistan), demanding that the Taliban fulfill its promises and grant women the legal right to education and employment. Twenty-four-year-old Marriam Ebram told reporters that in recent weeks, she and other women had been told they could no longer continue working, or that they were immediately sent back upon entering their offices (Al Jazeera, 2021). This 24-year-old woman bravely spoke out on Twitter, encouraging Afghan women not to fear, to stand together, and fight for the rights that belong to them (Don't afraid, don't afraid, we are together).

     This series will analyze the harsh realities faced by women in Afghanistan—a country undergoing regime change and ravaged by war—from three aspects: employment, education, and bodily rights. This article will focus on analyzing and reflecting on employment and education rights; the next part will discuss Afghan women's bodily rights.

I. Women's Right to Employment under Regime Instability and Gender Discrimination

1.1 Current Status and Concerns Regarding Women's Employment Rights

Even before the Taliban entered Afghanistan's capital Kabul, Afghanistan had long been one of the most dangerous countries for women (Cahalan, Gitter & Fletcher, 2020), with a female labor force participation rate (FLFP) of only 19%, ranking sixth from the bottom globally in employment rate (ILO, 2016). The Taliban's takeover has further intensified international and domestic Afghan concerns about women's personal safety and equal rights, especially regarding the rights to work and education.

     According to The Paper, 26-year-old Afghan woman Zahra fears that with the Taliban in power, her educational opportunities and future prospects will vanish. She told AFP that as the Taliban approached, she had been unable to go to her office for nearly a month, forced to work from home. What worries her is that since the Taliban took over her city, she has completely lost her opportunity and freedom to work (Nan Boyi, 2021). 

     Not only are Afghan women's employment rights being drastically curtailed, but the lives of women who once worked for Western countries, especially the United States, are now under serious threat. Due to the nature of their previous work, they have fallen into dire straits. They wrote, "We and our staff regularly receive reports about women being targeted, threatened, kidnapped, tortured, and assassinated because of their jobs" (JAKES, 2021). Although the Taliban has claimed since taking power that it will establish an "inclusive government" and allow women to work and study "within the framework of Islamic law," many ordinary women remain pessimistic and are unable or afraid to work due to fear (BBC, 2021). The Taliban's armed forces and violence hang over their heads like the sword of Damocles; the prospects for women's employment are blurred and highly uncertain. 

     But only by clearly understanding what they have experienced can we truly empathize with their fear, and with the blood and tears behind Afghan women's struggle for their rights.


1.2 Causes of Women's Employment Difficulties

Regime changes and war turmoil, along with unreasonable gender divisions of labor and more entrenched patriarchal systems, have repeatedly pulled and weakened Afghan women's rights, gradually pushing them into a more vulnerable and helpless situation.

     For Afghan women, or more broadly Muslim women, the normative teachings of Islam contradict the way the Taliban treats women as reported in the media. In Islamic doctrine—regardless of sect—women are encouraged to pursue spiritual aspirations and actively engage in employment and education (JAKES, 2021). But in the 1990s, after the Taliban came to power, the status of local women plummeted (Xue Jing, 2021). Even in 2020, in Helmand Province—the former Taliban stronghold in Afghanistan—the Taliban banned women from receiving education or working, and opposed women joining security forces or working in government agencies (Zou Delu, 2020).

Image source: Baidu

Secondly, decades of prolonged war have brought rampant violence and low female labor rates or participation. Research shows that security issues are a key factor suppressing Afghan women's labor force participation (Desai & Li, 2016). Empowering women to participate in the workforce could bring significant economic improvement to Afghanistan (Cahalan, Gitter & Fletcher, 2020). 

     Next, unreasonable gender divisions of labor force most Afghan women to bear nearly all household responsibilities. The home has largely become a prison confining Afghan women, and even when they are forced to go out to buy clothes or seek medical care, they must wear the burqa—a garment like shackles. This prison-like clothing often causes Afghan women to lose balance and fall, and is completely incompatible with modern work environments. In short, lack of education deprives them of sufficient job training, making it difficult for them to compete in the labor market, while continuous domestic work blocks their path back into employment. This pattern of experience continues across generations among lower-class Afghan women, whose long-standing domestic labor is unrecognized and who face persistent discrimination in the labor market.

Figure 3. Armed Taliban members passing by a beauty salon (August 18, 2020)

Image source: The Paper 

Additionally, deeply entrenched patriarchal systems and the severe gender inequality under this system have generated violence embedded within Afghan women and their families. Middle Eastern and North African countries are among the lowest in the world in terms of women's labor force participation, a phenomenon attributed to traditional Islamic gender role norms (Korotayev, Issaev, & Shishkina, 2015). For example, mosques in Islam require women to be separated from men. Therefore, in such places, female labor participation levels are often below average (Korotayev, 2004). Even in families without strict religious rules, restrictions on women's mobility may be part of social norms, and these restrictions have been reinforced during wartime. Trani et al. (2011) argue that Afghan women are culturally isolated and restricted in their movement in public spaces, which indirectly translates into limitations on their employment, education, or social interactions.

     Even for the rare Afghan women who have received higher education, nepotism and corruption within the Afghan government still limit women's access to better positions and fair wages. They even have to contend with long-standing sexual harassment issues in the workplace (Payne, Burridge, & Rahmani, 2021). Moreover, contact between aid organizations providing employment and educational training for women and the women themselves is often subject to strict scrutiny and surveillance by the Kabul authorities (Fielden, & Azerbaijani-Moghadam, 2000), making it extremely difficult for ordinary women to gain the ability to independently enter the workforce.

     In reality, the employment crisis facing Afghan women is not irreversible. The religiosity of Islam and emerging feminism play a key role in shaping Muslim women's entrepreneurial capabilities and their resilience in enduring harsh conditions in conflict zones, helping to transform the bleak employment situation for Afghan women (Althalathini, Al-Dajani, & Apostolopoulos, 2021). Calls for affirmative action, advocacy for job training and mentorship programs, and efforts to secure higher education qualifications for women (Payne, Burridge, & Rahmani, 2021) are becoming primary tools for Afghan women defending their employment rights domestically.


II. Women's Right to Education Amid Multiple Challenges

2.1 Current State of Educational Challenges Facing Women

      Statistics on the number of Afghan children attending or out of school vary widely and are controversial. Reliable data is difficult to obtain in Afghanistan and often lacks accuracy. A 2015 report by the Afghan government stated that over 8 million children were enrolled in education, with 39% being girls. In December 2016, the Minister of Education announced the actual number of enrolled children was 6 million. In April 2017, a Ministry of Education official told Human Rights Watch that 9.3 million children were in school, with 39% being girls. In fact, all these figures were inflated by the government—some children who had left school three years earlier were still counted as enrolled. The Afghan Ministry of Education also admitted, "There is a large number of out-of-school children, but we do not know exactly how many, who they are, where they are, or their backgrounds."

     Although it is difficult to obtain accurate statistics on school enrollment in Afghanistan, all indicators suggest that girls' access to education is declining. According to the World Bank, the percentage of Afghan girls enrolled in first grade peaked in 2011 at 65%, dropping to 57% by 2015 (UNESCO Institute for Statistics, 2020). In January 2016, UNICEF estimated that 40% of all school-age children in Afghanistan were not attending school. According to Human Rights Watch, only 37% of girls in Afghanistan are literate, compared to 66% of boys; among adult women, only 19% are literate, compared to 49% of adult men (Human Rights Watch, 2017).

Image source: I Wont Be A Doctor, and One Day You'll Be Sick

 Girls' Access to Education in Afghanistan

     During the Taliban's rule from 1996 to 2001, a decree was issued banning girls over the age of eight from receiving education, enforced through punishments including stoning, flogging, and amputation, while restricting women to their homes unless accompanied by a male family member. This deprived large numbers of women of access to most jobs. The Taliban insisted this was only a temporary suspension of schooling, claiming women would return to schools and workplaces once facilities and streets were secured to prevent cross-gender interaction. This policy had devastating consequences. In Kabul alone, the ruling affected 106,256 female students and 8,000 female university students. Additionally, 7,793 female teachers were dismissed, leading to the closure of 63 schools due to the sudden lack of educators. Some women established secret schools at home for local children or ran schools under the guise of sewing classes for other women, such as the Golden Needle Sewing School.

     The Golden Needle Sewing School was an underground women's learning organization founded in 1996. Women would come to the school three times a week, ostensibly to sew, but in reality would attend lectures by a literature professor from Herat University. If religious police approached, children playing outside would warn them, giving them time to hide the books.

      Now, as time fast-forwards to recent weeks, with Taliban forces triumphing across the country, the era of mild disguise has ended, and negative reports about school closures, movement restrictions, and women being forced out of jobs continue to emerge. The Taliban spokesperson's claims of commitments to respecting women's rights—including the right to education—now ring more hollow than ever before. Women's access to education and the preservation of their rights hang in the balance, and we will explore from multiple angles the causes of this educational crisis, as well as the importance of education for women's empowerment and peacebuilding.

2.2 Causes of the Educational Crisis for Women

     Multiple factors contribute to the lack of educational rights for Afghan women. Ongoing war and political transitions constitute the greatest external barrier to women's access to education, while war-induced scarcity of educational resources and widespread poverty further restrict women's equal access to education. In addition, deeply rooted gender discrimination in Afghan society is another cause of the educational crisis. Due to these relatively backward cultural beliefs, Afghan families in times of poverty and instability are more likely to sacrifice women's interests. In short, the interplay of war, policy, economic conditions, and social attitudes has ultimately trapped Afghan women in a vicious cycle of educational deprivation.

 

War: Security Issues

     The Afghan civil war has severely deprived girls of opportunities to receive education. The United Nations Assistance Mission in Afghanistan (UNAMA) has tracked civilian casualties since 2009 and found that by 2016, civilian casualties in Afghanistan totaled 11,418 (3,498 killed, 7,920 injured), a 3% increase compared to 2015. Since 2009, the UN has recorded 24,841 Afghan civilians killed and 45,347 injured. In 2016 alone, 923 children were killed and 2,589 injured, a 24% increase from 2015. In 2016, UNAMA documented 94 attacks targeting education, resulting in 91 civilian casualties (24 killed, 67 injured). These included 13 targeted killings and 12 abductions involving 55 students and education personnel, 10 deliberate attacks destroying educational property, and 5 improvised explosive device (IED) attacks on education facilities. Among them, a female school teacher was shot dead, and a female school principal was abducted.

     Every child injured or killed in conflict results in more children being deprived of their right to education. The insecurity of the country and cities makes parents increasingly fearful of their children's journey to school; many schools that were once accessible by foot before the war have now become off-limits. Beyond war, girls' routes to school are also more dangerous than boys'. They often face a higher risk of criminal acts, including abuse, abduction, and sexual harassment. Many families use certain physical markers related to female biology to decide whether girls should drop out of school. For example, many parents use a girl's first menstruation or her height as indicators to determine whether she can continue her education.

 

Policy: School System

     Afghanistan's primary and secondary education system mainly consists of four types of schools. ① Government schools, managed and staffed by the government, often with donor support, mostly from the Ministry of Education. ② Community-Based Education (CBE), which has successfully reached many Afghan girls but operates entirely outside the government education system and relies on donor funding. ③ Madrasas, which focus primarily on religious education and do not teach core academic subjects. ④ Private schools, offering higher-quality instruction for families who can afford tuition.

     Within the school system, children face challenges in education primarily due to economic conditions, infrastructure, and teacher availability. Economically, although public schools do not charge tuition, miscellaneous fees still represent a significant expense. Families of government school students must provide supplies, including pens, pencils, notebooks, uniforms, backpacks, and sometimes pay for government-issued textbooks. Moreover, corruption in the education sector is common, resulting in textbooks not arriving on time or being in short supply, creating high indirect costs that are enough to force many poor families to withdraw their children from school.

     In terms of infrastructure, many schools suffer from overcrowding, damaged and dilapidated buildings, and lack of furniture and supplies, forcing many girls to study in tents. 30% of public schools in Afghanistan lack safe drinking water, and 60% lack toilets, making it difficult for many girls to manage hygiene during menstruation or causing them to stay home during their periods, leading to lower attendance, poorer academic performance, and increasing the risk of complete dropout. Additionally, many Afghan schools require gender segregation, dividing students into separate classes by gender, which often results in shorter class times for girls, making it impossible to complete the full curriculum.

     Regarding teaching staff, many schools face challenges such as short teaching shifts, understaffing, low wages, and unsafe working conditions. Afghan teachers typically earn less than $100 per month. There is a shortage of teachers, especially female teachers, and it is difficult to deploy them to rural areas. In seven of Afghanistan's 34 provinces, female teachers make up less than 10% of the teaching force, and in 17 provinces, less than 20%. The shortage of female teachers prevents many girls from attending school, as their families often do not accept male teachers instructing their daughters.

Economy: Poverty and Child Labor

In Afghanistan, poverty is one of the biggest obstacles to education. According to a World Bank report, in Afghanistan, 62% of children from wealthy families attend school, compared to only 48% from poor families. Moreover, the education gap caused by the rich-poor divide is widening over time. Between 2011 and 2012, school attendance among children from wealthy families increased by 1.8%; however, between 2013 and 2014, school attendance among children from poor families decreased by 6%.

Girls from poor families are often in a worse situation than boys. When poor families can only afford to send one child to school, Afghan parents tend to choose boys. Economic reasons also play a significant role. Afghan girls often leave their families after marriage to contribute to their husband's household, whereas boys are expected to continue supporting their parents. Given that educational opportunities are the result of family decisions, parents often view educating boys as a "wiser" investment.

Image source: I Wont Be A Doctor, and One Day You'll Be Sick: Girls' Access to Education in Afghanistan

Poverty forces many Afghan children to work either before starting school or during their education. A large number of children and adolescents engage in paid but informal work. For example, a significant number of girls must take on household labor when their mothers go out to work, or engage in carpet weaving, tailoring, begging on the streets, or selling small items. Statistics show that at least one-quarter of Afghan children aged 5–14 earn a living or assist their families through work. They work long hours for little pay under poor working conditions. Many children, including girls, may suffer illness, injury, or even death due to hazardous work.

Although Afghanistan has enacted laws to protect child laborers, these laws are rarely enforced. For example, Afghan labor law sets the minimum employment age at 18; children aged 15–17 are only permitted to work if the work is harmless to them, their weekly working hours are fewer than 35, and the work takes the form of vocational training, while children aged 14 and below are not allowed to work at all.

Mindset: Gender Discrimination and Child Marriage

Gender inequality remains deeply embedded in Afghan societal consciousness. Many Afghans do not regard "female education" as a serious issue, believing instead that "girls should not go to school" and should stay at home doing housework, or even viewing educated women as shameful. Some mullahs (Mullah, Muslim religious and Sharia teachers) also hold such views.

In marriage and family life, the traditional belief in "men work outside, women manage the home" still persists among Afghans, leading many girls to be forced into marriage before completing their education, beginning lives centered on child-rearing and housework. In Afghanistan, arranged marriages and child marriages are common. Afghan law sets the minimum marriage age for girls at 16 (or 15 with the consent of the girl's father or a judge), while the legal marriage age for men is 18. This disparity in treatment between men and women violates the spirit of international law, and in practice, even this law is rarely enforced.

Although the Afghan government has not explicitly enacted laws prohibiting married women from continuing their education, in reality, most women discontinue their studies after marriage. They often face greater obstacles from their families (such as household duties, pregnancy, and child-rearing), and their husbands often forbid them from going out in public. The harms of child marriage to girls extend beyond the loss of educational opportunities; they also include serious health risks (including death) for both the girl and her infant due to early pregnancy. Furthermore, girls who marry at a very young age are also more likely to become victims of domestic violence within their marriages.

III. Employment Rights, Education Rights, and the Women's Liberation Movement


     Despite immense difficulties, Afghan women have never ceased resisting and fighting for their rights. Empowering women is not only profoundly significant for individual women but also holds undeniable importance for the country's overall economic development. However, for over forty years, Afghanistan has remained a nation in conflict, making it difficult for Afghan women to obtain effective employment and economic empowerment protections. Due to long-standing patriarchal control and expectations of obedience, Afghan women's ability to exercise their rights through education, employment, and political participation often depends on the will of their male guardians. The complex realities make genuine equality and universal access to education and employment still distant goals. Nevertheless, women's empowerment remains a crucial breakthrough point for the Afghan women's liberation movement, closely linked to the current challenges of women's human rights and the trajectory of Afghanistan's peacebuilding process.

3.1 The Right to Education: An Indispensable Fundamental Human Right

Women's right to education is an indispensable fundamental human right and is connected to many other human rights issues. Audrey Azoulay, Director-General of UNESCO, has called for the protection of the Afghan people's right to education, stating: "Everyone must have unimpeded access to basic education, especially girls and women. Education is an indispensable fundamental human right for the Afghan people to exercise other rights and support Afghanistan's development." Its importance is especially evident during times of political instability. UNESCO calls on all parties to ensure the right to education without any discrimination. Students, teachers, and education workers must have a safe learning environment, and girls and women must be able to continue studying and teaching without any restrictions. Director-General Azoulay added, "Afghanistan cannot afford to lose the tremendous progress made in areas such as education. Education for girls and women must continue—Afghanistan's future depends on them" (UNESCO, 2021).

     At the same time, education is also a fundamental right enshrined in international treaties ratified by Afghanistan, including the Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC) and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR), and Afghanistan has ratified the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW), which includes an obligation to ensure women's equal rights with men, including equality in education.

3.2 The Importance of Education and Economic Empowerment for Social Development

If protecting women's rights is the foundation of human rights protection, then granting women the rights to education and free employment is one of the most important drivers of national development. Existing research has found a link between women's empowerment and overall social development, playing a key role in Afghanistan achieving its intended development goals and sustaining peacebuilding (Ripenburg, 2002). First, economic empowerment is a crucial aspect of women's empowerment. When women gain economic empowerment, gender equality is promoted, economic development is stimulated, and the national economy is pushed onto a sustainable development path. Opening labor markets to women and providing them with employment opportunities is the most important step toward economic empowerment. A 2015 study by the McKinsey Global Institute found that in a "full potential" scenario—where women play the same role in the labor market as men—global annual GDP could increase by up to $28 trillion, or 26%, by 2025. Therefore, the long-term exclusion of women from the labor market is not only an exploitation of individual women's rights but also harmful to the overall economic development of the nation.

 Image source: https://www.yicai.com/news/101149548.html

Ensuring and promoting the right to education empowers women economically and in employment, promoting labor market development. A 2013 study found that women with at most primary education are less likely to obtain formal employment, but secondary and tertiary education increase the likelihood of formal employment by 10.3% and 25.6%, respectively (Sandrine & Verick, 2013). Another 2019 study confirmed these findings, showing a significant positive correlation between secondary school attendance and labor market participation rates. Both studies indicate that when girls continue their education beyond adolescence, their participation in the labor force increases (Osundina, 2019).

     In addition, as Afghanistan's Minister of Education, Rangina Hamidi, pointed out, education is the best tool we have—not only to empower women but also to enable them to use this tool throughout their lives and in raising their children (Barton, 2021). Of course, educational empowerment involves more than just granting girls the right to attend school. On this point, the World Bank has provided a detailed explanation of what educational empowerment means—it includes "ensuring girls are in school and feel safe; giving them the opportunity to complete all levels of education and acquire the knowledge and skills needed to compete in the labor market; helping them learn the socio-emotional and life skills needed to navigate and adapt to a changing world; enabling them to make decisions about their own lives; and allowing them to contribute to their communities and the world" (World Bank, 2021).

   Educating women and girls generates important socioeconomic benefits that benefit the entire society. In 2005, the United States Agency for International Development noted, "These benefits include increased economic productivity, higher household income, delayed marriage, lower fertility rates, and improved infant and child health and survival." Similarly, the World Bank has stated that girls' education is considered one of its most strategic development priorities, as educated women can help lift families, communities, and nations out of poverty. A study by Ajasa and Salako (2015) also found a significant positive correlation between gender equality in education and the transformation of women and nations.

Moreover, empowering women through education also contributes to improving other national development indicators. These development indicators include lower fertility rates, better infant health and nutrition, and reduced child marriage, among others. For example, a study of maternal education across 175 countries over more than 40 years concluded that for each additional year of schooling a woman receives, her child mortality rate decreases by approximately 10% (Gakidou et al. 2010). Increased education may also help eliminate child, early, and forced marriage. According to the United Nations Population Fund, 33,000 child marriages occurred worldwide every day in 2020 (United Nations Population Fund, 2020). Compared to women with lower levels of education, women with higher education tend to marry later, have their first child at an older age, and have fewer, healthier, and better-educated children (Sheikh & Loney, 2020). 

Therefore, economic and educational empowerment of women is not only essential for safeguarding individual women's fundamental human rights, but also carries profound national and social significance. Economic and educational empowerment are often interlinked and mutually reinforcing; when women are empowered, societies become wealthier, safer, healthier, and more inclusive. Although Afghanistan has achieved some progress since 2001, the country still faces a long and difficult road toward genuine women's empowerment and a true women's liberation movement.

IV. Summary


Against the backdrop of the Taliban's second seizure of power in Afghanistan, this article reviews the lack of protection for Afghan women's rights to education and employment, and reflects on the significance of women's employment and education within the women's liberation movement. We find that historical context, political conflicts, and gender discrimination embedded in social norms and institutions are often key reasons behind the violation of women's rights. Given the broad scope of women's rights protection, the safeguarding of rights to employment, education, healthcare, political participation, and freedom of speech, association, and assembly are often deeply intertwined. Further research will help us re-examine the status of Afghan women's rights and provide insights into addressing women's protection in conflict-affected nations. 

     Amowitz et al. (2003) argue that in Taliban-ruled Afghanistan, policies restricting women's rights are not the product of traditional beliefs, social norms, or economic deprivation; rather, they are deliberately crafted policies that can be easily enacted—and easily revoked.

However, years of war and repeated changes in government have disrupted the normal functioning of Afghanistan's legal policies and social institutions for women's protection, making the violation of women's rights a common reality over the past two decades. On this point, Shah (2005) frankly stated that in Afghanistan, women's human rights have been violated under every regime—whether monarchy, secular rule, the mujahideen, the internationally supported Karzai government, or the Taliban in power.

From a broader perspective, the plight of Afghan women is a microcosm of the global refugee crisis. According to the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR), women constitute at least half of any refugee population. Like male refugees, they require respect for their human rights during flight, are highly vulnerable to sexual and other forms of violence, and often bear the responsibility of caring for more vulnerable family members such as children and the elderly (UNHCR, 2021). Therefore, the protection of women in conflict-affected nations is often highly complex, as historical customs, international disputes, social systems, and discursive frameworks intertwine and strip away the fragile rights women still possess. For Afghan women, civil war has displaced them, pushing them from an already marginalized and discriminated position into a deeper abyss. Twenty-five years ago, the war trauma brought by Taliban rule and officials' violations of women's human rights profoundly affected Afghan women's health (Rasekh et al. 1998). Today, it remains unknown whether the Taliban government will fulfill its promises and deliver a truly "inclusive" government for Afghan women. 

     Of course, refugee women are not merely passive victims forced to cease resistance in such social contexts. On one hand, deeply entrenched gender inequality in Afghanistan has shown signs of improvement. For instance, in recent years, residents in some areas—including both men and women—not only wish to see girls attending school (equal educational rights), trading in markets, or treating patients in hospitals (freedom of employment and social participation), but also understand these as fundamental to community peace (Firchow & Urwin, 2020). On the other hand, refugees are making their own contributions on the global stage. In August 2021, six athletes from the Refugee Paralympic Team at the Tokyo Paralympics represented 82 million displaced people worldwide, declaring to the world the resilience and strong agency of those living in conflict-affected nations. As female flag bearer Alia Issa said, refugee women should rise up, step out of their homes, participate in sports, and proudly display their strength. Indeed, the courage and achievements of the refugee team are deeply moving, but their second appearance is also undeniably regrettable. Women's protection is a shared global issue. We hope that under the leadership of the United Nations, the protection of women's rights in conflict-affected nations can be effectively addressed as soon as possible.
