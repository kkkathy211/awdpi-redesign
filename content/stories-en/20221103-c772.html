Evelyn is a professional counsellor with expertise in landscape design. She seems particularly special—destined even before birth with a mission to "save others." Though she struggled along the way between reality and ideals, in the end, she truly became an "oasis" nourishing minds and hearts, just like her name suggests.


When it comes to Evelyn, we prefer to call her Gezhou.


Evelyn has a poetic given name: "Gezhou." When we first met her, we were curious whether there was a touching story behind this name.

"I grew up in northwest China, where there are many deserts. Before I was born, my father decided that no matter if I was a boy or a girl, I should be named Gezhou, like an oasis in the Gobi Desert—someone who heals others and offers hope. "


Yet Gezhou seemed to struggle for a long time to live up to this poetic name. Though she always understood its origin and meaning, as a child she often felt troubled by being mistaken for the opposite gender, and didn't fully grasp the strength this name carried.


'As a child, I used to feed stray animals. Throughout my growth, I hoped to pursue a career that served others. But it wasn't until I became a professional counsellor that I truly felt capable of living up to the meaning embedded in my name. It has become my life creed, and through continuous practice, my understanding of it has deepened. "





Only by having the courage to follow one's inner voice and cultivating an independent self,

can one become an oasis.


Before becoming a professional counsellor, Gezhou once thought she would be a miserable landscape designer. Though she had dreamed of studying psychology since high school, when choosing her major in university, she was "defeated by reality."


"My family didn't support it, and at that time, career prospects in psychology were unclear in China, so I chose landscape design based on my hobby of drawing. But later, I realized that interest and passion are entirely different things. Without talent and genuine passion, it was hard to keep going. "


Fortunately, this 'painful' detour strengthened Gezhou's resilience and helped her truly confirm her passion, enabling her to firmly follow her inner voice. After completing her bachelor's degree, she went to New York to study psychology. Even when facing many difficulties during her studies in the U.S., she never felt suffering—this genuine passion made the so-called detours shine brilliantly, bringing her step by step closer to her dream.


After completing her master's degree, Gezhou chose to stay in the U.S. as a professional counsellor. Like all young people entering society, the real world she encountered at work was darker and more grim than what textbooks described. Sometimes, she felt enveloped by a grey haze— an overwhelming sense of helplessness washed over her..


"The first four years of my work brought the most difficulties, challenges, and insights. Fresh out of school with little social experience, and having been well-protected by my family since childhood, my first job required me to face and engage directly with much of society's cruelty. I experienced vicarious trauma and became deeply immersed in my clients' experiences, feeling powerless."


Research shows that On average, domestic violence survivors need to make seven attempts to seek help before they can truly and successfully escape their predicament and receive assistance. Yet even after receiving help, some survivors still return to the environments they once fled, for various reasons. Moreover, due to these harmful relationships and experiences, their worldviews undergo profound changes. They may see the world as bleak and struggle to believe their lives can improve. "This creates a deep sense of helplessness, both for the survivors and for us as counsellors. When I encountered such cases, my supervisor would remind me not to get trapped in that feeling. While empathizing, we must learn to step outside the client's experience and think about their issues from a more professional perspective."


In 2020, the first year of the pandemic, Gezhou was promoted to Director of Mental Health Clinic at her organization in the U.S. However, one year after her promotion, she chose to return to China. This decision wasn't only due to limitations on her career development caused by immigration status, nor solely due to homesickness from living abroad. For her, returning home held special significance.


"I deeply value family relationships, but if I had stayed with my parents, it would have been hard to achieve true personal independence. So my original goal in going abroad was to experience a different life through physical separation and achieve personal independence. During my time in the U.S., I gradually became aware of my own psychological maturity and independence. I realized that my initial goal had slowly been achieved."


In our interview, we could clearly sense Gezhou's passion for psychology and the positive impact her experience studying and working in the U.S. had on her. We believe it is precisely this courage to follow her inner voice, combined with her profound life experiences, that shaped her into an oasis in the Gobi Desert.





To enter someone else's inner world, one often starts with small details.


Gezhou has extensive experience providing psychological counselling in the U.S. After completing her master's degree, her first job was offering psychological counselling to women in New York, helping them return to the workforce and rebuild their personal lives. The cases mostly involved domestic violence, sexual assault, and human trafficking—each existing separately yet often overlapping. Most survivors' experiences of sexual assault occurred within domestic violence. In human trafficking cases, sexual assault was also common. Some women entered into marriages with unsuitable partners in hopes of immigrating to the U.S., only to endure violent relationships. Others were lured by traffickers with promises of jobs, only to suffer economic and sexual exploitation. For them, nightmares don't happen just once—they come one after another.


They each have their own names, but if we need a single term to describe them, we tend to habitually call them "victims." However, in Gezhou's eyes, she prefers to call them "survivors."


"Most of my clients face many hardships in life. We hope that by shifting the language of identity, we can convey more positive feelings to them—starting from small details, uncovering their strengths, changing the trajectory of their stories, helping them feel they are doing well, and letting them sense their own power. "


Of course, merely changing terminology is not enough. To truly enter a client's inner world, it seems necessary first to establish an equal and trustworthy inner connection.


Gezhou's second job involved working with another group—African Americans. This role required her to go deep into local communities and enter the living environments of survivors. Due to vastly different upbringings, the survivors had life experiences completely different from Gezhou's and were deeply affected by intergenerational trauma. In this role, Gezhou had to continuously strive to understand different people, empathize with them, and build connections.


"We're all so different, yet everyone has an instinctive longing for connection. Their responses to the attention, support, and praise you offer are highly conducive to building that connection. I used to focus more on techniques and therapies, but these experiences taught me that establishing an equal connection with people is the foundation of healing. "





Healing others means empowering them to heal themselves.


Gezhou often provides individual counselling on emotional management, romantic relationships, personal growth, and interpersonal skills. Among these four areas, she believes personal growth is ultimately the goal for everyone.


"These four areas are actually interconnected. But compared to personal growth, the other three tend to become focal points at different life stages. Ultimately, they all serve the purpose of personal growth, so we could say personal growth encompasses the other three. In the cases I've encountered, when clients don't have major issues in the other three areas, their goal is often simply to understand themselves better to support their own growth."


And among the different groups she has worked with, when discussing the psychological roots of problems, she also believes: "Even though their issues differ, from a psychological perspective, the general cause always comes back to ourselves. Your true self, your traits, your upbringing—all merge to form who you are, shaping your behavioral logic."


As we talked, we began to realize: the cure that brings new life in the Gobi Desert is not only the shady oasis, but also the soul filled with belief that continuously heals itself. For Gezhou, healing others often means helping them gain the ability to heal themselves.


This is especially true for girls.




Remember to wear the clothes in your closet more beautifully.


"When helping women recognize and reshape their own value, we always hope to assist them in uncovering the strength within themselves, supporting them to become more stable and independent individuals, and enabling them to draw more empowerment from their past experiences. It's like how girls always want to go out and buy new clothes, even though their wardrobes are already full."


When discussing women's self-growth, Ge Zhou used a metaphor that left a deep impression on us.


"Societal restrictions and constraints on women, as well as women's internalization of these limitations, are often the root causes of women's suffering. We need to spend a long time struggling against external constraints, and we also need to struggle with ourselves. While continuously breaking through external limitations, we must also explore how to accept our inner selves and acknowledge that we are inevitably influenced by them. "


If one cannot handle oneself properly and does not know how to reconcile with oneself, they may fall into the predicament of self-denial.


"For example, when we encourage survivors of domestic violence to recognize that they actually have the strength to confront abuse, most survivors still sink into deep self-blame. They think that since they had this energy, why didn't they do better before? But in reality, even with such awareness, we are inevitably constrained by various internal and external factors. The past has already happened; what we need more is to make peace with our former selves, harness our own energy, and focus on a brighter future. "





More topics on women's psychological growth






For now, we'll pause Ge Zhou's personal story here. The following stories are about us.


It has been several years since she last worked purely in psychological counseling for women's groups. Like a return after personal growth, Ge Zhou has now become a counseling volunteer with Avoice, carrying both her original intention to heal others and a more mature set of experiences. She hopes to help women shape a better sense of self in her own way. Through this opportunity, we have also learned more about women's psychological growth.


1

Self-protection and trauma healing in women's romantic relationships


Rather than framing it as how to protect oneself and prevent harm in romantic relationships, I prefer to say it's about how to stay true to oneself in such relationships—a more positive emphasis. In a relationship, when we sense discomfort, even if others don't validate it, we must not ignore that feeling. We need to trust our intuition within the relationship and find ways to express our discomfort, communicating it with our partner. Bringing issues to light helps us set healthier boundaries. Additionally, we can seek professional counseling to better understand our situation."


Women are inherently more vulnerable than men to relational trauma. This trauma may stem from early relationships with caregivers or from intimate relationships. Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR) therapy is highly effective in healing such trauma. In this therapy, we first need to retell the traumatic experience to activate it, and through repeated retelling, professionals help survivors reframe and understand the experience, gradually desensitizing them to the trauma. Survivors may still have natural reactions to past events, but these reactions will no longer be as intense as before. After desensitization, survivors gain more psychological energy for self-healing and redefining their experiences.


2

The role of art therapy for women


On one hand, it's about expression; on the other, empowerment. No matter what art form one learns, it's like learning a different language, a different mode of expression. Language itself has limitations, while art—such as dance—can directly convey messages through body language. For me, this bodily form of expression is liberating, not to mention that physical movement itself releases many happiness-inducing hormones! Moreover, during the creative process, we can shape unique works through our individual traits. This creation doesn't necessarily have to be artistic, but it carries extraordinary personal significance—it's our own imprint, a source of self-empowerment.




Finally, everyone should become their own oasis.





Li Gezhou


  • Master of Counseling Psychology, Yeshiva University

  • Trained at Milton Erickson Foundation and William Alanson White Institute
