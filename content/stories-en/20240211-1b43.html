Domestic violence is a very serious issue for many families, yet not all victims know how to respond to or resolve domestic violence. Dependence on the abuser, the victim's indecisive personality, and various other factors can lead to unimaginable consequences. So, what are the preconditions for domestic violence, and how should we respond to it? We will explore these questions in detail in the article below.



What is domestic violence


So, what is domestic violence? Its definition is simple.


Domestic violence is a form of violence that occurs between current or former spouses in physical, psychological, sexual, or economic aspects, and it may also occur among other family members.


In addition, repeated physical violence or any other type of violent behavior also falls within the scope of domestic violence. Many people are forced to live under domestic violence—not in a safe, harmonious family based on mutual trust and love among relatives, but in a household dominated by violence, anxiety, and fear.


When people talk about domestic violence, they often imagine scenes such as a husband beating his wife or mother, or abusing children. What first comes to mind might be a man addicted to alcohol or gambling, a husband driven by extreme jealousy or excessive religious devotion. But in real life, this is not the whole picture. Perpetrators of domestic violence are often women (mothers, wives, sisters, etc.), and even children.




Types of violence against women


Domestic violence can be categorized into the following types:


One

Physical violence


This causes direct or indirect harm to the victim, primarily through beating, slapping, kicking, or hitting, resulting in physical injury. Physical punishment of any family member is a form of domestic violence. Other forms of physical abuse include denying first aid, depriving essential needs (such as bathing or using the toilet), sleep deprivation, and forcing the victim to take drugs or alcohol.


Hurting other family members or animals to psychologically affect the victim is an indirect form of physical abuse.


One of the most brutal forms of physical abuse is rape. Its consequences may include unintended pregnancy and sexually transmitted infections. Often, victims remain silent about rape due to fear of public opinion.


This type of violence usually occurs alongside other forms of domestic violence and includes any form of sexual coercion, such as persuasion, blackmail, harassment, or bribery.


In our society, marriage is often seen as giving a man unconditional power to have sex with his wife, and he is believed to have the right to use force when his wife refuses.


Coercing someone using so-called marital duties is also sexual abuse, because it does not fall under marital responsibilities. In a healthy relationship, sexual activity should occur with mutual consent and bring satisfaction to both parties.


Forms of sexual violence also include exposing genitals and pornographic material.


Two

Psychological violence


This form of domestic violence is more common than other forms and may occasionally accompany physical abuse, but it is difficult to diagnose and prove because other forms of domestic violence have obvious physical effects on victims, whereas psychological abuse rarely leaves visible signs. However, the psychological harm caused by psychological violence is still very severe.


Forms of psychological abuse include endless verbal attacks—abusers may call them ordinary criticism, sharp jokes, or other humiliating remarks. In addition, there are various prohibitions, such as banning the victim from meeting friends or relatives, or going to work.


Other manifestations of psychological violence: committing abuse without physical contact.


A separate point can be made—gaslighting. This term originates from the film *Gaslight* (1944). The film shows how one person questions another's loyalty, and when the victim cannot confirm the reality of what is happening, it drives them to madness.


That is, denial of reality in real life might sound like this:


  • "I never said that—you're making it up," "It's all in your imagination," "It never happened" (denial of facts) ;


  • "You can't possibly be angry at me," "You feel bad, but actually you don't" (denial of emotions);


  • "Look, you're acting strangely, which might lead to bad consequences"; "It's not just a bad mood—you have depression" (implying mental illness) .

Three

Economic violence


Economic violence refers to the abuser's control over household financial resources, restricting the victim's spending, and using financial means to extort or coerce the victim. In addition, economic violence includes preventing the victim from working or receiving education, and deliberately misusing household funds (to create tension).


One form of economic violence: not wanting the other partner to work.



  Where to seek help from other women? 


In real life, when there is a glimmer of hope of taking legal action, many victims still prefer to withdraw their statements and remain silent. They often worry about bringing shame to the family and do not want relatives or colleagues to know about the violence they have suffered. Any form of torture they endure is seen by them as a disgrace.


This stereotype is opposed by various organizations, which also call on everyone to stand against domestic violence and provide various forms of assistance to victims. These organizations include the Women and Children Crisis Center in Moscow—"Sisters" Independent Charitable Center for Survivors of Sexual Violence, phone: (495) 901-02-01.


At the same time, on the internet, you can also find many other women's crisis centers and choose the way that suits you best to seek help.




  How to deal with the abuser—what methods should be used to cope with the abuser? What should you do if your husband hits you? 



If possible, you need to isolate yourself from him—do not make excuses, but seek other solutions. If such opportunities are consistently unavailable, then the abuser's violent behavior should be made public.


You can:

  1. Contact the authorities: notify the local police

  2. Seek support from relatives and file a statement with the police

  3. Document injuries promptly

  4. Inform neighbors; if they hear screams, ask them to call the police immediately


In situations where danger may occur, please:

  1. Leave the house quickly, and keep your keys, phone, money, and important documents in a safe and easily accessible place. This way, when you need to leave, you can grab them quickly

  2. Contact a crisis center, where you can receive legal and psychological assistance

  3. Seek shelter with friends or relatives whom the abuser cannot reach



Situations of domestic violence vary: in some cases, escaping the abuser is easier, but in others, immediate escape is not possible. In such cases, you must take self-defense measures.


Some women who are frequently beaten by their husbands are accustomed to self-defense. Sometimes, women fight back to prevent greater harm. The law defines the limits of self-defense as follows: the harm inflicted on the abuser must not exceed the harm suffered by the victim.


If a woman finds herself in a situation where she must protect her life and cannot determine the exact nature of the attack in order to respond appropriately, this is considered a "sudden attack." In such cases, the victim may protect herself by any necessary means.


If a woman is a victim of domestic violence, she should immediately contact the police so that they can take preventive measures against the abuser.


If the attacker has been injured, never attempt to remove traces of self-defense—this could be interpreted by the court as an attempt to evade responsibility. Victims do not need to clean up bloodstains or discard items used for self-defense, as such actions could be defined as intentional harm to the other party.


A victim forced into self-defense should document all details of the incident—this will make the situation more favorable for her. To prevent the situation from worsening, the victim should try to distance herself from the abuser as much as possible, even if this worsens her financial situation or causes other disadvantages—remember that life with the abuser will not end well. Take action, believe in yourself, and focus your energy on solving the problems in your life.



Domestic violence laws


The Russian legislature has made multiple attempts to pass laws defining domestic violence and establishing regulatory standards. Until 2019, no law had defined domestic violence as a legal issue. Therefore, such a law is necessary, and many advocacy groups and activists are pushing for legislation.


What does this mean?

If elderly people or women are forced to become victims of violence but are not seriously injured or killed as a result, this still falls under the category of a private prosecution case. This means the victim must go to the police or a magistrate, file a report about the assault, and prove that the violent incident actually occurred. Evidence of the beating will be photographed at a trauma center, and then a large amount of documentation must be collected. Usually, victims lack the energy and time to deal with these matters; they are often tormented by feelings of shame and cannot afford legal fees. As a result, such cases are rarely brought to court.


Russian law does not recognize the concept of domestic violence; only terms like "assault," "murder," and "death threats" exist.


Society has mixed reactions toward loosening laws related to domestic violence. Some believe it is correct—you should not send someone to prison for a single slap as in the past. Others argue that changing the law benefits abusers who do more than just slap someone yet still remain unpunished. 



Global situations 


Current status of domestic violence around the world:


Russia

Regarding under what circumstances perpetrators of domestic violence are held accountable: according to Article 116.1 of the Criminal Code of the Russian Federation, a perpetrator will only face criminal liability for domestic violence if the victim has previously filed a complaint on a similar issue with the court or police.


Regarding possible penalties for perpetrators: they may face a fine of up to three months' wages, up to 240 hours of compulsory labor, or up to three months of detention.


Regarding defining the perpetrator's responsibility: if the person is a repeat offender and repeatedly beats the victim, they may face criminal liability. However, if the incident occurs in a single context and one person is not habitually harming another, the perpetrator will only bear administrative responsibility. If the victim reports the incident for the first time, administrative responsibility applies only when the assault does not cause temporary disability or health damage to the victim.


Therefore, we can summarize as follows. Russia's 2019 law no longer treats domestic violence as a criminal offense unless it causes serious harm to the victim. If the victim seeks help for the first time, the perpetrator will face administrative responsibility. If the victim reports the incident again or seeks help from legal authorities, the victim may file charges against the perpetrator under Article 116.1 of the Criminal Code for "assault."


United States

Situations vary across countries, but in most nations, tolerance for domestic violence is very low. For example, if a person in the United States is convicted of domestic violence, they will still be prosecuted even if the victim suddenly withdraws the complaint.


Once police receive a call, officers arrive within five minutes. Since domestic violence can occasionally lead to death, rapid police response is essential. Moreover, many perpetrators are immigrants who are unaware that law enforcement agencies respond quickly.


When police arrive, they check the victim (96% of victims are women) for bruises, contusions, and other signs of injury. They inspect whether the home has been damaged (e.g., walls or furniture broken by fists). If such evidence is found, the perpetrator is arrested and detained in jail for 24 hours. During this time, even if the victim suddenly decides to withdraw the complaint, the court will still proceed with the case.


Perpetrators of domestic violence must undergo psychological treatment and are not allowed to return home before completing their sentence. Victims can apply for a "restraining order" to block phone calls or emails from the perpetrator. The perpetrator is prohibited from approaching within 20 meters of the victim. Detained individuals must comply with all conditions specified in the restraining order. These restraining orders and detention records are entered into a special criminal database, affecting the perpetrator's career, citizenship status, and more.
