Now more than ever, we need words that dare to confront pain and record the truth.


In an age of fragmented information, reading is how we repeatedly reshape our attention worn thin by short videos, and how we continually recognize our own circumstances through the stories written by others.


Today is World Book Day. On this special day, we are recommending six books.


These six books come from writers across different countries and generations, yet they all converge on one central question: How is it possible for women to survive and to tell their stories? When structural violence appears in various forms, how do women survive? How do they speak? And how do they reclaim the power to define themselves through writing?


1 | The Power of Women: A Report on the Survival of Congolese Women

Author: Denis Mukwege (Congo)

Translator: Ren Aihong

Year of publication: 2023

Douban link:

https://book.douban.com/subject/36294884/


In 1999, the "miracle doctor" Mukwege founded Panzi Hospital. Over the past two decades, he has treated more than 50,000 women who survived sexual violence—some raped in front of their families, others taken into the jungle and used as sex slaves.


Though deeply wounded in body and mind, these women have survived with extraordinary resilience in a country marked by extreme misogyny.


In this reflective and shocking work, Dr. Mukwege tells their stories. Their resilient presence shines like a powerful beam of light, piercing through the false notion of "masculinity" while also guiding and sustaining him through the turbulent periods of two Congo wars. Even after surviving six assassination attempts, Dr. Mukwege continues to stand with these women.

Douban user @Meicai Kou Rou Hu La Tang: A boy, if raised in an environment that teaches gender equality and that violence does not define masculinity, may become a feminist-conscious man like Dr. Mukwege and gradually stand alongside women; while a girl must be educated to understand that society's discipline, exploitation, and harm toward her are not her fault.


In the preface to The Power of Women, Dr. Mukwege states: "The backdrop of this book may seem somewhat dark, because the lives of many of the women in it, like my own, have been overshadowed by violence… My work is long-term, and progress is sometimes slow and frustrating.


As a doctor, I can examine patients, diagnose the root of the problem, and then resolve it through treatment or surgery. As an activist, I face the struggle of changing minds, attitudes, and behaviors. This is not a battle against disease or anatomical defects, but against a far more stubborn opponent—discrimination, ignorance, and indifference."


2 | She Is a Survivor

Author: Grace Cho (United States)

Translator: Chen Lei

Year of publication: 2024

Douban link:

https://book.douban.com/subject/36839197/


"In my life, I've had at least three mothers. In my childhood, my mother was a charming hostess, an ambitious 'Blackberry Lady' and 'Mushroom Queen.'


By my teenage years, she had developed schizophrenia and became a prisoner of auditory hallucinations. Having escaped war and immigrated to the United States, how did this once resilient and radiant woman transform into this? I began to explore the roots of her mental suffering, hoping to understand what 'killed' her. And thus, my third mother was born.


I not only discovered what brought this woman down, but also what she lived for. At the core lies a hidden family history and the drifting lives of a generation of women. I gathered the fragments about her and others, and wrote this story of survivors.


Some people wish to erase certain histories. But I know, survival is never predestined; I must speak for those whom society deems unworthy of tears."

Douban user @CaR: History takes countless forms—history books, documentaries, hometown food, a mother's scent. The author must have summoned immense courage to write all this. This book blends heavy themes like war, immigration, racial discrimination, and trauma, while also weaving in delicate elements such as family, food, grief, and the understanding and support among women.


Remembering through pain, thinking through love. Reading this book reminded me of Yang Benfen's Autumn Garden: "If no one writes down what happened, my mother's traces in this world will quickly vanish."


A daughter is initially shaped by her mother, grows up under love that is both intense and demanding, becomes herself by breaking free from that love, and ultimately becomes the person who understands her mother most deeply—viewing her mother through the most compassionate and respectful eyes. To record her mother's traces is to record oneself.


3 | Wordslut: How Women Reclaim Language

Author: Amanda Montell (United States)

Translator: Li Xin

Year of publication: 2024

Douban link: https://book.douban.com/subject/36849451/


Are swear words inherently demeaning to "her"? Can men not speak in a "feminine" way? Are women always "nagging"? Does gender affect how we speak? Does everyday language contain gender bias? Is language itself misogynistic?


In this book, linguist Amanda Montell reveals the patriarchal structures behind language and shows us how to use redefined language to create a world where everyone is respected.

Douban user @Hua Zhi'ao Bu Li Jie: The author uses her nerdy knowledge to trace the historical roots of misogyny in language systems, so much so that after closing the book, I truly became "the sharpest language ninja in the room" when listening to people speak.


I finally understood why I've always struggled to swear—the discomfort and hesitation I feel when using swear words stems from an unconscious resistance to reinforcing a power structure that does not recognize me.


Stop complaining that women's speech is full of "excuse me," "sorry," "you know," and "uh-huh"—it's because women have more empathy; she's telling you she's listening, thus moving the conversation forward!



4 | Caliban and the Witch

Author: Silvia Federici (Italy)

Translator: Gong Jin

Year of publication: 2023

Douban link: https://book.douban.com/subject/36374149/


This book centers on one core question: How do we explain the execution of hundreds of thousands of "witches" in early modern Europe? Why did the rise of capitalism coincide with a war against women?


The book is divided into five parts: the daily struggles of peasants and workers before the 15th century, the impacts of land privatization and expanding population policies, the rise of the mechanistic view of the body, the large-scale witch hunts across Europe, and the reenactment of witch-hunting strategies in the Americas alongside local women's resistance.


By revisiting this 300-year history, Federici demonstrates that the witch hunts were an expression of capitalism's rationalization of social reproduction, providing essential support for two core principles of modern social organization: labor capacity and self-ownership.


In other words, the control of women's reproduction and bodies was a key step in capitalism's primitive accumulation. Federici engages with Marx's theory of primitive accumulation and Foucault's theories of the body from a female perspective, offering a powerful challenge to traditional historical narratives and theoretical paradigms.

Douban user @Yan Shaonü: As a Marxist feminist work, it complements The Patriarchy and Capitalism—one offers theoretical analysis, the other historical origins. Many scholars studying capitalism's primitive accumulation focus on the formation and exploitation of the proletariat, yet overlook the hidden turning point in women's fates beneath it.


Federici meticulously traces how, during the historical transition from feudalism to capitalism, women's bodies became tools for labor reproduction and accumulation. Witchcraft accusations often centered on women's sexuality, contraception, and infanticide. Isn't the expulsion of women from the workforce and the control of their bodies and reproduction a form of womb envy?


As the ruling class consolidated power, the witch hunts ended—but have similar witch hunts truly disappeared today? Of course not. They merely continue in more covert forms. The war against women has never ended.


5 | A Woman's Story

Author: Annie Ernaux (France)

Translator: Guo Yumei

Year of publication: 2022

Douban link: https://book.douban.com/subject/36078195/


A Woman's Story is Annie Ernaux's account of mother and daughter, youth and aging, dreams and reality.


After her mother died of Alzheimer's disease, the author embarked on a daunting journey backward in time, attempting to capture the true woman—the woman who existed independently of her daughter, the woman born in a Normandy town and who died in a geriatric ward in a Paris suburb.


She explores the fragile yet unshakable bond between mother and daughter, the alienating worlds that separate them, and the inescapable fact that we must lose those we love.


In this calm yet powerful tribute, Ernaux seeks the greatest fairness for her mother: to portray her as she truly was. As the author says: "Now I write about my mother, as if it were my turn to give her birth again."

Douban user @Wang Xianzhang: The first sentence of this book, "My mother died," reminded me of the classic opening of Camus's The Stranger: "Mother died today. Maybe yesterday, I don't know."


Annie begins by writing about her mother's death, then circles back to recount her mother's life from the beginning. Although her writing is restrained, I can still feel her love and longing for her mother between the lines. Many parts of her book deeply resonated with me, such as this sentence: "During those years, my relationship with her was just a few return trips." This made me think of my own mother.


Ever since I started middle school, the days I spent accompanying my mother grew fewer and fewer; when I later went to university in another city, then married and settled elsewhere after graduation, it seemed that I could only drift further and further away from her.


There is no time machine in the world—every yesterday is a day we can never return to. After finishing this story, I want to connect the first and last sentences of the book and read them aloud: "My mother died, on Monday, April 7, in the geriatric ward of Pontoise Hospital. I have lost the last link to the world from which I came."


6 | *Bright Nights*

Author: Choe Un-yeong (South Korea)

Translator: Ye Lei

Year of publication: 2023

Douban link: https://book.douban.com/subject/36457094/


"After divorcing my husband, at age thirty-one I came alone to the seaside town of Hee-ryeong, where I unexpectedly met my grandmother, whom I hadn't seen in years. After initial awkwardness and silence, our lonely hearts gradually drew closer, and my grandmother and I became confidantes who shared our innermost thoughts.


In an old photo album at my grandmother's house, I discovered a woman who looked strikingly like me, leaning beside my grandmother as a young girl. One by one, vivid faces emerged from black-and-white photographs, from deeply moving letters, from distant memories—passing through an era when women's lives were as fragile as drifting weeds and as insignificant as blades of grass—until they stood before me.


The stories of my great-grandmother, grandmother, and mother have now reached me. Their lives unfold again before my eyes. Can I reach them now? Just as countless versions of the past have shaped who I am today, can I also meet the countless versions of myself from the past?"

Douban user @Jiladuo: I read it in one sitting, nearly crying several times. I remembered when I was in high school, my grandmother came to live with us for a short while. Every day at 6:30 after class, I would ride my bike home to eat the meal she had prepared. Under the dim light, she would watch me eat, making small talk now and then, before I rode back for evening study session starting at 7:30.


Riding at night was refreshing, and sometimes I even hummed songs along the way. When I first started working, I thought about living on my own and having my grandmother move in too, so we could have dinner together after work—but then I worried she might feel lonely in Beijing, since I'd be working most of the time.


These thoughts remained only in my mind, and later, she was gone. Reading this book today brought back many memories—bright memories, yet set against a backdrop of deep night. I miss my grandmother. I miss my mother. 


Recording is itself a form of resistance, and reading is the continuation of that resistance.


When you close the book, those seemingly distant Congolese women, the "witches" at the stake, the final gaze of a mother with dementia, the face of a great-grandmother in a black-and-white photograph—they all share the same longing as you: to be seen, to be understood, not to be forgotten.


May these books become lamps in your hands, illuminating those hidden corners of history, and also illuminating the untold parts of your own life.
