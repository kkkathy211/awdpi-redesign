                 Multiple Interpretations of Barbie's Female Identity

As Aqua sings in "Barbie Girl": "I'm a blond bimbo girl in a fantasy world / Dress me up, make it tight, I'm your dolly," Barbie has long existed as a symbol of objectification. This "objectification" is not only imposed from the outside, but also internalized—Barbie subconsciously "sees" and "defines" herself in this way. This article discusses not only the beauty standards associated with Barbie, but also the process by which the female derivative—Barbie—has been regarded as an object "meant to be watched."



Since 1959, after being created by Ruth Handler, Barbie became the best-selling toy on the global market. Barbie, modeled after inflatable dolls used by Nazi troops, has sold over one billion units worldwide. Today, Barbie has become a cultural icon of the United States, and even of Western society as a whole. For more than half a century, she has been continuously assigned various identities and definitions—some positive, some negative, with mixed reviews. One online entry openly states things like "It's unbelievable she's still alive at this weight!" "Holds multiple jobs, yet never seems overworked!" "Superhuman, devilishly perfect figure!" and so on. People have drawn up a blueprint of the "ideal" perfect woman, yet seem never to have considered whether these labels are reasonable.


Barbie's body is the focal point for consumers; she represents the elegant life of a young girl, exquisite clothing, and dreamlike scenarios. Despite variations in skin tone, the typical Barbie defaults to only one specific female body type—tall, slender, curvaceous, with an astonishing waist-to-hip ratio resembling that of a supermodel. Displayed in shop windows, Barbie appears dignified and elegant, yet radiates a deadly allure. In fact, Barbie has far exceeded the value of a mere doll; she defines and sells the idealized concept of the perfect woman. Barbie has brought enormous commercial profits to Mattel, and made Ruth Handler—the "mother of Barbie," as well as co-founder of Mattel—one of America's most successful female entrepreneurs. Yet Ruth Handler was contradictory: she appeared to contribute to female independence, but ultimately compromised with society, just as portrayed at the end of the film *Barbie*.



02#


Real vs. Barbie:

                 The Doll Utopia and the Path of Self-Exploration


On July 21, 2023, the live-action film *Barbie* was released, sparking widespread discussion.


In recent years, in response to increasingly diverse consumer demands, Mattel has introduced Barbies of different body types—a move some view as a marketing strategy. The film satirically reveals this tactic: when the CEO realizes that multiple body types of Barbie can generate huge profits, he immediately decides to produce and sell them. Such product adjustments may be more about adapting to market dynamics and maintaining brand competitiveness, rather than stemming from genuine social responsibility.


Before facing today's overwhelming criticism, Barbie actually represented a new trend of female liberation for a considerable period of time. The film *Barbie* opens with a tribute to Kubrick's *2001: A Space Odyssey*: in *2001*, when the ape realizes the power of a bone as a weapon, it excitedly throws it into the air. As the bone spins, the scene cuts to a highly advanced space station, symbolizing millions of years of technological evolution. Similarly, *Barbie* depicts a giant Barbie doll descending from the sky, and little girls angrily smashing their baby dolls upon seeing her—an image symbolizing women's cognitive awakening and transformation from traditional maternal roles to modern female autonomy.


As de Beauvoir said: "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman." In other words, female identity is not innate, but shaped by society and culture. Before Barbie's debut, most girl dolls were modeled after infants or young children, undoubtedly emphasizing the weight of the "maternal" role among female identities. Barbie, as the first fashion doll with an adult female figure, provided little girls with a new role model. Girls could now imitate adult daily life, experience various social scenarios, and try different role-playing, breaking free from the traditional constraints of merely mimicking motherhood. This was a tremendous leap in culture and the toy industry, marking the reshaping of female roles and identities.



Barbie exists not only as a cultural symbol, but also as a mirror reflecting social change. The revolutionary and regressive qualities embodied in her keep her constantly at the center of public discourse. Whether as a symbol of female liberation or as a representation of female objectification and market strategy, Barbie continues to provoke thought and discussion.


The film *Barbie* and Barbie's real history reflect each other, forming an in-depth exploration of female independence and autonomy. The film sets up two worlds: one is Barbie's utopian toy paradise, the other a more real and complex human world. The vast gap between these two worlds highlights the dilemma people face regarding female roles and values.


The vibrant, dazzling life of Barbie represents a false liberation for women in a seemingly female-dominated toy world. It portrays the ideal paradise and perfect state for the majority of women. Here, Barbies sing and dance day and night. In contrast to reality, men like Ken are the beautified, objectified handsome men—mere "female pursuers"—a complete reversal of traditional societal perceptions of gender relations. Barbie's world even has festivals like "Galentine's Day" that highlight female status. Yet the film's brilliance lies in its creation of a "toy-like" atmosphere: the characters' skin is plastic, vomiting and tooth-brushing involve no real substances, and all buildings are made of plastic toys. This implies that the paradise is merely a utopian illusion: the state of female dominance is not real; it is nothing but a false mirage in a toy world, a setting created by human children playing house. At the same time, the film suggests that this idealization of women is unrealistic. Barbie may have once been the idol of countless girls, but as they grow, they begin to question whether such "perfection" is truly worth pursuing.


In reality, the Barbie designed by Ruth once carried countless girls' fantasies about the future and their dreams of a Barbie life. But over time, many girls realized the vast gap between Barbie's perfect image and the real world. This nearly unattainable standard brought them psychological pressure—the heavy, inescapable burden of "you must be like this." As they grow up, girls realize that Barbie may have unintentionally conveyed an idealized image that does not match reality, and their childhood illusions and ideals are shattered and overturned. Feeling deceived, girls begin to walk the path of self-awakening.



This sense of deception explains why girls like Sasha commonly put away, discard, or even hate Barbie, even though Barbie is just an innocent doll. Sasha vents her hatred toward the protagonist Barbie—who has just arrived in the real world—in an extremely harsh manner. Her criticism of Barbie is not merely dissatisfaction with the doll, but also a questioning of the social ideologies represented by the doll. She accuses Barbie of allying with consumerism and patriarchy, causing Barbie to diverge sharply from the real image of women.


Based on this, the film introduces a crucial concept: True autonomy and independent will. The Barbie doll believes she controls a perfect, female-dominated life. But in fact, she is a product of the design, consumption, and play of Mattel, child owners, and the entire human collective—just as in *The Truman Show*, the perfect world you believe in is actually created for you by others.


This extends to Barbie's entry into the real world, triggered by her awareness of the emptiness of her "autonomous joyful state." Suddenly, during a dance, she falls into sadness, and her skin loses its toy-like texture. "Toy" is precisely the opposite of "independence"—its very existence is subordinate to humans. Barbie's "humanization" constitutes resistance against this. In the end, the transformation of Sasha and her mother negates their previous attitudes toward Barbie, also illustrating an important point—The idea of an "independent woman" that excessively rejects external beauty is not true autonomy either; it is merely another form of "being defined" that conforms to contemporary social discourse. In essence, it is no different from Barbie's former "beauty brings everything."



Women should define themselves, rather than being defined by society and others. True autonomy is not merely external independence and beauty, but also internal self-awareness and the pursuit of personal value.


03#


The Plastic Straitjacket:

                 The Overlooked Power Illusion of Barbie and Ken


In addition, the film also reveals phenomena of power, identity, and alienation in modern society.


However, the film appears overly cautious when exploring women's predicaments under patriarchy. While this may be the optimal choice for a commercial film to secure box office success, it lacks sufficient consistency and depth in its critical examination of social ills. This circular approach around the theme of "independence" ensures stable box office returns, but when it comes to deeply exploring how true self-realization can be achieved within this framework, the film clearly lacks courage. This conservative treatment may aim to cater to mainstream aesthetics and commercial considerations, but it also means the film misses a valuable opportunity to thoroughly examine and reflect on social issues.


This conservatism is reflected in the portrayal of Ken. In Barbie's story, "Ken" is Barbie's boyfriend, often depicted as her accessory or companion. The exclusion of Ken in Barbie World mirrors the real-world exclusion of women. The plot strives to show that when one group overwhelmingly dominates another, both groups inevitably become victims of the system. This domination is not expressed through bloody violence, but through a playful, satirical musical comedy format. The film focuses more on the individual's path forward within the patriarchal framework, but unfortunately, it ultimately fails to offer a coherent, practical solution. The ending line, "We are all suffering," feels more like a compromise, avoiding a deeper excavation of the root issues.


Additionally, the character Allan is particularly interesting—his presence symbolizes marginalized groups. Just as Ken is Barbie's subordinate, Allan is Ken's subordinate. Allan even has a pregnant doll as his wife, and both appear as close friends of the Barbie couple. This setup implies their "third" status within this toy society, possibly representing broader human rights issues in the Global South beyond the gender struggles in developed nations.


In the film, both Barbie and Ken exist as "castrated" individuals—deprived of sexual consciousness and granted only "illusory power." Barbie is cast in various roles, whether as a businesswoman or president, but these are merely exaggerated fantasy settings. There is no corporate intrigue or political drama in Barbie Land. After experiencing the human world and discovering the "wonders" of patriarchy, Ken launches a "counter-Barbie" movement and establishes a "patriarchal paradise" in Barbie Land. The Barbies, once in high positions and respected statuses, suddenly give up everything without reason and willingly become beer-serving girls for the Kens.


Why can the Barbies so readily accept such a drastic change without resentment?



Because the power bestowed upon them was never real. Because the Barbies have never truly held power—they were merely programmed to appear powerful. Barbie Land was not built by the Barbies, but by those who created them. They are the winners of the patriarchal system, and the happiness in Barbie Land is merely a projection of their own happiness. A female utopia is nothing more than a leftover space defined by male victors.


When does the shift begin? It begins when the Barbies regain control of Barbie Land through a series of actions, and when Ken demands to be the Supreme Judge while Barbie is only willing to offer them a lower-level government position. Once the "toys" realize the status differences brought by power, they immediately understand the ruling class's stinginess. So Barbie must leave, must return to the real world. In the film's final scene, Barbie walks into a hospital and says she wants to see her gynecologist—this is proof that Barbie wants to shed her "toy" identity and become a real person, to obtain true power.



This is an ending, and also a beginning.
