The Origin of the "International Day for the Elimination of Violence against Women"

On November 25, 1960, Rafael Trujillo, the dictator of the Dominican Republic, brutally murdered the Mirabal sisters—political activists—in an attempt to eliminate opposition and consolidate power. After the end of Trujillo's 30-year dictatorship, the First Latin American Feminist Congress in July 1981 declared November 25—the day the Mirabal sisters were killed—as a day against violence, honoring these three "eternal butterflies" for their resistance against tyranny and violence, and helping countless women living under the shadow of domestic violence transform the shame of "family shame should not be aired in public" into the courage to firmly say "no" to violence.

 

In 1993, the United Nations issued the "Declaration on the Elimination of Violence against Women," clearly defining "violence against women" as "any gender-based violence that results in, or is likely to result in, physical, sexual or psychological harm or suffering to women, including threats of such acts, coercion or arbitrary deprivation of liberty, whether occurring in public or in private life." The Declaration also stated that domestic violence against women is a violation of women's human rights and fundamental freedoms, and a consequence of gender inequality.


In 1995, the Fourth World Conference on Women was held in Beijing, where opposition to domestic violence became a major theme and a key part of the adopted Platform for Action.


On November 5, 1999, a resolution was formally adopted by the 54th United Nations General Assembly (Resolution 54/134), proposed by the Dominican Republic and supported by over 60 countries, designating November 25 each year as the "International Day for the Elimination of Violence against Women," also known as "International Day Against Domestic Violence." The day aims to eliminate violence against women, serving as a mechanism to remind governments of their responsibility to end violence against women—a global action taken to prevent tragedies. This decision by the UN General Assembly represents both a milestone achievement in the international community's long-standing efforts against gender-based violence and a starting point for deeper action.


To date, this day and its associated campaigns continue to be used by individuals and organizations worldwide as an organizing strategy to advocate for, prevent, and eliminate violence against women and girls.
