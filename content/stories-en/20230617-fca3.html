Article Summary

A woman's successful title in certain fields often means entering the male world, which entails learning the language, vocabulary, and culture they have created. How can one break free from the old and new cages specifically designed for women within assigned gender roles and received education? In the continuous pursuit of the "truth" of "honesty," female writer Elena Ferrante offers an answer to this question.


In this article, we will focus on the female writer Elena Ferrante. Her works have achieved great success both in Italy and beyond. Books such as *Troubling Love*, *The Days of Abandonment*, and *My Brilliant Friend* "have in some way changed and propelled" the development of the entire cultural industry. This article is an interview, but also a "temporary conversation" with Elena Ferrante. She stated: "This conversation is precious, as it allows me to clarify the image of myself as a writer."


Elena Ferrante's books displayed on the shelves of a Harvard Book Store, Massachusetts


In 1992, Elena Ferrante made her literary debut with the novel *Troubling Love*. Mario Martone adapted it into a film of the same name. In 2006, *The Lost Daughter* was published. Maggie Gyllenhaal adapted and directed a film of the same name, which won the Best Screenplay Award at the 78th Venice International Film Festival in 2021. In 2011, the first volume of the "Neapolitan Quartet," *My Brilliant Friend*, was published. Saverio Costanzo drew inspiration from it to create a television series of the same name. In 2016, she was named one of the TIME 100 most influential people. In 2019, *The Lying Life of Adults* was published. Speculations about Elena Ferrante's true identity have been numerous, but none have been confirmed.

Elena Ferrante's Impact on the World

Q

Journalist

Your works have influenced the entire world in many aspects, such as film, publishing, and imagination (it is difficult to evaluate a writer using the above aspects, and I am sure you may not be interested in them either). Therefore, I would like to ask you this first question: How have your works influenced your pursuit of truth?


The concept of "truth" is too vast, and in some ways, even somewhat terrifying. Yet I am surprised by how frequently you use this word in your works and in the few interviews you have given. In your book *In the Margins: On the Pleasures of Reading and Writing*, you list "profanity" as one of the main enemies of women's truth.

The two main characters from the television series adaptation of the novel *My Brilliant Friend*


Previously, someone asked you why there was such a long gap between your first two works (*Troubling Love* and *The Days of Abandonment*). You responded: "There was actually no gap or pause. During those ten years between works, I wrote many things. But I was not satisfied with them. The content was rich and carefully considered, but they lacked truth."


A

Elena Ferrante

Everything has an impact; we live in conflict. I feel that, for better or worse, my books have given people some influence. Regarding the term "truth," I agree with you: it is a somewhat dangerous word, and perhaps I have overused it. The word "honesty" might be closer to what I mean. "Honesty" means excluding all that is fictional. But what interests me more is the struggle for truth within the realm of fiction. In the Gospel of John, Jesus calls himself the witness of truth, and Pilate ironically asks him, 'What is truth?' Today, for myself and my writing, Pilate's question does not seem so important. But the other aspect derived from it—truth about characters—seems more significant. In short, when I use the word 'truth,' what I mean is: Every good storyteller needs to write stories that are truer than so-called 'truth.'

Q

Journalist

You have been continuously writing and reflecting on writing: How do you describe the interaction between imagination and rational reflection—these two aspects of creation? Are imagination and rational reflection complementary? Do they consume your creative energy, or do you intend to express something else through them (could this dissatisfaction or unexplored part be a catalyst for creation)?


A

Elena Ferrante

In my view, they are not different: reflection is also a form of imagination. I believe reflection carves out a self from the body. Overall, for this self, writing is a primary and creative act. Without the ability to reflect, a writer would not exist. Writers write sentence after sentence, draft after draft, book after book. Perhaps it is precisely through the form of writing—through their interruptions, disappearances, and reappearances, through their embodiment in works large and small—that writers achieve creative fulfillment and coherence. This way of writing should be studied and described more deeply. In fact, the way a writer creates the self is the essential part of every narrative.



On the Female Body

Q

Journalist

Women's lives are at the center of all your works, and you have stated on multiple occasions that you believe in the existence of women's literature. Can you tell us about its nature? How can one avoid clichés when defining gender? In the film industry, when a female director produces a stronger work than average, such as Kathryn Bigelow, people often say, "She directs like a man."


A

Elena Ferrante

First, I would ask myself why saying a woman "directs like a man" sounds somewhat like a compliment, while saying a man "directs like a woman" is almost an insult. This use of language is very interesting. It immediately makes us women realize that entering the world with certain titles means entering the male world, which means learning the language, vocabulary, and culture they have created.



There are only two paths to success: either become an idol, a powerful figure, according to the standards they shape for their own needs; or constantly prove that we know how to thrive within the territory they have enclosed, so that they say: well done, you deserve praise, you understand how to express yourself in all fields just like us, and even outperform us in some cases.. Of course, there is a third path, much more difficult, but increasingly popular in this century: We strive to acquire the "tools" meticulously crafted by humanity in the best possible way, and then use them, change them, and employ them to truly express ourselves. 

But I believe that "we" as a collective does not inherently exist. The concept of "we" only emerges within our current circumstances, accompanied by the unspoken, the unsayable, the unthought, and the fragile achievements that remain perpetually at risk. Beyond these contingencies, we must entirely create the concept of "we" ourselves—this is both terrifying and beautiful.

Q

Journalist


Everyone, not only writers, belongs to the era in which they grew up, during which they experienced their first emotions and sorrows and received cultural influences. From the details and references in your novels, we can sense that you grew up in the 1950s. If you have indeed witnessed the changes in our society over the past half century, what conclusions would you draw? I am thinking of changes in gender relations and gender fluidity, especially among the younger generation.


A

Elena Ferrante


The twentieth century was a great and terrible century of transformation and destruction, yet many old things also reappeared during this time. It was a history confined within a prison, an era constantly being restructured, with blood continuously flowing within the cage. We must never forget this: every forced change and every repair is done upon living bodies. We are all beings trapped in the cage. Within local languages, birth environments, boundaries and traditions, networks of imperial authority, assigned gender roles, and received education—we are always struggling. Because of our struggles, many things, especially concerning women's status, have changed. But the regenerative power of old things remains strong and threatening.



Among all living beings, the female body faces the greatest threat: within the old and new cages built for us women, it is various conservatives and those attempting to restore the old order who ultimately triumph. Anyone or anything that can help us escape these cages should be encouraged. I appreciate gender fluidity. As long as we do not understand it as a game of moving back and forth between the cages of female and male—empty of substance, merely repeating outdated conventions, merely reviving social roles that belong to the past.


Female characters portrayed by Elena Ferrante


Q

Journalist


When I think of the "liberated" female characters in your novels, I find myself drawn, through memory, to read women writers like Colette and Willa Cather, or male writers like Flaubert. You've said elsewhere that the list of literary works you love is too long to compile, but I wonder if you could give me some more relevant insights: about crisis, about change, about transformation.


A

Elena Ferrante

I don't want to misrepresent any writer I admire, but regarding the topic you've raised, Clarice Lispector's work is essential. Her writings should be read and reread with great care.

Q

Journalist


Finally, I'm struck by the fact that you consistently conduct these non-interview dialogues via email. The conversations we publish on these web pages take the same form. These dialogues are also part of literary work—they may reveal unexplored aspects of the creative process, while simultaneously expressing a strong political stance of resisting pressure at all costs. Is that correct? What do these remote email dialogues represent for you?


A

Elena Ferrante


Gradually, over time, these dialogues have become an opportunity. In these opportunities, I can clarify, through writing, the figure I cut as a writer. The questions posed in these emails serve as a driving force for me, pushing me to search for and define my identity as a writer. Sometimes I succeed, sometimes I don't.


Q

Journalist


In the past, you've expressed the hope that Italy might have a government composed entirely of women. On the eve of the (2022) general election, what are your thoughts now?


A

Elena Ferrante


I truly would like to see a government made up entirely of women. But I hope people understand, when I speak of women, I don't mean the male stereotypes of women. I mean women who can free themselves from the powerful constraints of male-dominated political traditions. Women who know this tradition inside out, yet are able to transcend it and recreate it anew.
