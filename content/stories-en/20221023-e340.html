 Introduction:


We hope to clarify important issues such as the causes, development trends, and effective intervention methods of domestic violence through scientific research methods, thereby promoting the governance and resolution of domestic violence; however, there exist many difficult-to-fill gaps between the production and presentation of research outcomes and their practical application in social policies and interventions.

On one hand, high-quality academic research starts from clearly defined research questions and implies unique research perspectives, but to ensure rigor, scholars must sacrifice the complexity of reality. On the other hand, policymakers need to comprehensively consider multiple factors and diverse opinions— the complexity of real-world problems and the web of interests surrounding decision-making power cannot be resolved by a single high-quality academic study alone.



Non-academic impact of research





socialpolicyinstitute.wustl.edu


Research outcomes may generate "academic impact" within academic circles, or extend beyond academia to produce "non-academic impact" (Wang Nan & Luo Junwen, 2020).


For example, Scholar A studied the psychological characteristics of perpetrators in intimate partner violence and published three papers based on this research, which together received over 200 citations—such metrics as publication count and citation numbers can be seen as the "academic impact" of the research. At the same time, based on this research, journalist R wrote a popular science article that sparked online community discussions about "intimate partner violence" and "domestic violence," thus generating the research's "non-academic impact."


Broadly speaking, "non-academic impact" refers to "the influence, changes, and driving effects that research outcomes exert on broader society, economy, culture, public policy, healthcare, environment, and citizens' quality of life beyond academia" (Wang Nan & Luo Junwen, 2020, p.63). Although there is still disagreement over the precise definition of "non-academic impact," it is certain that the non-academic impacts of research are complex and diverse; this is especially true when we focus specifically on domestic violence and policy-making.


www.chicagobooth.edu


The causes, impacts, and consequences of domestic violence itself are highly intricate, making it inevitable to make trade-offs among different stakeholders when formulating policies, allocating funds, or evaluating interventions; Academic research can provide reference for such trade-offs, but it rarely offers a definitive answer.



The gap between research outcomes and policy implementation





cebcp.org


The Second Responder Program is a classic example of research evidence supporting policy implementation, yet failing to cover all aspects comprehensively.


Starting in the 1980s, a domestic violence intervention program known as the "Second Responder Program" began to be widely adopted in regions of the United States (NIJ, 2013): after police receive a domestic violence report, they respond as the "first responder" to handle the case; a crisis response team composed of professionals then follows up by visiting the victim's home within 72 hours or 7–14 days later as the "second responder," providing information about the nature and harms of domestic violence, victims' legal rights, and available resources. If the perpetrator is present during the home visit, the crisis response team also informs them of the legal consequences of domestic violence.


The ultimate goal of the Second Responder Program is to reduce the recidivism rate of domestic violence by informing victims of available resources and helping them develop long-term safety plans, enabling them to leave violent relationships, or, even if they continue to maintain intimate relationships with perpetrators, to learn coping strategies through social services and legal assistance (NIJ, 2013). This intervention is designed based on a common-sense assumption: domestic violence has a high recidivism rate, and victims are more likely to accept assistance that helps prevent secondary harm shortly after experiencing actual injury (NIJ, 2013).


Theoretically, this assumption is supported by scientific evidence. For instance, the high recidivism rate of domestic violence is an established fact globally; meanwhile, scholars have found that simple police intervention is rarely effective in addressing recurring violence risks. Multi-faceted support services involving the judiciary, social work, and psychological counseling—helping victims become less psychologically and economically dependent on perpetrators—are comparatively more effective in protecting victims from repeated harm, and such interventions need to occur shortly after the violent incident to achieve optimal support outcomes (Petersen et al., 2022).


So, do you think that arranging for professionals to visit victims at home after domestic violence occurs can effectively prevent domestic violence from happening again?

A. Yes, it is effective

B. No, it is ineffective

C. No, it is harmful


This question is taken from a WeChat article aimed at promoting effective philanthropy (He666, 2022). The correct answer provided by the article's author is: C. No, it is harmful.


At first glance, the more logically plausible answer seems to be "A. Yes, it is effective"; however, the author also provides their reasoning for choosing "C. No, it is harmful":



Yet, when connecting back to our earlier brief on the relationship between research outcomes and their societal application, this question may not be answerable as a multiple-choice item.


First, from the perspective of research data support, both views—supporting the effectiveness (Foundation, 2009) and ineffectiveness (Davis et al., 2009) of the Second Responder Program—have academic backing; overall, arguments against the program's effectiveness are based on more systematic research (Davis et al., 2008; Petersen et al., 2022). The article primarily draws from Davis et al. (2008), a meta-analysis in which the authors argue that current experimental data do not support the claim that the Second Responder Program effectively reduces domestic violence recidivism. Furthermore, in a more recent analysis, the authors call for (Petersen et al., 2022) prioritizing funding and grants for interventions already proven effective, in the absence of solid data demonstrating the Second Responder Program's efficacy in reducing recidivism.


Therefore, based solely on the conclusions of this meta-analysis, the authors merely suggest that the Second Responder Program may be ineffective in preventing domestic violence recidivism, but they do not prove it to be harmful; Second, the authors also note that the program's effectiveness is influenced by multiple factors, such as response time and follow-up evaluation systems. Currently, it appears that intervention soon after the incident (e.g., within hours) is more effective. Additionally, tracking the recurrence of domestic violence within the same household or tracking the same victim's experience of repeated abuse over time (possibly from the same or a new perpetrator) may yield different results. However, these require further research data, and no definitive conclusions can yet be drawn. It is also imaginable that variations in the professional competence of crisis response teams and disparities in available support resources across regions could affect the program's implementation outcomes in different areas.


Meanwhile, other scholars have also suggested that the broader social context significantly influences the effectiveness of interventions in domestic violence cases. That is, when assessing whether an intervention is effective, we cannot ignore the broader social environment in which the intervention policy is implemented: providing women with education and employment opportunities does not always yield the expected positive outcomes. In societies with relatively equal gender relations, such resources may empower women; however, in environments that have not yet achieved gender equality, providing such resources may trigger backlash—some men may escalate violence due to perceived threats to their power and resources (Kiani et al., 2021). Moreover, these findings are based solely within a binary gender framework; when we consider domestic violence from perspectives of diverse genders and sexual orientations, might we arrive at different conclusions?


However, the phrasing in this WeChat article inevitably leads readers to interpret it as: "Arranging for professionals to visit victims at home after domestic violence occurs is harmful to the victims." For instance, the reason for increased reporting frequency is not actually the research question addressed in the cited analysis; that is, while the authors mention possible hypotheses about why reporting frequency might increase, there is currently no empirical evidence supporting any of these claims.


We use this example not to argue that the WeChat article is wrong: the article's presentation aims to encourage people to invest charitable funds more rationally into projects with proven effectiveness. Our purpose in writing, however, is to explore ways of presenting academic findings to the public in a more comprehensive manner, and to promote the effective integration of research outcomes into policy design and implementation considerations.


Therefore, when attempting to translate research outcomes into assessments of policy effectiveness, we must establish appropriate contexts to ensure the rationality and validity of our application of research.


More importantly, presenting this issue in the form of a multiple-choice question inevitably oversimplifies the complex issue of domestic violence.



The complexity of domestic violence and application cases





Different countries and regions have varying legal definitions of what constitutes domestic violence; what kinds of relationships qualify as abusive and cause harm to victims? Academic research has reached some consensus on this— the essence of domestic violence is power and control (Stark, 2007). Environments characterized by power imbalances and extreme controlling behaviors are the "fertile ground" where domestic violence thrives. Some specific, common situations include:

  • physical violence such as punching and kicking

  • economic control—for example, the abuser controls all financial resources, and/or restricts the survivor's spending

  • psychological abuse—for example, persistent belittling or undermining of the survivor

  • social control—for example, restricting the survivor's social circle

  • sexual abuse—for example, forcing the survivor into sexual acts

  • controlling the survivor's daily clothing choices

  • threatening the survivor that if they defy the abuser's will, they or their friends and family will pay a price

  • among gender and sexual minorities, the abuser threatens to expose the survivor's sexual orientation or gender identity to others, such as family or employers, unless the survivor silently endures the abuse

So, is helping survivors leave the abusive environment the optimal solution to domestic violence?


Yes, if:

  • the survivor has full economic independence

  • the survivor is unafraid of public attention or gossip

  • the survivor has the financial ability to support their children or other family members

  • the survivor is mentally prepared for temporary independent living

  • the survivor is emotionally ready to independently support their children or other family members

  • the survivor has a clear understanding of domestic violence and a strong support network, so as not to enter another abusive relationship

  • the survivor is able to give up all previous social resources and networks

  • the survivor can move beyond the shadow of abuse

  • .......

At the same time:

  • the abuser will not stalk the survivor after separation

  • the abuser will not harbor intentions of revenge

  • the abuser will not carry out retaliatory acts against the survivor

  • the abuser will not retaliate against the survivor's family or friends

  • the abuser will not escalate harm toward future victims

  • .......

Considering all the conditions listed above, when revisiting the question, "Can assigning someone to visit a survivor at home after domestic violence effectively prevent future incidents?" it becomes clear that without a proper context, the answer to this question cannot easily be categorized as "effective," "ineffective," or "harmful." , Because "assigning someone to visit a survivor at home after domestic violence" is merely one component of a comprehensive support system needed to address domestic violence.


nysenate.gov


In 2019, New York State enacted the Domestic Violence Survivors Justice Act (Persaud, 2019), allowing judges to impose reduced sentences in cases where domestic violence survivors kill their abusers. In domestic violence situations, survivors often live in constant fear and threat; many only find the courage and ability to resist when the abuser is asleep, intoxicated, or otherwise incapacitated. Is killing an abuser while they are asleep considered self-defense? This is an interdisciplinary issue that continues to be debated. Although controversial, the current consensus is that these survivors should not be sentenced the same as in ordinary murder cases.


This represents a relatively optimistic application of understanding the complexity of domestic violence.


In academic research, solving the "what should be done" question is always the most difficult. But academic research is never a manual for real-life guidance; it offers unique perspectives on understanding the world, and inspires an unyielding spirit to explore specific fields. Therefore, when ordinary readers encounter popular science articles published on non-academic platforms, we hope that we can critically examine any text that draws firm conclusions without laying out specific contexts. And if we are not merely bystanders, but also members of organizations working to address domestic violence, bridging the gap between academic research and policy implementation is a responsibility we cannot avoid.
