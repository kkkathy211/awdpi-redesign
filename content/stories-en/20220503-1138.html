Introduction


Domestic violence as a pervasive social issue, is an undeniable part of Indonesia's current human rights agenda. According to reports from Indonesia's National Commission, the global COVID-19 pandemic has further exacerbated this problem. Data shows that from January to May 2019, Indonesia recorded 892 cases of reported domestic violence, most occurring after the implementation of lockdown policies, while many more unreported cases remain hidden across Indonesian society. Historically, the Chinese community has been an indispensable force in Indonesia's social and economic development, and in the context of the new era, Indonesian Chinese continue to play a significant role in areas such as commerce and cultural exchange. Therefore, paying attention to the living conditions and legal rights of Chinese women is of urgent practical importance. This study focuses on the current situation of domestic violence against Chinese women in Indonesia and explores pathways for self-rescue or seeking social assistance, with attention to existing policies protecting women's rights and laws related to domestic violence, aiming to popularize policy knowledge among local Chinese women and provide strong guidance for self-help..




Domestic Violence Against Chinese Women in Indonesia


Demographic Composition


Indonesia (hereinafter referred to as "Indonesia"), as the world's fourth most populous country, has over 300 distinct ethnic groups and language communities. According to the latest 2020 census data, Indonesia has a total population of 270.2 million (Statistics Indonesia, 2021). Recent data indicates that the Chinese community makes up approximately 3% of Indonesia's total population and holds strong influence in commercial sectors (2022). There is no exact record of when the first Chinese immigrants arrived in the Indonesian archipelago. By the time the Dutch arrived, Chinese people were already living in many parts of Indonesia. The older generation born in China who later migrated to Indonesia still live in Indonesia today, generally aged between 70 and 80, known as "totok Chinese" (Irianto, 2012). Present-day Indonesian Chinese trace their ancestral origins to various regions, primarily Southern Fujian (Minnan), Chaozhou, Hakka, and Guangdong, and reside across different regions and islands of Indonesia. For example, Minnan people mainly live on Java and Sumatra, Chaozhou people have settled in Java and Sumatra (especially Riau Islands), Hakka people reside in West Kalimantan, and Guangdong people are spread across various islands (Arifin et al., 2017). It is worth noting that ethnic Chinese are not part of Indonesia's mainstream ethnic groups, and it was not until 2000 that census data or nationwide surveys on ethnic Chinese became available. Based on researchers' analysis of the 2010 Indonesian census data, ethnic Chinese then accounted for 1.2% of the total population, making them the 15th largest ethnic group. (Cunningham et al., 2019).



Therefore, in a society as ethnically diverse as Indonesia, studying domestic violence against Chinese women must consider the multidimensional context of race, religion, and gender, yet the lack of data and information makes it particularly difficult to depict the real situation of abused ethnic Chinese women in Indonesia. The challenges faced by Chinese women in Indonesia stem not only from internal family and intimate relationships themselves, but also from a complex sociocultural environment, including deep-rooted anti-Chinese sentiment and racial discrimination, dominant local religious cultures, and broader, macro-level issues concerning women's and children's human rights. Research indicates that ethnic Chinese women in Indonesia often suffer from extreme racial discrimination. During the riots on May 13 and 14, 1998, not only were shops and buildings severely damaged and looted, but Indonesian Chinese women were also subjected to brutal sexual violence. Racial and gender-based discrimination against Chinese people in Indonesian society reached its peak during the "New Order period" (the New Order regime) (Irianto, 2012).


Domestic Violence Issue


In Indonesia, the most commonly used term to describe "domestic violence" is kekerasan terhadap perempuan (violence against women), often abbreviated in media as KDRT "KTP". This term is the official one used in Indonesia's groundbreaking 2004 anti-domestic violence legislation—Law No. 23/2004 (titled "Penghapusan Kekerasan Dalam Rumah Tangga," meaning "Elimination of Domestic Violence"). For women's rights protection, the enactment and implementation of this law marked a milestone breakthrough. It defines domestic violence as:


 "Any act that results in physical, sexual, or psychological suffering or neglect towards a person, especially women, within a family context, including any threat, use of force, or restriction of freedom carried out in violation of the law."


"violence is any act against a person, especially a woman, resulting in physical, sexual, psychological, and/or abandonment of the physical, sexual, psychological and/or neglect of the household, including the act of threatening unlawful conduct, coercion, or deprivation of liberty within the scope of the household." Forms of domestic violence according to Law No. 23 of 2004 "include physical violence, psychic violence, sexual violence, or household neglect."


The annual report of Indonesia's National Commission on Violence Against Women indicates that, based on 2019 reporting, various forms of violence against women in Indonesia increased, 6%with the majority being domestic violence cases. Due to stigma, shame, and lack of social support, many victims do not report abuse. The outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic and a series of lockdown policies have worsened the domestic violence situation. According to the National Commission's report, from January to May 2019, a total of 892 cases of domestic violence were reported, most occurring after the implementation of lockdown policies (CEDAW, 2019).



Rape and domestic violence remain significant and undeniable parts of Indonesia's human rights issues. One in every three Indonesian women aged 15 to 64 has experienced physical or sexual violence from an intimate partner. Compared to rural women, urban women are more frequently victims, and nearly 40% of victimized women have a high school education or higher, while about 36%of the victims are unemployed (CEDAW, 2019). Research shows that although Indonesian law prescribes prison sentences of 4 to 14 years for rape, in actual judicial practice, attempted rape is often punished leniently, and many convicted rapists receive only the minimum sentence. Notably, marital rape is not classified as a criminal offense, but is categorized under domestic violence as "forced sexual intercourse" (forced sexual intercourse in national legislation on domestic violence) and is subject to criminal penalties. Due to the lack of legal enforcement and protective measures, many divorced women do not receive alimony, and the law requires divorced women to wait 40 days before remarrying, while men face no such requirement.



To support and protect women who seek legal recourse, especially victims of gender-based violence, the Indonesian government issued Supreme Court Regulation No. 3 of 2017 (Perma)—the "Guidelines for the Trial of Cases Involving Women Offenders." This regulation covers civil and criminal cases involving women as victims, defendants, and witnesses. "Perma" attempts to counter existing gender bias in courts and judicial procedures, but in practice, its effectiveness is questionable, and some Indonesian feminists have criticized it: the existence of the "special treatment" provisions in "Perma" contradicts the Judicial Code of Ethics and Guidelines for Judicial Behaviour, which require judges to remain neutral in trials, while "Perma" demands the opposite (Saraswati, 2020). When the criminal justice system fails to provide effective support, the Family Court becomes the last resort for abused women seeking self-help. However, in reality, due to inconsistent implementation of specific regulations and differences in women's identities (such as whether they are Muslim, plaintiffs, or civil servants' wives), victims' rights are not fully protected, and actual judicial practices still favor men.


Self-Help Measures for Chinese Women in Indonesia Facing Domestic Violence


Based on limited available literature, assistance policies, and legal provisions related to domestic violence in Indonesia, this report provides the following possible self-help measures, listed in chronological order, for victims to adopt.


Seeking Assistance


(a) Call Indonesia's emergency police hotline 110, and report the incident to the police promptly.


Victims should report domestic violence to the police immediately; police records will serve as one of the key pieces of evidence.


(b) Together with the police, apply to the court for a temporary protection order.


Within twenty-four hours of becoming aware of or receiving a domestic violence report, the police must immediately provide temporary protection to the victim. Before implementing temporary protection, the police must apply to the court for a protection order. Temporary protection lasts up to seven days and begins within twenty-four hours of the police receiving or processing the domestic violence report. During temporary protection, police may collaborate with medical personnel, social workers, volunteers, and/or mental health counselors to assist the victim. The Chief Justice of the Supreme Court must issue an order containing instructions to protect the victim and other family members within seven days of receiving the police application (Arief, 2018).


(c) Call Indonesia's domestic violence assistance hotline: (0274) 553333 Seek assistance.



 Evidence Collection


Evidence of domestic violence plays a crucial role in proving the occurrence or imminent danger of abuse and can strongly support a victim's pursuit of legal aid. Collecting and preserving evidence immediately after an incident can maximize its integrity and effectiveness, increasing the likelihood that women will receive the legal assistance they deserve. Given that Indonesia's legal provisions on domestic violence still require improvement and courts tend to impose lenient penalties on perpetrators, the following evidence collection methods are intended to help victims increase their chances of success when using legal means to defend their rights.



Evidence collection may include:


(a) police dispatch records, police interrogation transcripts, and injury assessment results;


(b) audio-visual materials of the domestic violence incident, including audio or video recordings of the perpetrator committing domestic violence, as well as photographs or videos of bodily injuries and the scene of destruction. During the incident, victims should, without escalating the violence or endangering their lives, try to record the entire process via audio or video, and immediately after the incident, when safe, document the scene and their injuries using photos or videos.


(c) medical records or photos, and receipts for medical expenses;


(d) witness testimony, statements from minor children, and records of the victim's own testimony, ask neighbors, colleagues, minor children, or others who witnessed or heard the domestic violence to testify in court.

Their testimony can serve as one of the pieces of evidence proving the existence of domestic violence, to be used together with other evidence. In addition to witnessing or hearing the act of violence, witnesses who have seen the scene of the violence or the victim's injuries can also provide testimony.


(e) Any audio recordings or written records made by the perpetrator admitting to the facts of domestic violence, including confessions, messages, or written statements.



Medical and psychological treatment for physical and emotional trauma


After domestic violence occurs, victims should promptly seek medical treatment to avoid potential irreversible harm due to delayed care. In addition to physical injuries, victims should also pay attention to psychological trauma and seek timely counseling from mental health professionals. At the same time, victims are encouraged to actively communicate with trusted and close individuals to receive emotional comfort and support, and maintain a positive and healthy mindset when seeking legal assistance, never giving up any opportunity to protect their rights. If victims are unable to independently access medical or psychological counseling resources due to physical or financial constraints, they may contact local Indonesian nonprofit organizations that support victims of domestic violence for assistance (see below for an introduction to domestic violence support organizations in Indonesia).




Support measures for Chinese Indonesian women experiencing domestic violence


Domestic violence support policies and relevant legal provisions in Indonesia


Law No. 23/2004 (titled "Penghapusan Kekerasan Dalam Rumah Tangga," meaning "Elimination of Domestic Violence") defines domestic violence as "any act against a person, especially women, within a household that causes physical, sexual, or psychological suffering or neglect, including any threat, use of force, or restriction of freedom carried out in violation of the law." Article 2(1) specifies that the household includes husbands, wives, and children, as well as individuals related by blood, marriage, breastfeeding, guardianship, custody, residence in the household, or working for the household. Furthermore, the definition includes individuals who have resided in a household and worked there for a period of time and are regarded as household members.


According to Indonesia's 1945 Constitution and the Pancasila state philosophy (the Indonesian state philosophy of Pancasila), the Indonesian government and communities share the responsibility to prevent domestic violence, protect victims, and punish perpetrators. Additionally, the law stipulates that law enforcement officers, health workers, social workers, volunteers, and mental health counselors are obligated to protect and assist victims, making them more sensitive to and responsive to any actions that may threaten family harmony and interests, rather than remaining silent (Ghofur & Susilo, 2017; Arief, 2018; Susilowati, 2018; Arif, 2018).



Under Article 12(1) of Law No. 23/2004, "the government, as the responsible party in preventing domestic violence, shall (a) formulate policies to eliminate domestic violence; (b) organize information exchange and education on domestic violence issues; (c) organize socialization campaigns on the prevention of domestic violence; (d) conduct gender-related education and training to address domestic violence, and establish gender-based service standards and certification methods."


According to Article 15, "any person who hears, sees, or knows of domestic violence occurring shall, to the best of their ability, strive to: (a) prevent ongoing acts of domestic violence; (b) provide protection to the victim; (c) offer emergency assistance; (d) assist the victim in submitting applications for protective measures."


Within twenty-four hours of becoming aware of or receiving a report of domestic violence, the police must immediately provide temporary protection to the victim. This temporary protection lasts for a maximum of seven days and begins within twenty-four hours after the police receive or process the report. Before implementing temporary protection, the police must request a protection order from the court. While providing temporary protection, the police may collaborate with medical personnel, social workers, volunteers, and/or mental health counselors to assist the victim. The Chief Justice of the Supreme Court must issue an order containing instructions to protect the victim and other family members within seven days of receiving the police request (Arief, 2018).


Law No. 23/2004 does not include provisions allowing victims to claim compensation for bodily harm, property damage, or financial loss. However, it does outline victim rights: victims have the right to (a) protection from family members, police, prosecutors, courts, lawyers, social institutions, or other parties, which may be temporary protection or protection granted under a court-issued protection order; (b) health services based on medical needs; (c) control over their own confidential information; (d) assistance from social workers and legal aid providers at every stage of case review, as stipulated by law; and (e) psychological counseling services (Arief, 2018).


Introduction to domestic violence support organizations in Indonesia


KontraS (Commission for the Disappeared and Victims of Violence)



Basic Introduction


KontraS (Commission for the Disappeared and Victims of Violence) was established on March 20, 1998, as a special task force composed of civil society organizations and community leaders dedicated to addressing cases of forced disappearance and protecting victims of violence. The task force was initially known as KIP-HAM (Independent Human Rights Monitoring Committee), founded in 1996. As a committee committed to monitoring human rights issues, KIP-HAM received numerous complaints and input from communities, including from victims of violence and individuals seeking to improve local human rights conditions. Both victims and communities expressed the need for a dedicated institution to address cases of forced disappearance and continuously respond to violence in Indonesia. At that time, a mother named Tuti Koto proposed the establishment of this special institution. Ultimately, KontraS (Commission for the Disappeared and Victims of Violence) was formed to address cases of forced disappearance and protect victims of violence..


Today, KontraS not only helps resolve cases of abduction and forced disappearance in Indonesia but also handles various forms of violence occurring in Aceh, Papua, East Timor, Maluku, Sambas, Sampit, and Poso. Furthermore, after evolving into an independent organization, it has also engaged in addressing violence and human rights violations resulting from abuse of power.


KontraS's vision is: that people's human rights will no longer be violated in any form—such as fear, oppression, or violence—regardless of the reason, including gender-based causes, and that democracy will be realized based on the full sovereignty of the people.


KontraS's mission is: to raise public awareness about the importance of respecting human rights, especially regarding various forms of violence and serious human rights violations caused by abuse of state power; to hold the state accountable through various advocacy efforts, seeking justice and accountability for all forms of violence and serious human rights violations; and to encourage continuous reform of national legal and political systems to protect people from all forms of violence and human rights abuses.

Institut Perempuan (Indonesian Women's Institute)



Basic Introduction


Institut Perempuan (Indonesian Women's Institute) was established on August 9, 1998, as a women's organization fighting for the rights of Indonesian women. The organization believes that achieving justice, equality, and humanitarian values for women is an essential component of maintaining democratic life and human dignity.



The organization's mission is to fight for and defend women's rights through the women's movement to achieve a life of justice, equality, and humanitarian values..


The organization's goals are to advance the women's movement on equal footing with other people-centered movements in community and national life..


The organization's main operational programs are feminist education, women's empowerment, victim protection, and values advocacy..



Feminist Education


The institute uses feminism as an ideology to help women develop consciousness and build relevant skills. Feminist critical education refers to critical pedagogy conducted within institutional frameworks that oppress and treat women unequally. This education aims to awaken women's consciousness, transform women's thinking, and thereby advance the women's rights movement..


The program organizes community outreach activities involving diverse social actors—such as grassroots activists, law enforcement officers, government officials, and students—in training on gender and feminist thought, exploring and disseminating feminist knowledge and values across political, social, cultural, legal, human rights, economic, psychosocial, artistic, and literary themes.


One of the most common themes of the program is organizing discussions, workshops, trainings, research, and module development on violence against women and children. Additionally, the program runs feminist schools specifically for community activists and offers feminist courses for members of society interested in women's and feminist issues.



Women's Empowerment


This program is dedicated to protecting victims of violence and providing diverse services, including psychological support, legal aid, social services, and childcare. The program operates by establishing grassroots community organizations in various regions of West Java Province. Given that women and children in West Java are particularly vulnerable to human trafficking and labor rights violations as migrant workers, the program focuses specifically on addressing violence and rights violations in West Java.


Since the project recognizes that women's economic development issues arise within the global economic hegemony system that oppresses women, at the community level, it aims to establish regional women's empowerment economic systems to effectively improve the ongoing exploitation threatening women and children locally.



Furthermore, to initiate independent and autonomous women's movements, the project is actively exploring sustainable operational models, such as producing and selling items like T-shirts, traditional fabrics, accessories, and books to generate more funding.

LRC-KJHAM



Basic Introduction


LRC-KJHAM is a non-governmental organization established on July 24, 1999, dedicated to addressing serious human rights issues affecting Indonesian women..



LRC-KJHAM promotes gender equality by intervening in Indonesia's regulations, law enforcement, and policy-making, including advancing gender justice in public life and within families. At the same time, LRC-KJHAM provides women's assistance and counseling services, encourages research that advocates for legal and policy changes, promotes feminist education, and monitors violations of women's rights.


The vision and mission of LRC-KJHAM are: To achieve gender justice by strengthening poor and marginalized women's access to legal and human rights resources..


The mission of LRC-KJHAM is: To enhance vulnerable women's access to legal aid for gender justice; promote the dissemination of feminist knowledge; strengthen the women's movement as a force for social change; and strengthen accountability in organizational governance.

Summary and Outlook


In a multi-ethnic society like Indonesia, studying domestic violence against Chinese Indonesian women must take into account the multidimensional context of race, religion, and gender. The challenges faced by Chinese Indonesian women stem not only from within their families and intimate relationships, but also from a complex sociocultural environment, including deep-rooted anti-Chinese sentiment and racial discrimination, mainstream local religious culture, and the broader, more intricate human rights issues concerning women and children. Domestic violence against Chinese Indonesian women not only harms individual women, destroys families, and affects society, but the social discourse and stereotypes arising from such violence also undermine friendly relations between the Indonesian and Chinese peoples.


Currently, the implementation of the Belt and Road Initiative is steadily advancing, and as China is Indonesia's largest trading partner, the relationship between the two countries is growing closer. As of 2018, nearly 40 Chinese enterprises have been established in Indonesia's Central Java, East Java, Yogyakarta Special Region, Maluku, and North Maluku provinces, with investments totaling approximately 600 million USD and employing around 13,000 people, of whom fewer than 1,000 are Chinese nationals. Chinese companies adhere to the development philosophy of "giving a man a fish and you feed him for a day; teach a man to fish and you feed him for a lifetime" and "taking from the people, giving back to the people," training local employees and giving back to local communities (Luo Yang, 2019). Moreover, the Belt and Road Initiative has brought numerous development opportunities for women in host countries along the route and effectively increased female employment rates in these countries (Han Shenchao & Xu Shan, 2020). Lu Meini, Minister-Counselor at the Embassy of Indonesia in China, mentioned in her keynote speech at the 2021 China-ASEAN Women's Forum titled "Gathering Women's Strength to Jointly Build the Belt and Road" that "we need to carry out various practical training and exchanges under the framework of China-ASEAN cooperation to help empower women." In her speech, she encouraged women to actively strive for fair and sustainable multi-channel participation in economic and social development and to benefit from Belt and Road construction (China Women's News, 2021).



Besides advancing Sino-Indonesian relations through joint efforts in the Belt and Road Initiative, improving Indonesia's legal system related to domestic violence, and strengthening local gender education to prevent domestic violence, it is also crucial to help each individual domestic violence victim break the silence, seek timely self-help, protect their legal rights, and access information and support services—matters that directly affect the well-being and happiness of every individual. Therefore, this article focuses on self-help methods and support measures for Chinese Indonesian women experiencing domestic violence, aiming to minimize the harm from each incident and ensure victims receive timely protection and assistance.


Although Indonesia has enacted specific legislation on domestic violence, incomplete legal provisions, lack of methodological guidance in practical implementation, and deeply entrenched gender inequality in Indonesian society mean that Indonesia's efforts to combat domestic violence remain long and arduous. We hope that both official and non-official institutions in Indonesia will pay greater attention to establishing and improving domestic violence support systems, so that all Indonesian women can receive more respect and fair treatment..
