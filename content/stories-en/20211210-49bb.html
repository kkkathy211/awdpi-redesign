Research Report on Domestic Violence against Chinese American Women

(Part Two)

December 2021



Four, Comparison with Other Racial and Ethnic Minorities:

Taking Latinas as an Example


Current research on violence against women does not adequately represent the voices of women of color; for example, many studies overlook the presence of race and ethnicity as variables, use insufficient sample sizes and inappropriate measures, and ignore within-group heterogeneity. Therefore, when focusing on abused women from racial and ethnic minority backgrounds, we need more nuanced insights to examine experiential differences and specific circumstances across different groups..


Previous studies indicate that race and ethnicity vary significantly—for instance, Native American women are most likely to report victimization from rape and physical assault. The structural intersectional dynamics of immigrant women's nationality and citizenship status represent one aspect of identity politics and marginalization related to domestic violence. More specifically, family structure, acculturation, immigration status, community response, and histories of oppression shape the experiences of women in minority communities.


Immigration status often influences and shapes how women understand domestic violence, their access to resources, and their responses to abuse. In addition to language barriers, concerns about child custody, and the common fears that all abused individuals face before seeking social assistance, there are also race-specific considerations. However, current research on comparative analyses of domestic violence is limited and primarily focuses on comparisons between racial minorities and White populations, leaving a gap in comparative analyses among different racial minority groups.


Therefore, this section focuses on domestic violence research among racial minorities in the United States, aiming to conduct a comparative analysis of social help-seeking behaviors between Latina and Chinese American women, in order to further understand the unique challenges faced by Chinese American women as racial minorities experiencing domestic violence under immigration status.


This section draws upon case studies of 14 Latina and 14 Asian women (Heidi et al., 2010), analysis of intimate partner violence among Latinas (John et al., 2016), and social help-seeking analysis among Asian and Latina immigrants experiencing domestic violence (Bauer et al., 2000). It focuses on differences in concerns about seeking social assistance after experiencing domestic violence, hoping to help understand how sociopolitical and sociocultural factors influence Chinese and Latina immigrants' decisions to seek social assistance after experiencing domestic violence..


(One)

Similarities Between Chinese and Latina Immigrants Analysis


Chinese and Latina immigrants share many similarities, which lead them to make similar decisions not to seek social assistance after experiencing domestic violence due to shared sociopolitical and sociocultural influencing factors. In addition to sharing immigrant status and belonging to racial minority groups, their countries of origin have both experienced prolonged periods of colonization and anti-colonial movements, have similar cultural customs (such as paper cutting), and share many other cultural characteristics.


Sociopolitically, both Chinese and Latina survivors are reluctant to seek external help due to language barriers, fear of deportation, and lack of information about social assistance services; socioculturally, they both believe that family unity outweighs individual needs and that they have responsibilities toward raising children, and these traditional cultural values greatly hinder their help-seeking behaviors.


(Two)

Differences Between Chinese and Latina Immigrants


01

Reporting of Domestic Violence Incidents


Community surveys based on Mexican immigrant populations show that 11%up to 13% of Mexican American women are affected by partner abuse. Surveys of English-speaking Latinas indicate that the rate of domestic violence among highly acculturated Latinas is similar to that of immigrants (10%up to 17%). Additionally, some studies find that U.S.-born Latinas experience higher rates of partner abuse than immigrants, suggesting that culture may influence the occurrence of partner abuse. (Sorenson & Telles, 1991).


However, to date, there has been insufficient community or clinic-based survey research sampling a sufficient number of Chinese Americans to estimate the prevalence of partner abuse within this racial group. This reflects not only insufficient attention from the current U.S. research community to domestic violence within the Chinese American population, but also inadequate attention and research on domestic violence among racial minorities as a whole..


02

Sociopolitical Influencing Factors


First, lack of law enforcement in countries of origin. Compared to China, which has legal protections such as the Anti-Domestic Violence Law, Criminal Law, and Marriage Law, some Latina respondents come from countries where police and other institutions do not handle domestic disputes.


These women are taught that intimate partner abuse is a private matter and that victims have no recourse. After immigrating, many expect these issues will be similarly handled in the United States. This expectation makes them less likely to disclose abuse to healthcare providers or seek help. As one Latina respondent explained: "I did hide something, or more precisely, the problems I had at home [intimate partner abuse]. Because in my country, in my culture, we ignore intimate relationship problems, and I also know I have no right to protest the abuse I suffered."


Second, racial discrimination and bias. Latina respondents perceive racial discrimination and bias during healthcare delivery, a perception that is not as evident among Chinese American women.


To some extent, these women feel disconnected, powerless, and unfairly treated in healthcare settings. Under these circumstances, they are unwilling to openly discuss their abuse. One Latina respondent said: "Doctors don't ask you if you're having problems handling family matters, but I think they should at least try to be more human, because their service involves a lot of discrimination. I've noticed they change their attitude when serving White Americans—they become very kind, not 'No, go on, sit down,' which are two completely different attitudes." Although few studies have investigated healthcare providers' perceptions of racial discrimination, this may be a widespread phenomenon.


During interactions with healthcare providers, nonverbal communication (e.g., tone of voice, lack of eye contact) may allow patients to detect implicit messages, which they may interpret as discomfort or lack of trust. Regardless of the provider's intent, these experiences may negatively affect abused women's willingness to seek social assistance.


Therefore, in terms of sociopolitical influences, both Chinese and Latina racial minorities, as immigrants in the United States, face sociopolitical barriers related to social assistance services. Both Chinese and Latina women describe their social isolation, language barriers, fear of deportation, and confusion about accessing information on social assistance services.


However, a unique factor for some Latina respondents is the perception that they face racial discrimination from social assistance service providers. Combined with the lack of domestic violence enforcement in some Latin American countries of origin, this makes accessing social assistance after domestic violence even more difficult.



03

Sociocultural Background


First, the sacredness of marital bonds. Compared to Chinese women, Latina respondents believe that the sacredness of marriage and their love and loyalty to their husbands make them more willing to tolerate abuse. Some Latinas believe they have no right to complain, protest, or seek help due to the self-sacrificial nature of marriage. One Latina respondent noted, "We love love so much that for love, we are capable of covering up the truth for our husbands. To stay with our husbands, we do not report him." Another Latina explained: "I don't know either. In my country, there is only sexual violence, but violence between spouses does not exist, because we are taught that this is the cross we must bear for our husbands."


Second, religious beliefs. Religious belief is another factor that more uniquely influences Latinas' decisions to seek help for domestic violence compared to Chinese women. A 47-year-old Mexican mother, who had been with her partner for 19 years, married for 11, and had four children, said: "Yes, I pray to God to find a solution to my problems: Why is this happening? It's wrong for a woman to go from one partner to another. That is not walking with God."


Third, shame. Compared to Latina immigrant survivors, many Chinese American women are more concerned that disclosing their abuse to healthcare providers would bring shame upon themselves, their families, and their communities. Moreover, while they view their husband's violence as shameful, they also believe it reflects their own failure as a wife.


Additionally, Chinese respondents believe that external intervention could ultimately lead to the end of their marriage, and the stigma of divorce and single parenthood would make life even harder. One Chinese respondent said: "I don't want to tell anyone what happened; sharing what occurs at home with others makes me feel ashamed." Another explained: "Many Asians hold the belief that even if they endure violence, they want to keep the family intact rather than let it break apart, and they think people will view divorced individuals differently. After my divorce, a friend once told me something similar—she said she didn't want her daughter playing with children of single parents."


In social and cultural influences, due to the particularly high value both Chinese and Latino communities place on family, concerns about single parenting and societal views toward seeking social assistance can also hinder help-seeking. Family values such as devotion and sacrifice in marriage among Latino women may prevent them from reaching out for external help; meanwhile, feelings of shame and stigma around divorce among Chinese women further increase their hesitation to seek social support.


In summary, immigrant women experiencing domestic violence are not only marginalized due to gender, race, and the specific forms of abuse they endure, but also face marginalization and confront unique challenges rooted in social, cultural, and political dimensions.



V. Possible Solutions


The above discussion and current research indicate that, due to lack of social representation and objective language barriers, it is more difficult for ethnic minority women to access social support resources—such as psychological or legal information and shelters—compared to white women. It is more difficult (Netto et al., 2001).


At the same time, women who have experienced domestic violence are also more likely to develop other mental health issues, such as low mood, depression, anxiety, and suicidal thoughts. According to the U.S.-based Chinese-language media outlet World Journal, on November 6, 2019, the San Bernardino County Juvenile Dependency Court in California held a hearing on a case involving a Chinese woman who came to the U.S. after marrying her husband in 2013 and soon began suffering frequent domestic violence. Starting in 2014, due to postpartum depression and other factors, she began exhibiting symptoms such as mental confusion and other psychiatric conditions, and started taking medication. Ultimately, the court ordered her to receive treatment at a psychiatric rehabilitation hospital, while her two children were placed in government welfare facilities.


Therefore, addressing the various challenges faced by ethnic minority domestic violence survivors requires more diverse social support organizations and a more equitable and inclusive justice system..


(1)

Diverse Social Support Organizations


Diverse social support organizations mean two things:

▶ on one hand, communities need organizations that provide services in minority languages;

▶ on the other hand, survivors need safe pathways to access effective support services.


01

Linguistic Diversity


In multilingual support settings, information can be conveyed most effectively and thoroughly between service providers and users, which is critically important for survivors living in a foreign-language environment to protect their individual rights. This is because, when English is a foreign language, speakers of minority languages often feel pressured to use simpler language to quickly end or avoid conversations, creating practical barriers for relief measures and psychological counseling that require detailed communication.


Additionally, if service providers and users differ significantly in upbringing and daily habits, this may lead to a lack of cultural sensitivity toward culturally related triggers, making it difficult to effectively identify problems and offer viable solutions.


In other words, a single, mainstream support channel cannot truly understand the urgent struggles of ethnic minority female survivors through effective communication, and instead may widen the gap between ethnic minorities and support systems, intensifying survivors' distrust toward social organizations, and reducing their likelihood of seeking legitimate help. The fact that ethnic minority domestic violence survivors face unique challenges in asserting their rights due to cultural differences is precisely why it is essential to distinguish between ethnic minority survivors and white survivors as separate groups.


Therefore, communities need anti-domestic violence organizations structured by ethnicity and language to provide targeted support, bringing together survivors who are otherwise isolated in the shadows, helping them build trust and a sense of belonging toward social organizations, and transforming traditional support models into an active, interactive process; through legitimate channels, survivors can, in this new environment, advocate most effectively for their own rights.


Currently, ethnically diverse states such as California and New York have begun to establish networked systems of minority-language social support organizations, integrating multilingual services into websites, hotlines, counseling, and shelters.


However, for ethnic minority survivors living in states with relatively homogeneous populations, targeted support organizations remain limited in number. Survivors with limited financial means lack transportation to travel long distances, making such scarcity nearly equivalent to inaccessibility. Moreover, existing targeted organizations in these states often lack public institutional status, and are frequently driven solely by the personal sense of justice of their founders. These organizations often lack effective operational mechanisms, primarily reflected in the fluctuation of support conditions based on the founder's personal capacity. In an era of recurring pandemics, such instability has made support organizations and shelters even scarcer..



02

Effectiveness and Safety of Support Services


A genuine social support organization should enable survivors to access effective support services through safe channels. However, in some cases, survivors are reluctant to seek help because they risk exposing personal information during the process, which could lead to significant physical danger and increased self-rescue costs..


For example, some abusers strictly monitor the electronic devices used by survivors; if they discover a history of visiting support organization websites, they may accuse the survivor of attempting to flee or call the police, using this as justification for even greater harm. Therefore, when building support websites, an emergency exit should be placed on every page, allowing survivors to instantly jump to a safe, neutral page, while simultaneously clearing browser history..


The core of reducing the cost of support ⟧164⟧ lies in reducing the costs survivors themselves must bear when seeking help. This means the support system should start from the survivor's perspective, identify the cost components at each stage of assistance, and provide comprehensive, targeted social solutions. This requires not only attention to the differing issues caused by cultural differences, but also demands that service providers. This means that the support system should start from the victim's standpoint, identify the cost components at each stage of assistance, and provide comprehensive and targeted social solutions. In this process, in addition to paying attention to the differences in problems caused by victims' cultural backgrounds, service providers are also required to maintain sensitive and careful observation of resource utilization. If a support system fails to effectively assist survivors, its designers should reflect on whether its structure truly responds to survivors' needs and whether it offers a safe access channel..

02

The practical challenges of legal remedies


As racial diversity has brought a richer social environment to the United States, it also means that, at the legislative and judicial levels, lawmakers and legal practitioners in the U.S. need to balance the diverse interests and values represented by different races and cultures.


Since the beginning of the 21st century, overt racial discrimination has gradually decreased, but the marginalization of ethnic minorities still persists in judicial practice (The Sentencing Project, 2008). For African Americans, the impact of historical injustice is staggering. According to According to the latest 2021 national survey statistics from the NAACP, although African Americans make up only a portion of the total population, 13.4%they account for: 22%fatal police shootings, 47%wrongful exonerations, and 35% people sentenced to death. In state prisons, African Americans are incarcerated at a rate five times that of whites. Compared to prisoners of other races, African American men face disproportionate harsh experiences of incarceration.


Currently, for first-generation Asian immigrants, reporting domestic violence remains relatively low. 607 individuals, In a survey involving a sample of 607 people, 25～38%indicated that they knew at least one Asian woman who met the criteria for being a domestic violence victim (Yoshioka et al., 2000). However, despite full operation, mainstream domestic violence service agencies rarely see Asian women. For many Asian women, due to the various reasons mentioned above, domestic violence support services are inaccessible..


In terms of legislation, the reauthorization by Congress in 2013 of the "Violence Against Women Act" (VAWA) included many marginalized groups previously overlooked, adding provisions for LGBTQ individuals, Native Americans, and victims of human trafficking. By adding stalking to the list of crimes eligible for U visa protection, the legislation expanded protections for immigrant women.


However, VAWA it did not increase the number of U visas, nor did it increase government interest in researching violence within immigrant communities. According to data from the Migration Policy Institute in 2014, the total number of immigrant women in the United States was 18.9 million, accounting for 12%of the female population. Due to the lack of nationwide research investigating IPV domestic violence in immigrant communities, the number of immigrant IPV victims remains unknown (Modi et al., 2014). In other words, research on domestic violence against immigrant women from minority racial groups remains confined to academic interest and receives only localized attention. .


Addressing systemic racial inequalities in the criminal justice system fully aligns with the pursuit of public safety and a fair judicial system. In the context of domestic violence, this means the United States should provide more accessible and minority-friendly legal remedies, creating genuine equal opportunity, so that minority women, when facing the same challenges as the mainstream American population, have equal access to legal assistance and can obtain effective and welcoming judicial relief and support.



VI. Summary and Outlook


Chinese American women in the United States, as a relatively large group among local racial minorities, face not only the common challenges experienced by women suffering domestic violence but also additional unique difficulties due to their distinct circumstances.


Although individual circumstances, marital status, family structure, and other factors influence how Chinese American women address domestic violence, the help they can receive when seeking external assistance is limited. Their own weak social capital and racial minority status lead to language barriers and cultural sensitivities, as well as experiences of racial discrimination, making it difficult for them to access local social aid and social security support—such as legal intervention, public education, community activities, mental health services, and food assistance—even after experiencing domestic violence.


At the same time, complex immigration policies and laws make it not only extremely difficult for Chinese American women to obtain fair and welcoming judicial relief during the process of seeking help, but they may even face the risk of deportation, becoming a high-risk, marginalized group..


From the above discussion, it is clear that compared to white women, Chinese Americans and other racial minorities face greater difficulties in accessing assistance through formal social channels..


Therefore, to address the various challenges faced by Chinese American and other minority victims of domestic violence, the United States must not only establish more diverse social service organizations, provide multilingual support services, create effective pathways to access help, and ensure the personal safety of those seeking assistance, but also needs to create an equal and inclusive legal environment, using social and legal resources to provide Chinese American and other minority women with more comprehensive, professional, and long-term domestic violence relief programs, fully considering the interests and values of minority groups at the legislative and judicial levels, so that minority women have equally fair opportunities to access effective and welcoming judicial relief and support..


Today, the overseas pandemic situation remains uncertain, compounded by the resurgence of conservatism. Addressing the domestic violence crisis among Chinese American women during the pandemic requires further proactive responses and effective measures. At the same time, we hope for more research that offers targeted attention and analysis in light of the complex situations arising from the pandemic..


Additionally, in terms of characterizing the profile of abused Chinese American women, academic research on this population remains insufficient, making it difficult to form a unified and comprehensive understanding of their current challenges. Among these, particular attention must be paid to the fact that factors such as state government policies, local population composition, levels of public safety, and the size of Asian communities in different regions of the United States all influence the likelihood of Chinese American women experiencing domestic violence, yet these factors are often overlooked in most studies, leading to analyses of domestic violence among Chinese American women lacking more detailed discussion due to the absence of comprehensive, direct data and information..


This article, within the context of existing research comparing domestic violence among mainstream minorities and whites, attempts to conduct a comparative analysis among minority groups. It not only analyzes the similarities between Latina and Chinese American women from the perspectives of A comparative study of domestic violence among mainstream minority groups and white populations the current research landscape, attempts to conduct comparative analyses of domestic violence among different minority groups not only from social politics and social culture, but also reflects on the unique social challenges these two minority groups face when seeking social assistance for domestic violence, striving to contribute to the understanding of domestic violence among U.S. racial minorities as a whole and within individual ethnic groups, raising awareness of domestic violence issues among minorities among U.S. social service agencies and workers, researchers, and policymakers, and promoting deeper research and responses.

Avoice Overseas Chinese Women Protection Project is dedicated to helping overseas Chinese women experiencing domestic violence and improving the living conditions of vulnerable women abroad, building a bridge between women in need and various resources, becoming their solid support and warm shelter..

We emphasize self-empowerment of the target female population, while also providing multidimensional support, covering language, psychological, legal, medical, shelter, channels, education, economic assistance, storytelling and advocacy, and community support, extending the breadth of aid while creating a virtuous cycle of mutual support.

If you or someone you know has any of the following needs:

1. Requires relevant consultation and assistance

2. Wishes to receive Avoice storytelling and advocacy support

3. Wants to join the overseas Chinese women's protection community

4. Wants to collaborate or provide resources for the project
