AWDPI New York recently hosted its first in-person sharing event, inviting artist, curator, and spiritual mentor Gao Daxin as guest speaker for an in-depth discussion on art, spirituality, and contemporary female identity.


This was AWDPI's first face-to-face event in New York, with the venue filled with participants from diverse cultural, racial, and age backgrounds, while the online meeting room gathered community members from multiple countries — all drawn by curiosity about art and self-exploration, their voices in different languages intertwining in unexpected harmony, giving the event a warm sense of "connection" from the very beginning.


For the entire community, this was not just an ordinary conversation, but a starting point for women's narratives, spiritual exploration, and cross-cultural dialogue to take tangible form.



From business to creation: a transition without rupture

CREATION

Daxin's career path itself reads like a deeply reflective coming-of-age story, each step marked by clear self-inquiry. She once worked in the PR department of a luxury brand's China division and taught Chinese in the UK and the US, experiences that gave her a fresh worldview and expanded perspective; later, she returned to entrepreneurship, first launching a highly creative venture in Shanghai — during the early rise of youth culture communities, she curated a "bus + DJ" mobile urban party, a novel format that attracted massive participation, sparked viral buzz, and even drew multiple investment offers; subsequently, she founded a commercial curation agency linking art and business, leading numerous large-scale brand art crossover projects. In recent years, she has been active internationally as an independent artist, with her works presented in cities including New York, Mexico City, Chiang Mai, Shanghai, Guangdong, and Xi'an; in 2025, she will join the School of Visual Arts (SVA) in New York as a resident artist, focusing on a new phase of creation in her studio. She is now establishing Heart Haus in the US, a space dedicated to emotional healing and community connection, using art, ritual, and relational practices to create a sanctuary where people can "rest their inner selves";


These identity shifts, in her view, are not "disconnected turns," but natural extensions. During the talk, she said: "Many people see business and spirituality as opposites, but for me, the structural awareness and attention to detail gained from business actually support the realization of artistic practice; while art and spirituality help me rethink 'why I create,' guiding me back to the original intention behind my actions." Daxin shared: "Spirituality is not an escape from reality, but a way to face oneself more honestly. Past business experiences aren't discarded — they're part of my journey, a source of energy nourishing my current creativity." This clarity of not rejecting the past nor blindly following the present allows her to maintain balance across multiple identities, earning her the nickname "a clear-headed player in the game of life" — neither enslaved by business efficiency nor carried away by abstract spirituality.




"A serious player in the game of life": finding freedom within the rules

FREEDOM

When asked how she would define her attitude toward life, Daxin brought up the phrase "a serious player in the game of life," which she sees as an accurate self-description. This metaphor isn't meant to trivialize life, but rather reflects her deep respect for and active engagement with it: "The 'game' I mean isn't about taking things lightly, but about first understanding its rules — such as real-world responsibilities and social boundaries — then finding your own rhythm within them, actively experiencing life instead of passively accepting it. The key to playing a game isn't just knowing 'it's a game,' but knowing how to use the rules to enhance your experience. Life is the same." She gave an example: when working on business projects in the past, she strictly followed processes to ensure every step was executed, never betraying her team's trust; now, in artistic creation, she follows her inner intuition to adjust direction, but never uses "spirituality" as an excuse to procrastinate or cut corners — for instance, during her residency at SVA, she spends time meditating to ground herself and find inspiration, but also keeps detailed creative notes and works passionately through day and night. "Listening to the voice of your 'higher self' and fulfilling real-world responsibilities aren't contradictory. Just like playing a game, you need to understand the rules while daring to explore new ways within them — that's what it means to 'play seriously.'" This mindset gives her work both spiritual warmth and practical grounding, allowing her to maintain rare clarity in balancing "sensitivity and rationality" and "inner exploration with outer action."




"Five Choices of a 33-Year-Old Woman":

Female power hidden within an installation

POWER

At the event, Daxin spent considerable time explaining her most celebrated work — "Five Choices of a 33-Year-Old Woman." This is not a single piece, but a multidimensional art project integrating narrative, video, performance, and installation, centered on five "fictional yet real" female characters exploring contemporary women's struggles and strengths in identity, freedom, and choice, embodying her complex reflections on matriarchal societies, identity transformation, spirituality, and liberation.


These five characters come from vastly different cultural backgrounds: a woman from Oaxaca, Mexico; a woman from Chiang Mai, Thailand; a woman from Shandong, China; a woman from Adelaide, Australia; and a "Light Walker" constantly moving through global metropolises. Though their stories differ in focus, each carries a trace of Daxin herself: the Chiang Mai woman releases her strength in a boxing ring, echoing Daxin's own experience training in Muay Thai there; the character who abandons stability to create while wandering reflects her own departure from structured systems to embrace art; and the mobile "Light Walker" symbolizes her ongoing journey of self and spiritual exploration.


The work is presented through a five-tiered cabinet — the cabinet itself an old piece of furniture Daxin sourced from a New York salvage warehouse, each wooden plank bearing the marks of time; the objects placed within each layer are "treasures" she collected across different cities: handmade fabric from Chiang Mai markets, small sculptures from Mexico, old family photos from Shandong, or perhaps a seashell picked up on a California beach, making each character's "life" tangible and palpable.


"These objects may seem unrelated, but together they feel harmonious," Daxin smiled. "Just like female identity — we can be strong, and we can be soft; we can be successful, and we can be lost — these aspects aren't opposing, but together form the complete 'I.' I want this work to tell people: don't let the voice of 'you should be like this' trap you. You can have many choices. In this vast world, you might be everything, and some part of everything might be you — energy flows between us all."




Energy-based creation at Washington Square Park: connection without preconception

WASTINGTON PARK

In addition to the installation, Daxin shared another project that particularly intrigued the audience — a performance art piece titled "Soft Channel," conducted in New York's Washington Square Park. This was not an impromptu event, but a long-planned "ongoing creative project," centered on "using the body as a medium to record energetic connections between people."


The rules are simple: Daxin marks out a space, places down a canvas and a few pens, informs passersby of the intent, and invites willing participants to "co-perform" — no talking, no specific actions required, just maintaining silence and allowing energy to flow naturally. Daxin observes the person's expression, breath, and physical presence, captures the moment's energy, and translates that feeling into abstract symbols (such as lines or color blocks), directly recording it on the canvas with pen.


In response to questions like "Is this spiritual sensing?" or "Is there a special technique?", Daxin laughed and denied it: "It's not that mystical, and there's no special 'spiritual trick.' I simply empty myself, not assuming 'they might have some problem,' not deliberately thinking 'I should do this or that,' just fully following the moment. When you face someone without judgment or ulterior motive, your body 'reads' their energy before your mind does, and responds in the most natural way." She emphasized that the significance of this creation isn't about producing a perfect artwork, but about "documenting that non-verbal connection": "Sometimes, two people sitting quietly together can generate more powerful energy than endless conversation. This connection itself is art, and offers us a new insight into human relationships — even without words, as long as there is genuine acceptance, connection happens."

The heart as metaphor: from biological organ to energetic vessel

HEART

"Heart" is the central motif running through Da Xin's entire artistic practice, which she calls "Heartverse"—an image that not only reappears repeatedly in her personal creations, but also led her to collaborate with multiple brands and launch an art series centered on "ceramic hearts." Even her personal slogan is "heart," inspired by the character "xin" (heart) in her name.


One of the most representative series is centered on the Chinese philosophical concept of the "Five Elements | Fire." In this series, she revives traditional thought through contemporary expression by integrating ceramics, wool felt, ancient artifacts, digital media, and natural elements, constructing an artistic energy field where the "heart" serves as the vessel and "qi" (vital energy) as the flow. The artist consistently weaves in the concept of Heartverse, building an organic and mysterious inner universe through the pulsation of fire, the nourishment of water, the support of earth, the growth of wood, and the boundaries of metal. "In Eastern philosophy, 'fire' symbolizes light, rapid transformation, and regeneration—it can burn away the old to make space for the new, and also nurture warmth and hope," Da Xin explained. "I feel this is very much like the energy of the heart: the heart keeps beating, sustaining our physical life while carrying our emotions and spirit—it can feel 'heavy' from pain, or 'light' from joy; it can 'get hurt' and also 'heal'; it can 'grow weary' and also 'renew its strength.' This is precisely a kind of 'transformation and regeneration.'"


She particularly mentioned the "vessels (blood vessels)" within the heart: "People always say 'follow your heart,' but rarely do they think about how the heart can 'move.' Each vessel becomes a channel of consciousness—leading to the body, and also to the world."

"I know I am a 'vessel,'" Da Xin said seriously. "I don't have some lofty 'God's-eye view,' but rather serve as a bridge, a medium—acting as a conduit to help others feel the energy that already exists within themselves. My art, my work with Heart Haus, all aim to help people 'reopen' and rediscover their own strength." She also mentioned that this state of being a "vessel" brings her both calm stability and vibrant hope for life—these two qualities are not contradictory, but instead reside within the philosophy of "balance."



Extending Connections: A Sharing That Never Ends

 NEVER END

This AWDPI New York sharing event brought together many different voices: a recently graduated international student asked in Chinese about her struggle between pursuing a "stable job" and an "artistic dream," and Da Xin offered advice based on her own entrepreneurial experience; a woman who has lived in New York for many years shared in English how she saw herself reflected in "Five Choices of a 33-Year-Old Woman." Near the end of the event, a woman rushed in—she was a life coach who teaches "mandala," arriving just as the sharing was drawing to a close, yet insisted on sitting down to listen. Perhaps the significance of this gathering lies precisely in such "extensions"—it didn't end with the event, but instead became a starting point for new connections and collaborations. As Da Xin said: "Art is never a 'finished product' displayed in a gallery, but rather an energy that continuously grows through human encounters and connections."


For AWDPI, this is only a successful beginning. Everyone believes that in the future, there will be more such sharing events and more such connections, allowing the energy of art, spirituality, and women's power to flow, grow, and impact more people on a larger scale.
