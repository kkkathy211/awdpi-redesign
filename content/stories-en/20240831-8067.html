Introduction:


In July, the Olympics began on the banks of the Seine River, where golden female statues slowly rose in the rain and silver-armored female knights rode white horses through the water—progress toward gender equality in sports was inspiring. Yet globally, the situation for women remains deeply concerning. Women and girls displaced by conflict, social unrest, and natural disasters in Haiti are facing unprecedented levels of sexual and gender-based violence. In The Gambia, although female genital mutilation (FGM) was outlawed in 2015, much work remains to raise awareness about the harms of the practice and to provide comprehensive medical care for survivors.


At the same time, gender-based violence against Asians continues. The case of an Asian woman who was followed home and brutally murdered in 2022 has recently reached a verdict, with the perpetrator sentenced to 30 years to life in prison. In Utica, New York, the police shooting of a 13-year-old ethnic Karen boy from Myanmar sparked outrage in the local Burmese immigrant community, with residents demanding justice and truth. On the other hand, Asian voices on the global stage are gradually growing stronger, being heard and recognized. Chinese residents in New York City took to the streets in protest, demanding that city officials respect and respond to the needs of the Chinese community; K-pop is entering the Grammy Museum, becoming one of the largest exhibitions ever curated by the museum and a major force in K-pop's expansion into the U.S. music market; in Brazil, the government has officially acknowledged and formally apologized for the inhumane persecution suffered by Japanese immigrants after World War II. In the face of present and historical inequalities, Asians are exerting their influence across different fields, actively combating discrimination and fighting for equal rights.

Gender Equality


News


The inclusion of female symbolism at the Paris 2024 Olympic opening ceremony has drawn attention, marking the first time in Olympic history that male and female athletes are equally represented, with women's empowerment seeing comprehensive advancement in competition opportunities, media visibility, and medal contention.


At 7:30 p.m. local time on July 26, the Paris 2024 Summer Olympics officially opened on the banks of the Seine River. The female symbolism during the opening ceremony sparked widespread discussion. Accompanied by the French national anthem, "La Marseillaise," ten golden statues slowly rose in the rain, honoring ten women who made outstanding contributions in the fields of women's rights, art, science, and politics in French history. At the end of the ceremony, a female knight clad in silver armor and draped in the Olympic rings flag rode a white horse through the waters of the Seine, resembling a messenger symbolizing the Olympic spirit.


Moreover, these Games mark the first time in Olympic history that gender parity has been achieved, with an equal number of male and female athletes—5,250 each—signaling a significant step forward for gender equality in global sports. In 1900, when Paris first hosted the Olympics, only 22 women competed. By the 1996 Atlanta Olympics, women participated in 26 events, making up 34% of all athletes. At the Tokyo 2020 Olympics, women competed in 33 events. Paris 2024 has achieved full gender balance across all Olympic events, representing a major leap forward in gender equality. Beyond numerical parity, these Games also aim to promote broader gender equality by increasing media exposure and public attention to women's sports. For example, 70% of people now watch women's sports events, compared to only 16% of sports media coverage dedicated to women's sports in 2022. In this Olympics, female athletes have 14% more social media followers than their male counterparts. In this way, the Paris Olympics will not only impact the female athletes competing but also have a profound influence on global sports culture.


The Paris 2024 Olympics are expected to become an important platform inspiring future generations of female athletes and promoting broader equality across all levels of society. The successful implementation of these Games will set a significant precedent for future international sporting events.

The United Nations commends Gambian lawmakers for supporting the ban on female genital mutilation.


On July 15, the National Assembly of The Gambia, a West African country, rejected a bill attempting to overturn the 2015 law banning the traditional practice of female genital mutilation (FGM). FGM is often performed on female infants and has long caused severe immediate and long-term physical and psychological harm to women, including infections, childbirth complications, and post-traumatic stress disorder.


The United Nations praised the decision, stating that upholding the ban on FGM aligns with The Gambia's international and regional commitments, and acknowledged the role of grassroots organizations, commending survivors, activists, civil society organizations, and religious groups for their efforts to eliminate FGM.


At the same time, the United Nations noted that legal bans alone cannot stop the practice of FGM. Data shows that over 73% of Gambian women aged 15 to 49 have undergone FGM, with many subjected to the procedure before the age of five. Therefore, efforts to eliminate FGM must not waver. Communities should continue collaborating with grassroots organizations, conduct public awareness campaigns, and provide comprehensive medical and psychological treatment for survivors.

Displaced women in Haiti face "unprecedented" insecurity and sexual violence.


In Haiti, a country plagued by political instability and gang violence, approximately 580,000 people are currently displaced, including 300,000 women and girls. A report by UN Women reveals that due to the lack of basic necessities in the camps, they face severe gender-based and sexual violence.


The report shows that nearly 90% of the women surveyed in refugee camps have no source of income; over 10% reported having engaged in or considered engaging in sex work or prostitution due to basic survival needs. In most camps, violence against women and girls is used as a deliberate strategy to control them and gain access to humanitarian aid. Threats, harassment, fear, and health issues are severely affecting their lives.


In response, UN Women is actively assisting women's organizations to reach and support displaced women and girls, urging Haiti's new government to take measures to prevent and respond to violence against women and girls, while calling on the Multinational Security Support Mission to immediately provide protection for women and girls and to ensure Haitian women's organizations play a leading role in camp management.

Crimes Against Asians


News


A 13-year-old ethnic Karen boy of Burmese descent was shot dead by police in Utica, New York, prompting strong calls for justice from the Karen community.


On June 28, 13-year-old Nyah Mway, a Burmese-Karen boy, was shot and killed by police in Utica, New York. The incident occurred when police stopped Nyah, believing he matched the description of a suspect in a recent robbery. According to police, Nyah raised an object resembling a handgun while fleeing, which was later confirmed to be a replica firearm.


Police-released video shows an officer stating he needed to frisk two teenagers to ensure they were not carrying weapons. Then, Nyah ran away. Police highlighted a frame in which Nyah appears to point the replica gun at pursuing officers. Meanwhile, a video posted on Facebook shows an officer chasing Nyah, tackling him to the ground, and punching and kicking him as two other officers arrive. Gunshots were fired as Nyah lay on the ground.


LuPway Doh, a Karen community leader in Utica, said they are demanding to know whether racial profiling was used when the teenagers were stopped and whether police used excessive force. This city of only 65,000 residents is home to thousands of refugees from Myanmar, most of whom fled oppression in their homeland. Doh said Nyah's death reminded them of the trauma they experienced in their country of origin, making his death even more infuriating and unbearable.

The man who followed an Asian woman home and murdered her has been sentenced to 30 years to life in prison.


The perpetrator in the murder case of Asian American woman Christina Yuna Lee was recently sentenced to 30 years to life in prison by the Manhattan Supreme Court. Nash pleaded guilty on June 18 to charges of murder and burglary.

 

The perpetrator, Assamad Nash, 27 years old, followed Lee back to her apartment in Manhattan's Chinatown on the evening of February 13, 2022. As she opened the door, he rushed in, forced his way inside, and shut the door, where he then carried out acts of violence and sexual assault. Neighbors called 911 after hearing the struggle between Lee and Nash, but police did not break into the apartment until over an hour later. When discovered, Lee was found in the bathtub, suffering more than forty stab wounds.

 

Lee's death sparked concern within the local Asian American community and was seen as possibly part of the wave of anti-Asian violence in the United States during the COVID-19 pandemic, although Nash was not charged with a hate crime.

New York Chinese community erupts in protest against city plan to establish a homeless shelter in Chinatown


On July 28, thousands of Chinese New Yorkers took to the streets, beginning near the Federal Courthouse, marching across the Brooklyn Bridge, and ending at Cadman Plaza in downtown Brooklyn. Carrying signs such as "Equal Justice for All," demonstrators protested against New York City's plan to establish a homeless shelter in the Chinese community and demanded the release of New York City Council Member Susan Zhuang, who had been arrested earlier.


On July 17, local time in the United States, New York City announced plans to establish a homeless shelter at 86th Street and 25th Avenue in southern Brooklyn, expected to house 150 single men, including individuals with mental illnesses. The announcement triggered anger and protests within the Chinese community, who expressed concerns that the shelter's proximity to schools and senior centers could pose safety risks. On that day, police erected metal barricades on the streets. During an incident in which Council Member Susan Zhuang assisted a 78-year-old woman who had tripped over a barricade, a conflict with police ensued, during which Zhuang bit a police officer's arm. She was subsequently arrested and charged with assault and resisting arrest.


John Chan, president of the American Asian Federation, spoke at the rally, stating that since the pandemic, the Asian American community has been struggling to cope with hate crimes and worsening public safety issues, yet the city government has consistently ignored the community's urgent needs. A participating resident said the demonstrators were not opposed to the homeless themselves, but rather to building the shelter in an inappropriate location. Protesters demanded greater community involvement in decisions regarding local development.


Beyond protesting the shelter construction, the organizers broadened their demands to include wider issues, aiming to gain broader public support. While many participants had initially come to protest the shelter and support Susan Zhuang, for many others, the march carried deeper significance, reflecting long-standing frustration and dissatisfaction among Chinese community members over the city government's poor communication and lack of consensus on improving public safety and resource allocation.

The Grammy Museum will collaborate with South Korean entertainment company Hybe to launch a K-pop exhibition


The Grammy Museum will launch its first full-floor K-pop exhibition, showcasing numerous rare artifacts and artworks related to South Korean entertainment company Hybe and its star artists, including BTS and LE SSERAFIM. Titled "Hybe: We Believe in Music, The Grammy Museum Exhibit," the exhibition will run from August 2 to September 15 this year at the museum in downtown Los Angeles.


According to President and CEO Michael Sticka, the 4,000-square-foot exhibition is one of the largest ever organized by the museum. In addition to displaying performance costumes and props from popular groups such as BTS, SEVENTEEN, and LE SSERAFIM, the exhibition also includes items related to Hybe's emerging new artist teams.


During the exhibition period, the museum will host various interactive experiences, including random dance performances and photo opportunities with artists, and even a dedicated fan zone where K-pop fans can express their passion and share their stories. Hybe's Chief Operating Officer Kim Tae-ho stated that the exhibition aims not only to attract hardcore K-pop and Hybe fans but also to engage music lovers interested in gaining deeper insights, and to help promote Hybe's new artists in the U.S. music market.

Brazilian government apologizes for post-World War II persecution of Japanese immigrants


On July 25, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, chair of the Amnesty Committee and member of the advisory board of Brazil's Ministry of Human Rights, officially apologized on behalf of the Brazilian government to Japanese immigrants and their descendants for historical persecution during a congressional meeting, and pledged compensation for victims. This apology marks Brazil's formal acknowledgment of the racial discrimination and persecution inflicted upon Japanese immigrants in the mid-20th century.


Brazil joined the Allied Powers in 1942 and severed diplomatic relations with Japan; afterward, the Brazilian government confiscated property owned by Japanese immigrants and banned them from holding gatherings or speaking Japanese in public. A report from the Amnesty Committee revealed that between 1946 and 1948, 172 Japanese immigrants were sent to a detention camp on the coast of São Paulo, where they suffered abuse and torture.


Japanese immigrants and their descendants welcomed the apology, viewing it as recognition of the hardships and injustices they endured, and expressed hope that this action would encourage more countries to acknowledge and rectify similar historical wrongs. The Brazilian government stated it remains committed to repairing relations with the Japanese community and taking measures to ensure such historical injustices are never repeated.
