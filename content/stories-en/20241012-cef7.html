Content Overview

Li Hong is 50 years old. Married. Her only daughter is married and has given birth to two grandchildren. The family lives in a bungalow in Zhengzhou. Like many girls, 18-year-old Li Hong once had many dreams… the dream of going to university at 18, the dream of love at 25, and the never-realized dream of traveling the world. Day after day, her dreams were postponed for "things more important to others," then buried, forgotten… until, at 50! She resolved not to wait any longer. She immediately got her driver's license, set off alone in her car, and with one press of the accelerator, drove toward a new world of her own.

 

The film is adapted from the real-life story of Su Min, a trending figure on Douyin known as the "50-year-old aunt who travels by herself." Li Hong/Su Min's story reflects the reality of millions of Chinese families. It's something everyone in society knows but rarely discusses openly…



Patriarchy, motherhood, and domestic labor


"They say it's love. We say it's unpaid work…"

Neurosis, suicide, frigidity: the occupational diseases of housewives.

——Silvia Federici, 1974


(Still from *Her Own Way* from Douban)

The film visually contrasts the roles Li Hong and Sun Dayong play within the family through spatial arrangement. The setting physically separates Sun Dayong from Li Hong. Li Hong's busy figure doing housework contrasts with Sun Dayong's static, seated posture. The only sounds Sun Dayong makes are his nagging complaints about Li Hong and the rhythmic tapping of him playing table tennis alone, echoing against the clanging of Li Hong's cleaver in the kitchen and the dripping water in the bathroom.

In addition, the director uses the shadows of the old-style residential compound's balcony iron bars, casting them onto the characters—especially onto Li Hong's sweat-drenched face—to emphasize the "private nature" of the "family," trapping Li Hong in daily domestic labor like a cage.

(Still from *Her Own Way* from Douban)

Even though Li Hong is not entirely economically dependent on Sun Dayong in the film, she clearly bears far more labor than he does—labor that Sun Dayong takes for granted and considers unimportant. At one point, Sun Dayong suddenly and inexplicably questions Li Hong, "What are you even doing this for?"—a moment that leaves many viewers confused. But in fact, this question is a way for Sun Dayong to belittle both the amount of work Li Hong does and her motivations, to devalue the worth of housework itself, and to undermine Li Hong's status in the household, thus rationalizing his own passive benefit. Ironically, this so-called "unimportant" labor clearly causes major disruptions to the entire family's functioning when Li Hong goes on strike several times: no one looks after the grandchildren, her daughter and son-in-law can't work normally, and Sun Dayong no longer has time to go fishing or play ball…

In reality, Ms. Su Min not only endured daily belittlement and blame from her husband but also suffered long-term domestic violence. In the film, Sun Dayong doesn't display extreme behaviors such as domestic violence—only being "inattentive," "unconsiderate," and "stingy." This has led many viewers to feel the director's portrayal is too conservative. Some viewers even remarked that Sun Dayong is actually a "good man," and that their own fathers or husbands in real life are far worse. Yet this very portrayal emphasizes Li Hong's depression, which seems to come "out of nowhere" but is in fact caused by years of exhausting domestic labor that is constantly devalued. In this context, even if Sun Dayong does nothing overtly abusive, his silent acceptance of patriarchy enables the exploitation of women's labor and reproduction within the private family, making him a beneficiary of the system.

Engels, in *The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State*, pointed out that marriage and women's domestic labor are key mechanisms for maintaining the continuity of private property. In capitalist society, patriarchy assigns importance to the private family, surnames, and marriage in order to pass private property to the next generation. From a Marxist feminist perspective, Federici further explains that women's domestic and reproductive labor ensures the reproduction of labor power: her daily service allows her husband to participate in labor with a healthy, capable body, while her reproductive function ensures a continuous reserve army of laborers. Compared to other forms of work, domestic labor—including housework and childbearing—is not defined, recognized, or assigned equivalent value and wages by the social contract. As a result, women who perform this labor are not seen as "workers." Domestic labor is viewed as a natural function of the female body, an expected social trait, and more cruelly, it is imbued with meanings of love and motherhood.

Although Li Hong's marriage began in China around the 1980s, domestic labor has not been "socialized" to the extent Engels described, so the exploitation of women like Li Hong still persists. Moreover, China is an ancient patriarchal society where the idea of "men work outside, women manage inside" is deeply ingrained. Online, one still sees comments like "women can't sit at the dinner table to eat," yet ironically, this Chinese saying resonates with Western feminist struggles to "stay at the table." Whether one can sit at the table ultimately reflects who makes the decisive choices in a given space, and who is relegated to repetitive, devalued, and ignored labor. The film shows Li Hong's family of origin: her father is a worker, her mother a housewife, and she has a younger brother. Many shots in the film suggest that the father is always the one making major decisions, including forcing Li Hong to drop out of school and work to support her brother. The mother is often positioned in the shadows or margins, silently doing housework. From childhood, Li Hong was taught skills like "kneading dough" to care for her brother, and as an adult, she was expected to financially support her brother's business. In other words, the Li Hong–Sun Dayong household is a continuation of Li Hong's parents' family, and the patriarchal exploitation of women's domestic labor is passed down through generational kinship ties.

Li Hong's daughter, Sun Xiaoxue, is a woman of the 1980s who received compulsory education and holds independent feminist ideas. Yet after marriage and childbirth, she becomes the main person relying on and exploiting Li Hong's free labor. She may support her mother, but when her own interests are threatened, she becomes an oppressor. As Sun Dayong's favored daughter, she may have long been accustomed to the idea that it is her mother—not herself, her father, or her husband—who should do more. Thus, being female is a position of oppression, not merely a biological category. When Li Hong chooses to "leave," the burden of her "strike" falls onto Xiaoxue, who is also socially and biologically female. Xiaoxue's husband, though verbally deferential and concerned about Li Hong, in reality does almost nothing to relieve the domestic labor burden on the two women in the household. At this moment, patriarchy's exploitation of women becomes even more hidden and insidious. In the final shot, the person sitting in the cage-like balcony is now Xiaoxue—the cage holds the condition of women. The father still goes fishing, the husband works normally. Why is it always the mother who must bear this private, unpaid labor?



How to face the "Nora" who leaves


Although the character Li Hong in the film hits the road, and the real-life Su Min also chose to go far away, like Li Hong's friend Ma Jie, many women currently in similar situations do not have the conditions to live the life they want. This contrast leads many viewers to feel that women who don't live for themselves are weak. But one must be clear: the circumstances women face are inherently unfavorable. Li Hong is indeed brave, but we have no right to judge others as cowardly.

Just as Xiaoxue once retorted to an interviewer, if she's unlucky, next time she applies elsewhere she'll face corporate questioning and scrutiny again, even having to conform to male-centered logic to be hired—over time, this wears one down. So rather than using bravery as a moral standard to judge women in difficult situations, we should instead focus on where they need help, offer corresponding emotional support and a supportive environment, and assist them in confronting their current reality.

(Reference image 1: Cover of *A Doll's House* by Ibsen)

Bravery requires confidence—where does this confidence come from? Since the May Fourth era, the "Nora who leaves" and the destination she seeks have remained focal points in feminist discourse. On the journey of leaving, "Noras" always harbor a myth about their lingering worries. Unlike the answer given by revolutionary-era works like *The Song of Youth*, *Her Own Way* focuses more on the individual, and its epilogue becomes increasingly dreamlike with the support of capitalism. Yet whether it's the former or the latter, collective or individual, female narratives have essentially never received sufficient affirmation and recognition centered on women themselves, because the foundation of female subjectivity still remains defined in relation to patriarchal frameworks.

In today's society, whether it's Li Hong or Aunt Su Min, even if they have left, gained economic independence, and become platform streamers, even if they don't fall into decline, they still face the problem of how their stories end. Returning in old age would mean betraying their dreams once again; continuing forward means challenging even greater limits. The end of the road may not be all scenic beauty, but beyond that, the "Noras" have no other retreat to choose from. Ultimately, we not only need the idea of economic independence, but also need to deconstruct patriarchal models and build a system distinct from patriarchy—one that allows the self space for coherence and respite. And this pioneering path can only be explored by women themselves through lived experience.



Narrative structure from a female perspective


Like the recent Chinese premiere of "There Is Still Tomorrow" at the Pingyao Film Festival, the director focuses the narrative more on the perpetrators rather than the victims when handling scenes of violence and oppression. Many details, along with skillful control over pacing and transitions, allow the film to maintain a tender and bright tone, somewhat softening the intensity of its conflicts. By placing the ending at the beginning, the film alleviates the oppressive weight of the mundane and fragmented middle sections, and more powerfully expands the protagonist's space of life.

(Photo: Poster of "There Is Still Tomorrow")

Interestingly, the film does not adopt a structured, chapter-based approach like a documentary, nor does it follow a conventional chronological timeline as a typical narrative film might. Instead, it tells its story through interwoven fragments, creating a highly lifelike effect. For instance, at the end, Li Hong sees several young women riding away on a truck—these girls might be people she actually sees, or they might represent her former self. This method not only reveals how life itself is inherently fragmented but also helps viewers realize how women are worn down, day after day, bit by bit, within such circumstances. It is precisely this kind of approach—rooted in women's lived experience and perspective—that allows us women to see countless versions of ourselves in the film, rather than just one story or a few characters.

Even if the path ahead remains unclear, even if the journey is still long, women's steps are growing firmer and their voices louder. Finally, we would like to close this article with a quote from Aunt Su Min in one of her videos: The world has already placed enough and heavy enough shackles on women. Be proud of yourself—you've made it through so many difficult times alone. May you have enough courage to face the wind, face the snow, face the rising sun in the rest of your life; may you live freely, always on your way to see the flowers bloom.
