Salt Town is a work of nonfiction written by Yi Xiaohé, a journalist by training, who spent a year immersed in an ancient salt-producing town in Zigong, Sichuan. The book weaves together the life stories of 12 women in descending age order: from 90-year-old Granny Chen to 17-year-old Huang Xinyi, spanning decades of social transformation, yet tracing eerily similar cycles of fate.


Author: Yi Xiaohé


They live in obscurity, ignored and forgotten their entire lives—no one knows how they exist or how they live. It's not that they don't exist, but that they are overlooked and erased. With delicate yet sober prose, Yi Xiaohé uses writing to lift a corner of this forgotten world, retrieving and presenting the silenced fates of rural Chinese women. She emphasizes that she is not merely writing a "book about women," but standing from the perspective of a shared human destiny, hoping these stories will be heard, seen, and understood by more people, and thus spark change.


After reading Salt Town, what we see is not just a series of personal joys and sorrows, but an entire system of gender-based oppression deeply rooted in society and institutions. In this land, how do women survive between the narrow margins of marriage and poverty? How do they resist within cycles of intergenerational violence? And in the face of institutional indifference and social prejudice, how do they support one another and save themselves?



Domestic violence:

The intergenerational cycle of silent endurance


In the opening story of Salt Town, 90-year-old Chen Bingzhi (Granny Chen) recounts her turbulent life to the author. From childhood, the shadow of domestic violence loomed over her: her father, a staunch believer in male superiority, abandoned the family after her mother gave birth to four daughters, plunging his wife and children into poverty. Young Chen Bingzhi witnessed firsthand her mother's struggle to raise several children alone: gathering wild vegetables in the mountains to stave off hunger, chopping firewood to sell for meager income, even resorting to frying salt to mix with rice. Her father's abandonment was not only economic desertion but also emotional abuse, planting deep insecurity in young Chen Bingzhi and quietly setting in motion the intergenerational cycle of domestic violence.


Poverty, like a heavy shadow, limited Chen Bingzhi's ability to think. Her motivation to move forward in life seemed driven only by an instinctive fear of instability and chaos. Knowing all too well the bitterness of being "without support," at 18, Chen Bingzhi set out alone in search of survival, hoping marriage would be her escape from hardship—only to be met with deeper irony and injury. Three men entered her life, yet not one became a true pillar of support.



The first man abandoned her while she was pregnant, forcing her to wander from place to place performing in opera troupes. Then, on a pitch-black night, the young Chen Bingzhi, heavily pregnant, faced the most difficult moment of her life alone. With no family by her side, she held a rusty old pair of scissors and, through a mud wall, tremulously asked a neighbor how to cut the umbilical cord. No medical care, no light—only a few distant instructions from an aunt next door. In that moment, what she cut was not only the baby's umbilical cord but also her last shred of hope in marriage as protection.


That single snip marked both the beginning of a new life and the continuation of an old fate. Years earlier, her mother had endured the same desperate childbirth alone—and now the scene repeated itself in her daughter, as if fate had silently completed a cruel cycle. Marriage failed to become a safe harbor; instead, it became a storm that pushed her back to where she started.


Her second husband, Zhang Yuncheng, was a fisherman with a drinking problem, prone to jealousy and violence whenever Chen Bingzhi spoke to others. He dragged and beat her, once smashing her head into a pickle jar until she bled profusely. She said: "Every time he hit me, he'd pull out clumps of my hair."


Townspeople remained indifferent; domestic violence was seen by locals as an ordinary "family matter." Once, just seven days after giving birth, while still in her postpartum confinement, she was dragged from her bed and beaten. Without legal intervention or public support, she was trapped in a vicious cycle of "beating—fleeing—reconciliation." Only Zhang Yuncheng's accidental death brought this prolonged suffering to an end.


What is heartbreaking is that Chen Bingzhi's experience is not unique. In Salt Town, many women's lives follow similar trajectories: one matchmaker in the book, despite knowing her husband had a history of domestic violence, chooses to remarry him under threat and helplessness, like countless others, stepping once again into a river already muddied beyond clarity.


The trauma of one generation of women often quietly passes to the next—not only replicating family structures but also deeply planting gendered beliefs. The father's abandonment and the husband's violence stem from the deeply entrenched patriarchal order of rural society. When violence is treated as a "family matter" and met with silence, it becomes an invisible "inheritance," silently passed from one generation to the next. Trapped on a stage pre-scripted by fate, tragedy after tragedy unfolds by rote. 


The survival dilemma of gender inequality:

Choices made at the end of the road


The roots of domestic violence go far beyond individual brutality and ignorance. A deeper problem lies in the long-standing inequality in education and economic resources, which pushes women at the bottom into a state of powerlessness and self-rescue. Chen Bingzhi attended school for only a few days and cannot even fully recognize her own name. Lack of education doomed her to exclusion from decent work, forcing her to survive through physical labor: as a young woman, she worked odd jobs in teahouses; later, to support five children, she became a laborer at a grain station—carrying heavy sacks in exchange for meager wages.



In Salt Town, gender differences determine fate from birth. Girls have few chances to study, limited paths to earn money, and marriage is forced to serve as an economic stepping stone—their only exit. Yet this exit, seemingly a way out, leads instead to another trap. Chen Bingzhi entered marriage with this faint hope, only to discover that a "good family" was a promise never meant to be kept.


When marriage fails to protect and instead becomes a new prison, women find themselves in a no-win situation: leaving may mean losing everything; staying means enduring violence and oppression.


This dilemma is common throughout Salt Town. Some women, even when achieving a degree of economic independence, still dare not easily divorce, due to society's prejudice and scrutiny toward "divorced women." The seemingly successful female entrepreneur in the book, skilled and capable in business, can only painfully maintain a marriage long fallen apart, fearing public opinion and the burden of child-rearing. Clearly, under deeply entrenched gender inequality, marriage is not always a safe haven; sometimes, it becomes a double shackle alongside poverty, binding women tighter.


In such a cramped space for survival, some women are forced to make extreme and difficult choices. The story of the "cat house" in Salt Town is heartbreaking. "Cat," or mao'er, is a local Sichuan Zigong slang term for sex workers. At age 58, Chen Bingzhi reluctantly opened such a "cat house." At first, she only wanted to sell tea to survive, but business was poor. A local cattle dealer named Huang Juguang, seeing her struggle to raise a group of children alone, suggested she switch lines. Soon after, several older middle-aged women came knocking, seeking shelter and food in exchange for selling their bodies to support their families.


These "cats" were mostly desperate women—mothers caring for elderly parents and young children, surviving on their own. They were no longer young or beautiful, unable to find a place on the margins of the city, so they settled for this modest little shop. Chen Bingzhi understood their hardship, charging only a symbolic five yuan commission per transaction, and taking nothing if no clients came, even providing three meals a day. Neighbors saw her as a madam profiting from "dirty money," whispering cold insults behind her back; but to the women adrift in storms, this shop was a rare sanctuary—not only earning them money to survive, but also a measure of dignity.


Through the real window of the "cat house," we glimpse how women at the bottom survive in desperation. Sadly, this reality has not disappeared with the passage of time. In 21st-century Salt Town, a woman over ninety and a girl as young as seventeen are engaged in the same flesh trade. Chen Bingzhi raised her children by sheltering sex workers; girls like Huang Xinyi, born after 2000, dropped out of school, worked as hostesses in KTVs, and were led by so-called "boyfriends" into the gray world. Different ages, different eras, yet repeating the same fate.


What is it that pushes generation after generation of women into the same abyss?


It is the gender inequality rooted deep in systems and culture. When the barriers to education stand high, when decent jobs remain half-closed to them, when marriage is no longer an escape and social protection is absent, they can only struggle to survive within severely limited options. Some choose endurance; others take desperate risks. Whichever choice, behind it lies a heavy fate.


As written in the book, their lives continue to repeat an ancient cycle: trapped in marriage, trapped in poverty, trapped in a fate of invisibility. This is not merely individual misfortune, but a silent and prolonged collective pain.


The cycle of violence is not caused by gender,

but by the system.


Salt Town contains a quiet yet powerful story—about love and hardship between two women. Tong Hui, in her fifties, was once strikingly beautiful and remained unmarried her whole life; Hong Mei, a middle school teacher, had a brief marriage and one son. Under the town's rigid gaze, they drew close to each other, living together in defiance of social norms. People whispered about them as "lesbians," yet in silence they wove a tender life that belonged only to them.


At first, people assumed their union was an escape, a gentle protest against male-dominated power structures. Yet as time passed slowly, the deep-seated ghosts of patriarchy quietly returned between them. Hong Mei gradually adopted the vices of the town's men: drinking, gambling, expressing anger through fists. Tong Hui, in turn, became the silent sufferer—like countless women pinned by fate between kitchen and bedroom, she said nothing amid Hong Mei's drunken insults and violence, swallowing her tears into the night.


Their quarrels resembled a worn-out script played countless times before: who should manage money, whose name should be on the house deed, who should care for parents and children… These were the trivialities of countless "couples," now being reenacted between two women. They had thought love would bring liberation, but in the end it became another form of imprisonment.


The breakdown of their relationship was not because they were women, but because society had already preset a format for "intimate relationships": one controls, the other obeys; one speaks, the other stays silent. In this script, gender is merely the ticket to play a role, while the true determinants of the outcome are power structures and cultural narratives. Hong Mei was not inherently violent—she had simply been taught how to become the "strong one"; Tong Hui was not born weak—she was simply too familiar with the posture of "enduring hardship," and even on a new stage, she still knew when to bow her head.


Their intimate relationship did not escape traditional scripts, but instead unconsciously repeated them. Even with no men on stage, society's expectations of "who should lead, who should submit" remained—this time, performed by two women. The gendered division of roles, seemingly belonging to heterosexual marriage, was not broken by a change in gender composition; like a ghost, it clung on, dragging what should have been a liberating relationship back into a power struggle.


When Hong Mei raised the liquor bottle to vent her rage, she played the "dominant" role assigned by the system; Tong Hui retreated into silence and endurance, merely continuing the posture of the tamed "subordinate." In this quiet tug-of-war, love became unrecognizable, and the script of violence grew unmistakably clear.


The collapse of this relationship ultimately stemmed not from their gender, but from a deeply internalized structural inertia: who dominates, who yields; who speaks, who stays silent. Intimate relationships seem pulled by a predetermined pattern—even when the cast changes, the script remains familiar. What truly harms is not that someone has changed, but that women have never had a chance to escape the script of violence. 


Female solidarity:

Lighting a lamp in the darkness


Although Salt Town documents the suffering and struggles of many women's fates, between oppression and heaviness, there also lies a gentle light of mutual support and self-redemption among women.


Chen Bingzhi is one beam of that light. After losing her husband, she relied on no one, raising five children alone by working as a porter and in teahouses, eventually opening a "cat house" out of necessity to survive. The world may not understand her "earning tainted money," but she used this small business to shelter a group of desperate, socially abandoned women—taking only symbolic commissions, providing food and shelter, offering crucial help to others in crisis. To society, she was a "madam"; to the trapped women, she was a harbor where they could briefly escape the storm.



Chen Bingzhi's resilience is not an isolated case. The women in Salt Town, squeezed in the cracks without institutional protection, often can only lift each other up and light one another's way. They resist in the faintest but most genuine ways: some choose to silently endure within hardship, some attempt to leave the town to make a living, and others quietly awaken amid their heavy daily lives.


As Yi Xiaohe wrote: "When you think you've hit rock bottom, you'll find others are already there—your lives lift each other upward." These unremarkable acts of mutual aid are not as dramatic as revolution, but they are beams of light piercing through an iron curtain, revealing hope and illuminating each other's paths. They have not given up on life, but in the torrent of fate, they tear open a crack of light with stubbornness and warmth.


When they cry for help,

who is listening?


The most heartbreaking scene in Salt Town is not the suffering itself, but the empty alleys behind that suffering. The women in the book repeatedly stand at the edge of life, reaching out for help, yet rarely receive institutional acceptance or positive social response. Their predicaments stem not only from the ups and downs of personal fate, but more deeply from systemic apathy toward women.


First, there is the slowness—or even absence—of institutional intervention. In that era, domestic violence was not a crime, but a "family matter"; beating one's wife was not illegal, but a sign of "a man with a big temper." When Chen Bingzhi was physically abused by her husband in her youth, neighbors only advised her to "endure it, it'll pass." The system offered her no protection.


Even today, although the Anti-Domestic Violence Law has long been enacted, it lacks enforcement in remote towns. Grassroots shelters and professional psychological support are lacking, leaving women in urgent need of help out of reach. The matchmaker in the book, who once fled her abusive husband but eventually remarried him, did not do so out of love, but because "divorce is harder to survive than marriage." The absence of institutional and social support plunges women into even deeper abysses after escaping violence.


Regrettably, before the law, women whose very survival is at stake are not rescued, but punished. In her later years, Chen Bingzhi was convicted for sheltering several impoverished middle-aged and elderly women who "sold their bodies," and her monthly subsistence allowance was canceled. Cracking down on illegal activities may be legally justified, but rigidly applying the law without considering or addressing real-life circumstances only makes the weak weaker and the underclass sink further. In this reckoning, men remain invisible, as if they never existed. Women's bodies become targets, while men's responsibilities are quietly erased. This is prejudice, not justice.


Even colder is the numbness of human sentiment. In her later years, Chen Bingzhi was defrauded of all her savings after mistaking a stranger for her long-lost younger daughter. Her trust stemmed from maternal instinct, from a warmth deep within that had not yet been completely extinguished. But her neighbors showed no compassion, only coldly sneering that she "got what she deserved." They chose to forget how she once fought desperately for her five children. They only remembered she ran a "cat house," attributing her suffering to "reaping what she sowed." The same indifference was directed at Tong Hui and Hong Mei—they were always seen as outsiders, and Tong Hui still could not bring herself to say "we are a couple" years later. In Salt Town, if a woman's choices do not conform to "virtuous, frugal, enduring," she is labeled, and even in distress, she is not worthy of pity.


Behind these appearances lies a deeper, structural neglect: women's voices are silenced, women's pain is normalized, women's misfortunes are treated as "fate." When institutions fail to act, their cries for help become echoes falling into a well—growing fainter, until no one cares.


Salt Town is a mirror, reflecting the trajectories of countless women's fates. It shows us: gender inequality is not an isolated tragedy, but a systematically orchestrated reality. When suffering is seen as normal, when hardship is blamed on the victim as "deserved," what is truly missing is understanding, positive response, and change. And writing, telling these stories, helping more men and women see and understand, is the first step toward breaking the silence and driving change.
