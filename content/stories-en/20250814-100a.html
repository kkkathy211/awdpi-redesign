I. Introduction: The Multidimensional Definition of Gender-Based Violence


Gender-based violence (GBV), as a global human rights crisis, is defined by the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW) as violence directed against women because of their gender, or that disproportionately affects women. It includes acts causing physical, mental, or sexual harm, threats of such acts, coercion, and other deprivations of liberty. The United Nations Declaration on the Elimination of Violence against Women defines it as any act of gender-based violence that results in, or is likely to result in, physical, sexual, or psychological harm or suffering to women, including threats of such acts, coercion, or arbitrary deprivation of liberty, whether occurring in public or private life. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines sexual violence as any sexual act, attempt to obtain a sexual act, or act targeting a person's sexual autonomy, carried out through coercion or force (Violence against women).


Synthesizing definitions from WHO, CEDAW, UN Women, and other international bodies, we can conclude the following: First, forms of gender-based violence include rape (including marital rape), sexual harassment, sexual abuse of children, forced prostitution, trafficking of women and girls, non-contact humiliation, child and forced marriage, and violations of women's sexual integrity (such as female genital mutilation, forced virginity testing); physical violence, including beating, bodily harm, and traditional harmful practices (such as female genital cutting); psychological violence, including mental control, threats, social isolation, and intimidation; economic control, such as deprivation of property rights and restrictions on employment freedom; and institutional violence, including state-tolerated violence due to legal gaps. Second, gender-based violence occurs across a wide range of settings—within families, workplaces, communities, and other public or private spaces—and also exists within state systems, meaning violence committed or condoned by the state. It also extends into digital spaces, where WHO emphasizes "non-contact forms" of sexual violence, such as the non-consensual sharing of intimate images. Of course, gender-based violence is not limited to women; it also affects men and gender minorities. The WHO World Report on Violence and Health acknowledges male victims and same-sex violence, but emphasizes that the majority of victims of gender-based violence are still women and girls, and the perpetrators are predominantly men and boys.


Beyond international organizations' definitions of gender-based violence, many philosophers have also explored violence, power, and gender. Foucault argues that violence is a product of unequal power structures, and gender-based violence is the result of gendered power imbalances. Gender essentialism holds that gender differences and traits are innate and biologically determined. Foucault challenges gender essentialism, proposing in The History of Sexuality that gender is a normalized construction produced by power. "Sex is not a natural anatomical fact, but a 'historical necessity,' a product of strategic deployments of power." (Foucault, 1976) In short, gender differences are power strategies, not biological facts. Butler refers to gender as "a performative effect." (Gender Trouble) These power strategies include establishing ideal body images, setting standards of what is 'healthy' or 'natural,' behavioral norms, and systems of reward and punishment, thereby disciplining individuals into gendered beings. (Foucault, 1976) For example, population management brings women's bodies under state control, categorizing women with strong reproductive capacity, maternal instincts, and emotional docility as "healthy women," while those who do not conform are labeled "abnormal." (Foucault, 1976)

Moreover, gender norms are repeatedly transmitted through discourses in education, medicine, and the media (Foucault, 1976). When individuals fail to conform to these norms, gender-based violence becomes a tool to uphold them—it is the punishment imposed by power on those who violate norms. As Foucault states, normative judgments consolidate power by excluding the "abnormal," and gender-based violence is the materialization of such judgments. (Foucault, 1975)


Galtung's triangle model of violence, introduced in Cultural Violence, offers a deeper explanation of this form of gender-based violence. He expands violence into three mutually reinforcing dimensions: direct violence, structural violence, and cultural violence (Galtung, J., 1990). The most widely recognized form of gender-based violence is direct violence—physical, psychological, or verbal harm directly inflicted by individuals or groups. This violence "creates fear through pain, forcing victims to comply with norms," for example, a husband beating a "disobedient" wife. Beyond direct violence, which causes visible harm, structural and symbolic (cultural) violence are invisible forms of gender-based violence that are difficult to detect and acknowledge, and their harms lack visibility. Yet their destructive impact is no less severe than direct violence, making them even more critical to identify.



First, structural violence refers to social structures—such as laws, economic systems, and political institutions—that systematically obstruct specific groups from meeting basic needs like survival, dignity, and freedom (Galtung, 1990, p.292). For example: Foucault, in The History of Sexuality, discusses how medicine and law pathologize non-binary genders: intersex individuals are required to undergo surgical "correction," and homosexuality is diagnosed as "mental perversion." This institutional erasure is at the core of structural violence—it deprives those who do not conform to gender norms of their right to life under the guise of "treatment." (Foucault, 1976)


Second, symbolic violence refers to "cultural elements such as religious doctrines, gender norms, and historical narratives that aestheticize violence as 'tradition,' 'honor,' or 'natural order.'" (Galtung, 1990, p.291) Taking the stigmatization of women as an example, Foucault argues that labels like "slut" are gallows of discourse—they pin women to a moral pillar of shame, causing their social death, which is more efficient than physical annihilation. Women, in turn, become self-regulating prisoners in order to avoid being labeled as such (Foucault, 1969).


Among these three forms of violence, direct violence is the visible tip of the iceberg, structural violence is the submerged bulk beneath the water, and cultural violence is the sea that stabilizes the entire iceberg—through carriers such as religion, art, and language, it makes violence appear natural or legitimate to society (Galtung, 1990, p.292).


Galtung emphasizes that the three forms of violence reinforce each other: the violence triangle is a death spiral, in which any one form reproduces the other two (Galtung, 1990, p.305). First, cultural violence enables structural violence: gender discriminatory beliefs, such as the idea that women are less capable workers than men, rationalize structural violence like wage gaps and glass ceilings, making the exploitation of women's labor seem natural. Second, structural violence triggers direct violence: economic deprivation strips women of autonomy, forcing them into dependency on partners, making it impossible to leave even when abuse occurs. Finally, direct violence reinforces cultural violence: when men commit honor killings of women deemed morally "impure," such murders are portrayed in media as "protecting family honor," reinforcing distorted values like "female chastity above life."



In summary, although many international organizations and philosophers have explored gender-based violence and expanded the scope of violence, we still face a series of challenges today, including the invisibility of gender-based violence, ambiguous definitions, ineffective prevention systems, and low societal acceptance of these definitions. Academic definitions of gender-based violence lack penetration and are disconnected from public understanding. Therefore, this study aims to construct a definition framework for gender-based violence that combines academic rigor with public communicability. At the academic level, clear definitions are the foundation for deeper research; at the public communication level, this study seeks to enhance public awareness of structural oppression by deconstructing hidden forms of gender-based violence. The first part of this report will analyze the manifestations and contexts of gender-based violence; the second part will focus on philosophical and epistemological analyses of gender-based violence.


II. Manifestations of Gender-Based Violence



The concept of gender-based violence has been defined above. Based on the visibility of violent forms, gender-based violence can be divided into overt (visible) violence and covert (invisible) violence. Physical and sexual violence are typically overt forms, usually exhibiting clear external signs, and victims can clearly perceive direct physical or psychological harm when experiencing such acts. These forms are more readily recognized by society and can be addressed through legal systems. Covert violence, on the other hand, refers to hidden and difficult-to-identify forms of violence. Although such violence lacks directly observable external signs, its harm and impact on victims are often more profound. By analyzing these two types of violence, we aim to reveal their long-term effects on victims and their interwoven presence across different social contexts.


1

Sexual Violence

Sexual violence is a serious violation of human rights and a public health issue that transcends class and racial boundaries, occurring both in times of peace and in conflict settings. The World Report on Violence and Health (2002) defines sexual violence as: "any sexual act, attempt to obtain a sexual act, or act of trafficking in persons for sexual purposes, committed against anyone through coercion, harassment, or unwanted advances, regardless of the relationship between perpetrator and victim, and occurring in any setting, including but not limited to the home and workplace."


Sexual violence can occur in a variety of contexts and settings, including: rape within marriage or romantic relationships; rape by non-intimate acquaintances; sexual abuse of victims by individuals in positions of trust (such as religious figures, medical personnel, or teachers); stranger rape; gang rape; sexual assault through deception, coercion, blackmail, or against individuals who are unable to defend themselves or who are incapacitated by drugs or alcohol; rape during armed conflict; sexual harassment (including demands for sexual acts in exchange for employment, academic grades, or other benefits); unwanted sexual contact; rape of men in prisons; forced exposure to pornography; sexual abuse of individuals with mental or physical disabilities; sexual abuse of boys or girls; and violence targeting sexual integrity (including female genital mutilation, virginity testing, forced anal examinations, and human trafficking for the purpose of sexual exploitation). Therefore, sexual violence encompasses diverse victim-perpetrator relationships, various forms of sexual acts, differing degrees of coercion, a range of vulnerable situations, and may occur in multiple locations.


In the digital age, sexual violence has also undergone significant transformations in form and method, manifesting as "technology-facilitated sexual assault," "revenge pornography," "online sexual harassment," and gender-based hate speech. These behaviors not only expand the exposure of victims but also pose new challenges for law enforcement, judicial processes, and resistance efforts (Powell and Henry, 2017).


According to statistical data from Dartnall and Jewkes (2013) and the World Health Organization (WHO) multi-country study framework, sexual violence against women is categorized into different types to enable comparable investigations and governance globally. These categories cover sexual violence within intimate relationships, non-partner sexual violence, and childhood sexual abuse, among other relational and situational contexts. Specifically, intimate partner sexual violence refers to women being forced into sexual relations with a spouse or romantic partner against their will, or being compelled to consent due to fear of the partner, even being forced into sexual acts that feel humiliating or degrading. This form is highly prevalent across multiple regions, with 58.6% of women in Ethiopia province reporting such experiences. Non-partner sexual violence includes forced sexual acts committed by non-intimate individuals when the woman is 15 years or older, such as sexual assault while intoxicated or drugged and thus unable to give consent, or rape occurring in group-perpetrated situations. This category has a lower prevalence but remains significant, typically ranging between 1%–11%. Childhood sexual abuse refers to any form of sexual contact or forced first sexual experience suffered by children under the age of 15, with prevalence rates around 1%–21%, reaching as high as 21.3% in urban areas of Namibia. To improve survey accuracy, WHO has proposed operational definitions based on specific behavioral scenarios, emphasizing the importance of focusing on the non-consensual nature of the act and the vulnerability of the victim, rather than relying solely on narrow legal or cultural definitions of "rape." Additionally, multi-country prevalence data show that the rate of forced first sexual intercourse reaches as high as 29.9% in some countries (such as Bangladesh province), reflecting the particular vulnerability during childhood and adolescence. These data indicate that sexual violence is widespread across various social relationships and settings, transcending age, relationship type, and culture, and must be systematically identified and addressed in theory, law, and public health prevention.


2

Sexual violence

"Physical violence within intimate relationships"

Pelser et al. (2005) comparative results show that physical violence is the most common form of gender-based violence within intimate relationships, with 30% of surveyed women reporting having experienced some form of physical violence from a partner. Intimate partner physical violence is defined as acts causing physical harm to the victim, including but not limited to: being slapped or having objects thrown at them that could cause injury; being pushed or shoved; being hit with fists or other objects that could cause harm; being kicked, dragged, or beaten; being choked or intentionally burned; or being threatened with or actually harmed by firearms, knives, or other weapons; or combinations of the above behaviors.

According to Sardinha et al. (2022), globally, an estimated 27% (range: 23–31%) of women aged 15–49 who have a spouse or partner have experienced physical or sexual violence (or both) at least once in their lifetime, while 13% experienced such violence in the 12 months preceding the survey. Violence begins in adolescence, with 24% of women aged 15–19 having experienced such violence at least once since age 15. The prevalence of violence is significantly higher in low-income countries and regions compared to high-income countries, with even greater disparities in recent-year prevalence. In some countries (such as Kiribati, Papua New Guinea, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and Afghanistan), the lifetime prevalence of violence among women exceeds 50%. The study notes that the COVID-19 pandemic and associated lockdown measures may have further exacerbated the existing burden of violence.


"Domestic violence"

Domestic violence can be summarized by five elements: a pattern of aggressive and coercive behaviors, including physical, sexual, and psychological attacks, as well as economic coercion; acts committed by adults or adolescents against current or former intimate, marital, or cohabiting partners (including heterosexual and lesbian women); a behavioral pattern composed of multiple strategies, some of which cause physical harm and some do not, some of which constitute crimes and some do not, and which often occur repeatedly, even on a daily basis; a purposeful pattern of behavior aimed at forcing the victim to submit or achieving control over them; and a combination of physical attacks and control tactics through which perpetrators instill fear in the victim and cause physical and psychological harm.

Kartas (2017) points out that women trapped in such relationships, if experiencing physical, psychological, or sexual violence within intimate relationships, often become caught in a cycle of violence, believing they are powerless to change their situation and afraid to leave the abuser due to fear of losing their lives. Forms of violence are not limited to spousal relationships but also include psychological, physical, sexual abuse, and economic control by parents over daughters.


3

Psychological violence

Psychological violence, as a common form of gender-based violence, refers to behaviors that seriously impair another person's psychological integrity through threats, coercion, or other actions. Psychological violence includes various manifestations such as emotional abuse, verbal insults, emotional manipulation, stalking, and harassment. It often co-occurs with or precedes other forms of violence (such as physical, sexual, and economic violence), reflecting the intersectionality of multiple forms of violence (EIGE, 2023).


Research on psychological violence originated in marriage violence studies in the 1970s. Initial studies revealed that women experiencing physical violence often also faced emotional oppression and psychological control from their abusers. These early studies provided preliminary evidence on psychological violence, showing that although this form of violence lacks direct physical harm, the damage it inflicts on victims' mental and emotional well-being is often more profound (Follingstad et al., 1990). Much of this research came from victims' anecdotes, describing patterns ranging from cutting remarks to abusive interpersonal control, with some victims indicating that the trauma and anxiety caused by psychological violence are often more difficult to endure than physical violence. As research deepened, scholars began to systematize the concept and provide theoretical support. The manifestations of psychological violence gradually erode the victim's mental world by continuously attacking their self-esteem, emotional security, and psychological stability (Rodenburg and Fantuzzo, 1993).


However, in the research and data collection on psychological violence, the academic community generally faces a series of difficulties and challenges, primarily reflected in the hidden nature, subjectivity, and intersectionality of psychological violence with other forms of violence. First, the definition and manifestations of psychological violence are relatively complex, involving not only verbal insults, emotional manipulation, and threats, but also many implicit and progressive violent behaviors, which are often difficult to clearly identify and quantify (Follingstad, 2007). Unlike physical or sexual violence, psychological violence lacks obvious external evidence and often manifests as long-term emotional harm and psychological trauma, making its impact on victims difficult to measure directly. This invisibility leads to many incidents of psychological violence going unreported or excluded from traditional violence statistics.


Second, the measurement and data collection of psychological violence are also constrained by sociocultural and institutional barriers. Many countries and regions lack a unified understanding and definition of psychological violence, resulting in significant differences in data collection standards and methods across regions. In the case of the European Union, many member states do not recognize certain behaviors such as emotional manipulation or "cold violence" as forms of violence, and thus fail to effectively record or classify them during data collection. Even when some countries collect data on psychological violence, the data often lack basic information about the gender and relationship between victims and perpetrators, making cross-national comparison and comprehensive analysis difficult (EIGE, 2023). For victims, due to insufficient awareness of psychological violence, many fail to recognize that they are experiencing such abuse, or choose to conceal their experiences out of fear of retaliation from the perpetrator, further exacerbating data gaps.


Therefore, research and data collection on psychological violence not only face limitations in conceptual definition and measurement tools, but are also closely linked to structural factors such as sociocultural norms, legal frameworks, and public awareness. The lack of a unified definition and standardized measurement tools often leads psychological violence to be overlooked or downplayed, resulting in research findings and policy recommendations failing to accurately reflect its true extent.


4

Economic violence

Economic violence refers to the act of perpetrators controlling, restricting, or depriving victims of economic resources and financial autonomy, forcing them into economic and practical dependence, thereby exerting economic oppression and control over their behaviors and choices. Economic violence is not merely direct financial control or restriction; rather, it more often involves using economic means to affect victims' quality of life, social status, and personal independence. It may manifest as controlling or limiting income sources, controlling or withholding household finances, restricting decision-making power over consumption, enforcing economic dependency, coercing debt, or committing financial fraud (Conner, 2013).


According to survey data from the European Institute for Gender Equality (EIGE), economic violence is widespread in intimate relationships and exhibits significant gender disparities. The data show that on average, 12% of women in the EU have experienced economic abuse by a partner since the age of 15. This proportion reveals the pervasive reality of economic violence in women's lives, particularly in heterosexual relationships, where women face notably greater economic control and oppression (EIGE, 2023).


Moreover, the impact of economic violence is not uniform; multiple marginalized identities within social structures significantly increase women's vulnerability. Women living in poverty, those with disabilities, or those belonging to marginalized groups (such as ethnic minorities, gender minorities, or immigrant populations) find it harder to achieve economic independence and are more susceptible to economic control. For example, a study conducted by Women's World Banking and its partner, the Observatory for Women's Equality in Latin America and the Caribbean (Observatorio para la Equidad de Mujeres, hereafter OEM), on the situation of violence against women in Cali, Colombia, found that among all women who had experienced physical violence, 44% were full-time unpaid caregivers, highlighting the close link between economic dependence and vulnerability to violence. The study further emphasized the intersectional impact of economic violence: among victims of economic violence, 67% lived in the lowest socioeconomic areas, and 35% engaged in unpaid domestic labor within the household, showing high overlap rates between race, class, occupation, and economic violence. At the same time, domestic violence reporting rates were significantly higher in marginalized communities, underscoring the geographical structural connection between resource deprivation and the occurrence of violence (OEM, 2023).


III. The systemic concealment of gender-based violence under monopolized definitional power


The previous section clarified the distinction between overt and covert violence: while physical and sexual violence are often more easily recognized and intervened due to their direct and visible nature, a large amount of covert, structural, and institutional violence is often hidden within daily life, emotional ethics, and cultural common sense. These invisible forms of violence are not only difficult to define as "violence," but are also normalized, rationalized, and even romanticized within social discourse and cognitive structures, leading to systemic neglect of victims' experiences. The key issue lies not only in the hidden nature of violent acts themselves, but more fundamentally in who holds the power to define what constitutes violence, who counts as a victim, and which lives are deemed "worthy" of mourning. It is precisely within this monopolized structure of definitional power that the legitimization and invisibility of gender-based violence are achieved. Using Pierre Bourdieu's theory of "symbolic violence" as an entry point, this section will analyze in depth how gender-based violence is ignored and taken for granted in social cognition, thus being institutionally concealed.


1

How is gender-based violence legitimized?

— Starting from Bourdieu's concept of symbolic violence

Discussions on the systemic concealment of definitional power over gender-based violence must return to the origins of cultural formation and development. Bourdieu introduced the concept of "arbitrariness of culture," arguing that all cultural systems are fundamentally human constructs—historical products arising from the activities and interests of specific groups, which serve to legitimize unequal power relations among the population. Symbols, as one form of cultural carrier and a type of capital, generate benefits precisely through their function of legitimization, thereby turning symbols themselves into a form of violence (Bourdieu, 1977). The legitimizing function of symbols is essentially a collaboration between the symbolic system and dominant forces, maintaining and transmitting the existing political order without the awareness of those subjected to it.


Regarding the relationship between symbolic violence and gender-based violence, Bourdieu elaborated in An Invitation to Reflexive Sociology. He argued, "Gender domination is a typical manifestation of symbolic violence." Symbolic violence refers to violence imposed on a social actor with their own complicity. Social actors are knowing agents who, even when subjected to social determinants, can contribute to the efficacy of these very mechanisms by shaping them. In fact, the systemic concealment of gender-based violence is precisely achieved through such "complicity" between victim and perpetrator. Social actors often fail to recognize the violence imposed upon them; instead, they accept and legitimize it. Bourdieu termed this phenomenon "misrecognition." Social actors tend to view the world as self-evident and naturally accept its current state, because they hold a set of basic, pre-reflective assumptions. These assumptions are formed through subtle persuasion framed as the "order of things" (Bourdieu, 1998). Unlike Gramsci's theory of hegemony, symbolic violence does not require deliberate "manufacturing" or "persuasion," nor does it rely on coercive force to prevent victims from breaking norms. This is because the legitimacy of social order is not "the product of calculated, goal-oriented propaganda or symbolic deception," but rather the result of actors spontaneously perceiving the world as self-evident (Bourdieu, 1989). Thus, Bourdieu also referred to symbolic violence as "gentle violence" (Swartz, 2006).


The gender domination and violence established through "misrecognition" are in fact embodied as a form of bodily constraint. The conceptual construction of biological sex and the practice of embodied politics jointly constitute the somatization of gender domination—a process premised on, and simultaneously reinforcing, gender differences. This dual process of internalization imposes distinct sets of dispositions on men and women. The masculinization of the male body and the feminization of the female body lead to the embodiment of cultural arbitrariness, which continuously constructs the unconscious. The specific efficacy of male-centered theories of social justice in claiming definitional power over gender-based violence lies in deeply inscribing a relationship of domination onto a biological factor (a social construct naturalized as biology), thereby legitimizing this relationship (Bourdieu, 1989). After reinforcing the male-centered position in gender and labor division through the process of biological reproduction, men de facto gain dominance in gender relations and further consolidate their own interests by defining gender-based violence, establishing a system of gender domination over women.


To better conceal their intentions of domination and avoid potential risks (such as resistance from the oppressed), the male-dominated order employs a series of tangible mechanisms to mask the violent nature of male hegemony, further reinforcing the tendency toward misrecognition among the dominated, normalizing gender-based violence so that the majority voluntarily and subconsciously regard breaking such norms as shameful, or at least perceive it as an "abnormal state." In this process, the creation of "rituals" plays a crucial role. Violence is not merely a conceptual process; it must be enacted and completed through bodily experience, realized through repeated ritualized practices. Children's games are, according to Bourdieu, favorable rituals for reinforcing masculine traits in male bodies—especially those with clear sexual connotations, such as competitions over the amount or distance of urination, or homosexual shepherd games. Furthermore, societal game choices are also confined within gendered boundaries, such as honor games or war games considered suitable for demonstrating masculine courage, or politically, commercially, or scientifically valued activities widely recognized in developed societies, in which men often hold monopolistic positions.


And as the targets of constrained behavior—the recipients of violence—women are allowed to participate in a game positioned at the opposite end from men: soft, fragile, passive, and inward-looking. From the separation of boys and girls into different lines during childhood, to differences in the colors and designs of clothing and supplies, food portion sizes, responses to menstruation, feelings of shame around sexuality, and the tense discomfort of being objectified and stared at as sexual beings, women throughout their development receive disciplinary instructions from family, school, and society. Through repeated performance, these instructions ultimately form socially ingrained bodily habits, all leading toward their final purpose: women entering the institution of marriage as symbolic economic units, continuing social reproduction and increasing men's symbolic capital within the hidden framework of gender-based violence. The most "ingenious" aspect of this symbolic violence lies in the introduction of emotions and responsibility into its logic, wrapping what is essentially a naked material relationship in a warm, sentimental package—an even more concealed yet enduring mechanism of control. Power becomes permanently embedded in the bodies of the dominant through configurations such as recognition, admiration, respect, and love (conjugal love, parental love), persisting long after the social conditions that produced them have disappeared. Thus, we observe that once external constraints vanish and formal freedoms are gained—such as the right to vote, access to education, and opportunities to pursue all professions, including political ones—self-exclusion and the notion of "natural vocation" take over, continuing to operate in place of overt exclusion (Bourdieu, 2002). Women, confined by discourses of emotion and duty, are further stigmatized as willingly self-masochistic. This form of domination shifts the responsibility for women's oppression onto women themselves, implying that they choose to be submissive or even enjoy being dominated—that they "derive pleasure" from their own mistreatment—while the real perpetrators remain outside, hidden and untraceable. In this way, male domination is further solidified.


2

Whose pain deserves mourning?

—From grievability to the erasure of intersectionality

Butler's concept of "grievability" can be used to explain why women's experiences of gender-based violence are often ignored or underestimated. "Grievability" refers to whether a life is socially recognized as having value worth mourning upon death. If a life is deemed unimportant within the social power structure, the harm it suffers—or even its death—will not be taken seriously. Therefore, whether a life is mourned upon death reflects whether society has assigned value to that life. For certain marginalized groups—such as women, transgender individuals, and sexual minorities—their lives are not fully acknowledged as valuable by society, so their injuries or deaths may be categorized as the result of personal negligence or as normal, acceptable outcomes. The harm they suffer fails to provoke social concern or institutional reform. This structural neglect results in a monopoly over the definition of violence, causing many instances of gender-based violence to be overlooked and not recognized as serious violence, thereby institutionally obscuring the victims' suffering.


Crenshaw's theory of intersectionality further illustrates how gender-based violence against marginalized groups is systematically obscured. Intersectionality helps us understand how systems of race, gender, and class intersect to impact women of color, especially in contexts of gender-based violence such as domestic violence and sexual assault. The marginalization experienced by Asian women is not merely the sum of racial and gender discrimination, but rather a multiplicative effect—greater than the sum of its parts. Intersectionality can be likened to a traffic intersection: discrimination is like the flow of traffic passing through the intersection, possibly coming from one direction or another. If an accident occurs at this intersection, the cause may be a vehicle from any one direction—or even vehicles from all directions acting together. Victimization from gender-based violence does not stem from a single identity, but from the combined effects of multiple identities such as race, gender, and class. When society attempts to explain violence through a single dimension—such as "gender" or "culture"—it is like only seeing the vehicle from one direction while ignoring the impacts from other directions. Not all victims of gender-based violence are mourned equally; some, due to their intersecting marginalized identities, remain invisible and unacknowledged by society even after death or victimization. Intersectionality determines that certain individuals not only lack institutional protection when experiencing violence, but also fail to be included in the public narratives of mourning after the violence occurs (Crenshaw, 1991).


3

Rape logic and cultural attribution

—The everyday complicity of epistemic violence

Attributing gender-based violence to culture is also part of structural erasure. Gender-based violence against Asian women is often attributed to culture, thereby avoiding accountability from legal systems, institutions, and power structures. For example, oppression of Muslim women or gender inequality in East Asia is frequently generalized as cultural tradition, thus concealing systemic factors such as inadequate laws, patriarchal oppression, and colonial history. Female victims from certain racial, religious, or national backgrounds are instead treated as internal cultural issues, their victimization not taken seriously, not "mourned," and even used in turn to reinforce stereotypes about their communities. Phrases commonly heard about Asian women—"It's their family tradition," "It's just how that generation does things," "Chinese women are naturally more conservative"—blur the conceptual boundaries of violence, preventing violent acts from being named as "violence," and thus evading institutional response. (Volpp, 2000)


"Rape logic" can also describe how society systematically denies, rationalizes, deflects, or even blames victims for the existence of gender-based violence. It applies not only to sexual violence but also broadly to the mechanisms that obscure the entire structure of gender-based violence. Rape logic is very common when gender-based violence is scrutinized by the public, including victim-blaming statements such as "She was dressed too provocatively," "She shouldn't have been out late at night," or "If she didn't resist, she must have consented"; cultural attribution, such as "This is normal in some cultures" or "That's just how traditional culture is"; and the normalization of violence as "unavoidable," for example, treating rape as an overflow of male sexual instinct—"He was drunk, he couldn't control himself." As a mechanism of erasure, rape logic shifts gender-based violence—especially sexual violence—from being clearly labeled as "violence" into ambiguous territory, leading the public to direct blame toward the victim. The culture of blame and rape logic form a complicit structure, attributing violence to "cultural differences" or "personal choices," thereby weakening perpetrator accountability and rationalizing victimization. If society already believes through rape logic that "she was at fault," then her suffering becomes "not worthy of mourning." For racial minorities, immigrants, transgender individuals, and other groups, the erasure mechanisms of rape logic are even stronger, as they are often more systematically dehumanized. (Annie, 2015)


When intersecting identities are weakened by discursive systems and cultural attributions, gender-based violence ceases to be recognized by society as "violence." The denial of the right to mourn is not a consequence, but the core logic of erasure—violence remains invisible precisely because it has never been allowed to be mourned. After ritualized performances have framed women's basic responses to violence, media dissemination further exacerbates women's disadvantaged position in the face of gender-based violence on a broader scale. In the discursive production strategies of mass media—from text to context—different forms of masculinity interact and permeate one another, becoming a crucial mechanism in the operation of patriarchal social order. Through the daily reproduction and representation of marginalized images of women in media products, the media continuously reinforces stereotypes about women, ultimately shaping societal expectations of women's roles and influencing audiences' gender perceptions. (Bi Furong, 2015)


Through the forces of market and capital, women's scope of action is narrowed into a safe and controllable range, continuously reproducing existing gender relations. Professionalism, elite culture, and commercial power together constitute a triple discrimination against women. These three forces interact to form a "media conspiracy" within the patriarchal system—one that excludes women from certain domains while luring them into others, thereby reinforcing male-dominated gender power structures in society. (Lin Fangmei, 1996) Women's bodies are commodified as symbols for sexual consumption, a form of violence often masked by aesthetic or economic value, and widely normalized across everyday life. Whether in fashion magazines, commercial advertisements, films, television, or the entertainment industry, women's bodies become sexual resources catering to men's desire to "look." This sexual compulsion is difficult to challenge, and its violent nature is ignored within male-dominated gender orders, instead becoming an unspoken, collectively enjoyed resource. In the social sphere, women's issues are consistently prone to being trivialized or misinterpreted. Women's suffering is often unspeakable and unrecognized, perpetually met with discrimination and stigma. Exploitation by interest groups and attacks from public opinion leave women used and then discarded. An image of the victim who is never perfect gradually becomes an invisible social cognitive structure. The displacement of contradictions dilutes the criminal consequences of violence, intensifies the phenomenon of "misrecognition," and further strengthens the systemic concealment of gender-based violence.


Starting from Bourdieu's theory of symbolic violence, we understand how gender-based violence is legitimized as part of social order through cultural construction and embodied habituation. Through Butler's concept of "grievability" and Crenshaw's lens of "intersectionality," we see that violence does not merely happen to individuals, but is the result of selective "mourning" and "forgetting" within discursive systems. Moreover, the logic of rape and cultural attribution, as everyday mechanisms of epistemic violence, further obscure institutional responsibility and the power of perpetrators. Together, these three elements constitute a monopolistic structure over the definition of gender-based violence: whether violence is recognized, deemed worthy of response, or should be named, is often not determined by the survivor's experience, but by the social system that holds discursive power. This structural "definitional failure" not only produces silence and misrecognition, but also intensifies the persistence and invisibility of gender oppression. Only by deconstructing this concealment mechanism across epistemological, institutional, and cultural dimensions can we truly advance the "redefinition" of gender-based violence and secure voice, justice, and the right to be mourned for marginalized groups.


IV. Epistemological Revolution: Dismantling Narrative Violence



In the previous sections, we discussed how gender-based violence is legitimized through the monopoly of definitional power, mediated by social cognition and cultural mechanisms. Next, we will explore how, in the face of this predicament, we can practically achieve an epistemological revolution regarding gender-based violence, thereby dismantling gendered narrative violence.


1

Žižek's "Ideological Interruption": Breaking the Invisible Boundaries of Violence

In confronting the systemic concealment of gender-based violence, Žižek argues that we must create an "ideological interruption." This means we should not merely add new narratives, but cause the existing narrative itself to collapse. He contends that the key to ideology's persistence lies in its provision of a seemingly complete and natural picture of reality, within which all deviations can be rationalized. For example, domestic violence can be explained as marital conflict, and harm against queer communities can be downplayed as cultural difference. Therefore, cognitive revolution should generate "undigestible situations"—untimely speech, heterogeneous expressions—that tear open the witness of a perfect narrative (Žižek, 1989). The #MeToo movement is an excellent example. When "non-normative" voices—anonymous, emotional, lacking "evidence"—are collectively presented, the mechanism that previously required "rationality," "self-control," and "clarity" in exchange for being heard collapses. Such actions create fissures, forcing us to stop interpreting violence through familiar languages and emotional patterns, allowing previously unacknowledged experiences of harm to emerge as symptoms of "system failure."


2

Arendt's Counter-Narrative Path: Reconstituting the Existence of Violence Survivors

On the other hand, in understanding how to achieve an epistemological revolution on gender-based violence, Arendt offers a path that differs from ideological critique but aligns with it in practice. For her, the core of transformation is not to destroy existing narratives, but to allow people to appear as "who" they are, rather than "what" they are (Arendt, 1958). Specifically, Arendt argues that we do not need more statistics to prove the existence of violence, because such a narrative mode allows experiences of violence to exist anonymously, abstractly, and functionally. What we must do is allow survivors to be present in their own way, participating in public life through their own language, emotions, silences, and even fragmented expressions. This actually resonates with the core idea of the recently popular academic concept "body politics," which emphasizes ethical awakening of the public through the visibility and action of bodies. From Arendt's perspective, this shift in narrative mode is about constructing a "world"—a space where pain, memory, and encounters can be collectively borne, a realm where individual existence is no longer concealed.


3

Constructing Counter-Public Spheres: Reshaping the Power of Narration

Nancy Fraser's (Fraser, 2021) research tells us that marginalized groups need to create their own "counter-public spheres," relatively safe spaces where they can practice speaking out, develop their own discursive systems, and then effectively challenge dominant epistemic frameworks. These counter-public spheres operate under unique logics. Unlike mainstream public spheres that pursue universality and neutrality, counter-public spheres acknowledge and embrace difference and emotionality. Here, women do not need to translate their experiences into the language of "rationality" and "objectivity," but can express their experiences through "illegitimate" emotions such as anger, fear, and pain.


Women and marginalized groups can challenge mainstream epistemic hegemony by creating their own online communities, discursive symbols, and communication strategies. The success of the #MeToo movement lies largely in its creation of a "counter-narrative" space. Within this space, women no longer need to recount their experiences according to the prescribed script of the "victim," but can redefine what sexual violence is through their own language and rhythm. The value of these spaces lies not only in providing platforms for women to speak, but also in creating a new epistemic practice—collective validation of experiences and negotiated construction of meaning. This counter-narrative exhibits a decentralized quality (Fraser, 1990). Its power lies not only in challenging existing definitions of violence, but also in redistributing the power of narration. Women shift from being objects "spoken about" to subjects who "speak for themselves," from victims "defined by others" to survivors who "self-define."


4

Creating Dissensus: Redistributing the Sensible

The theory of "distribution of the sensible" also provides a crucial perspective for understanding epistemic violence. Rancière argues that politics is not fundamentally about the distribution of interests, but about the "distribution of the sensible"—the invisible mechanism that determines what can be perceived and what is excluded from perception (Rancière, 1999). In the context of gender-based violence, this means the "invisibility" of violence is not accidental, but the result of the distribution of the sensible. Society, through a series of subtle mechanisms, places certain forms of violence outside the realm of "perceivability." Space is politicized—home is constructed as a "private sphere," thus violence occurring within it is excluded from public concern. Time is also redistributed—some violence is categorized as "something from the past," thus losing its present urgency. Even bodies themselves are hierarchized—certain bodies are seen as "naturally vulnerable," so harm against them is normalized. The subtlety of this operation lies in the fact that it is not achieved through brute prohibition, but through shaping our very capacity for perception. When we say a woman is "overly sensitive," we are in fact recalibrating the boundaries of the distribution of the sensible—marking her perceptual capacity as "abnormal," thereby rendering her experience less credible.


One way to dismantle such narrative violence is to generate "dissensus"—that is, to disrupt the existing distribution of the sensible and forcibly bring invisible violence into the visible realm (Rancière, 2011). This is not simply "exposure," but a redefinition of reality itself. In the context of gender-based violence, moments of dissensus often occur at the intersection of personal experience and collective awakening.


When a woman first realizes that what she experienced was not "normal" interaction but sexual harassment, she creates a moment of dissensus. This moment not only changes her understanding of past experiences, but more importantly, it opens up the possibility of redefining reality. If such personal awakening resonates with the experiences of other women, it may form collective dissensus, thereby challenging the existing distribution of the sensible. Creating such dissensus requires "the courage of testimony"—the courage to speak out even without the guarantee of being believed. It also requires "an ethics of listening"—an openness willing to have one's own epistemic framework challenged by others' experiences.


5

Reclaiming Language: Conceptual Innovation and Discursive Reconstruction

The limits of language are the limits of the world. Language is not merely a tool for describing reality, but a force that constructs reality. When we lack appropriate vocabulary to describe a certain experience, that experience remains trapped in a state of "unspeakability." For example, before the concept of "sexual harassment" was coined, related experiences could only be vaguely described as "inappropriate behavior" or "boundary-crossing actions." This linguistic absence is not accidental, but a product of power structures—only when an experience is deemed "important" does society develop corresponding vocabulary for it (Fricker, 2007).


Therefore, conceptual innovation and discursive reconstruction become crucial components of epistemological revolution. In the context of gender-based violence, many concepts have been created through feminist movements. The emergence of concepts such as "emotional labor," "violence of the gaze," "atmospheric coercion," and "PUA" not only provides women with tools to articulate their experiences, but more importantly, redefines what counts as violence. Even more significantly, such conceptual innovation is often accompanied by a redistribution of discursive power. From "victim" to "survivor" and then to "resister," each lexical shift reflects women's redefinition of their own identities. This redefinition not only changes women's self-perception, but also transforms society's understanding of gender-based violence.


6

Body Politics and the Authority of Experience: Reconstructing the Epistemic Subject

Merleau-Ponty's (2013) phenomenology of the body tells us that we come to know the world through our bodies. The body is not merely an instrument of perception, but a site where meaning is generated. In the context of gender-based violence, women's bodily experiences are often dismissed as "subjective" or "unreliable." Yet in reality, these experiences contain profound insights into power relations. When a woman feels that a certain gaze is "uncomfortable," her body is performing a complex social analysis. This analysis may not be fully expressible in rational language, but it accurately identifies the inequality of power relations and potential threats.


Women's collective bodily memory likewise constitutes an important epistemic resource. Ahmed's (2013) "affective politics" points out that experiences of pain are sometimes collectivized. When women share their painful experiences, they are not only seeking personal healing, but also constructing collective knowledge. The significance of this body politics lies in its redefinition of what counts as valid knowledge and who can be a producer of knowledge. It challenges the myth of the "disembodied rational subject" in traditional epistemology, proposing instead an embodied, affective, and relational epistemology.


The ultimate goal of dismantling narrative violence is to enable everyone to become the authority of their own experience, and to ensure that every experience receives the respect and serious attention it deserves.


Five, Conclusion: Building Multidimensional Anti-Violence Frameworks


This article aims to offer a broader, intersectional framework for understanding gender-based violence—violence is not merely an individual act of harm, but the long-term result of social structures, linguistic orders, and recognition mechanisms (Butler, 2004; Fraser, 2000). We emphasize that the starting point for understanding gender-based violence should not be narrow, abstract definitions in legal texts, but rather the traumatic experiences endured by specific individuals. It is precisely these experiences that reveal the real forms of institutional harm, and provide the moral foundation and political direction for anti-violence action (Hirsch & Khan, 2020).


In reality, there are some productive public practices that not only enrich theoretical imagination around structural gender-based violence, but also demonstrate how different fields can intervene in violence in their own ways.


Some practice projects emphasize the synergistic relationship between public communication and institutional change, demonstrating a dual-track approach of transforming social awareness and advancing policy. The "Spotlight Initiative," jointly promoted by the United Nations and the European Union, is a typical example. Since its launch in 2017, the initiative has carried out cross-sectoral cooperation in 25 countries worldwide to comprehensively address violence against women and girls (UN Women & EU, 2023). In terms of public communication, the Spotlight Initiative has reached nearly 384 million people through large-scale media campaigns, digital technologies, and educational interventions, with a particular emphasis on engaging men and reflecting on and reconstructing gender roles (UN Women & EU, 2023). For example, in Kyrgyzstan, the initiative developed a game titled *Spring in Bishkek*, using interactive simulations to help young people identify and resist "bride kidnapping," an illegal but still prevalent practice (UN Women, 2022).


At the institutional level, the Spotlight Initiative has collaborated with national governments to support the development or revision of 548 laws and policies, helping 13 countries double their conviction rates in gender-based violence cases (UN Women & EU, 2023). Additionally, by training key groups such as parliamentarians, judges, religious and traditional leaders, the initiative has improved the effectiveness of law enforcement and public awareness of legal rights. For instance, in Papua New Guinea, the project supported the establishment of a dedicated parliamentary committee on gender-based violence and helped secure the country's first-ever national budget specifically addressing gender-based violence (UN Women, 2022). This dual strategy of advancing "narrative change" and "institutional reform" enables public opinion to effectively link with national institutional transformation, enhancing the systemic nature and sustainability of anti-gender-based violence efforts (UN Women & EU, 2023).


In other practices, community mobilization and the transformation of social roles have become crucial components of anti-violence work. For example, the Indian organization Breakthrough's "Bell Bajao" campaign uses community theater, mobile video vans, and television advertisements to encourage ordinary men to take the symbolic action of "ringing the bell" when they witness domestic violence, thus moving beyond the binary framework of victim/perpetrator (Breakthrough, 2012). Similarly, in the Middle East, the Egypt-originated HarassMap project uses text messaging and social media platforms to encourage citizens witnessing street sexual harassment to report incidents in real time, and combines training programs with universities and businesses to promote the "bystander intervention" approach. This mobilization strategy reshapes people's understanding of the role of "witnesses" in sustaining or dismantling violence, and its communication model has been replicated in Lebanon, Jordan, and other countries, becoming a new regional paradigm for social engagement (Abdelmonem & Galán, 2017).


Furthermore, a series of actions centered on art, bodily expression, and collective memory offer unique pathways to reveal and reflect on structural violence, transcending linguistic, institutional, and cultural boundaries. The Chilean art collective LasTesis launched the protest performance *A Rapist in Your Path* (*Un violador en tu camino*), using collective recitation, dance, and symbolic gestures to publicly denounce institutional gender-based violence (de Fina Gonzalez, 2021). This performance quickly spread globally, inspiring hundreds of spontaneous response actions and forming a transnational practice of collective body politics. The Monument Quilt project in the United States and Mexico stitches survivors' narratives onto red fabric panels and exhibits them in public spaces such as universities, plazas, and museums, giving "trauma" spatial and public dimensions (Martin & Gray, 2025). People no longer understand gender-based violence solely through text or legal language, but instead develop new understandings of trauma through the interplay of silence and visibility, remembrance and participation.


Although these actions take place in different countries and employ diverse methods, they resonate with each other in their underlying principles: violence does not begin with a single moment of physical harm, but is gradually produced through long-term cultural discipline, institutional arrangements, and linguistic mechanisms (Foucault, 1977). Therefore, responses to gender-based violence must also unfold across multiple dimensions: from institutional design to daily interventions, from technological engagement to emotional expression, from language in communication to artistic transformation.


At the public policy level, governments can further clarify the multidimensional nature of gender-based violence and systematically integrate it into broader social governance, education systems, labor policies, and urban planning (WHO, 2013), while promoting the establishment of cross-sectoral, multi-stakeholder anti-violence cooperation mechanisms. In the policy-making process, there should be greater awareness of gender norms embedded in public spaces, and careful assessment of institutional arrangements that appear "neutral" on the surface but have discriminatory impacts in practice. For example, regarding mechanisms for parental leave distribution or police response procedures to domestic violence reports, systematic gender impact assessments are recommended, along with related reforms to enhance institutional fairness and public safety (European Institute for Gender Equality, 2020).


For media, cultural producers, and educational institutions, assuming "narrative responsibility" is crucial. We call on all parties to re-examine the representation of gender roles in public communication, avoiding the reduction of violence to "isolated incidents" or "private matters." Mainstream media can strengthen structural and systemic reporting on anti-gender-based violence issues, avoiding sensationalism or stigmatization; education authorities can systematically incorporate anti-gender-based violence knowledge into primary, secondary, and higher education curricula, enhancing younger generations' ability to recognize and intervene in violence through gender equality education and emotional education (UNESCO, 2015). At the same time, the film, advertising, and social media sectors can encourage more diverse expressions of gendered experiences, breaking through existing gender stereotypes and creating safe, respectful spaces for narrating experiences of violence.


For non-governmental organizations and community activists, continuously expanding the breadth and depth of grassroots mobilization and community participation is a key foundation for building a sustainable anti-violence ecosystem. Such support systems may include: helplines for survivors, psychological support groups, legal counseling services, and public anti-discrimination training programs (Krause, 2015). At the same time, the widespread adoption of "bystander intervention" mechanisms should be strongly promoted, helping more members of the public understand what safe and effective actions they can take when witnessing potential violent situations. Research shows that when ordinary people can timely identify signs of violence and make appropriate interventions, they often effectively prevent escalation (Banyard et al., 2009). Therefore, cultivating an "anti-silence culture" and enabling it to take root in various organizations and communities is a crucial step in expanding the boundaries of anti-violence practice.


For the general public, every individual can be part of the movement against gender-based violence. We encourage people not to wait until "becoming a victim" to raise awareness, but instead to start with small, everyday practices and actively participate in the social process of preventing violence. Specifically, actions can be taken in the following areas: (1) Learn basic knowledge about identifying violence and intervention techniques, paying attention to covert forms of violence in intimate relationships, workplace interactions, and public spaces; (2) When witnessing inappropriate behaviors or remarks, gently intervene by asking questions, offering support, or stopping the behavior, or report the situation to professional organizations; (3) Proactively follow and share information and resources related to preventing gender-based violence, expanding public discussion on structural violence; (4) Reflect on one's own position and behavior within gender roles, and remain vigilant against internalized gender biases that may be reproduced in family education, intimate relationships, or peer interactions; (5) Participate in diverse anti-violence initiatives such as volunteering, community projects, and artistic expression, collectively creating a safer, fairer, and more just social environment.


Promoting a structural response to gender-based violence is not merely about optimizing systems or improving governance efficiency, but also a collective construction of public ethics. As Judith Butler has pointed out: "Those lives that cannot be recognized are often the ones most vulnerable to violence" (Butler, 2004). We urgently need to co-create a social ethical space that acknowledges differences, understands vulnerability, and respects diverse forms of existence—and efforts against gender-based violence are a crucial starting point in this process.
