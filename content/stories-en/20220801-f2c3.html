Introduction

At the beginning of the film *The Godfather*, there is a description like this: "She went to the movies with him and came home very late; I did not object. Two months ago, he and another boy took her for a drive, tricked her into drinking whiskey, and tried to take advantage of her. She resisted and preserved her chastity and dignity, but they beat her like an animal. When I arrived at the hospital, her nose was broken, her jaw shattered, and had to be wired shut." This reminded me of a passage from *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*, Rousseau said: "The more women are educated like men, the less power they will have over us." This is exactly what I want to say. I don't wish for them to have power over men; I only hope they have the ability to govern themselves.


*Roe v. Wade*

Last month, the overturning of *Roe v. Wade* in the United States sparked diverse reactions around the world, and the aftermath continues to ripple. I believe many American women are now forced to accept lives in which abortion is not permitted. Even if just one woman must bear the cost of this ruling, it is unbearable and unacceptable. If the surroundings are endless darkness, how can despair and thorns not bloom in the heart? Nothing has changed—the bustling city, the peaceful hometown, even the wind still blows—but many things and emotions are no longer felt. Fortunately, there are those who offer a faint care that illuminates another's path.

*Never Rarely Sometimes Always*

Just as depicted in the 2020 film *Never Rarely Sometimes Always*, which won the Silver Bear at the Berlin Film Festival. This is a screenplay written by American female director Eliza Hittman, inspired by a 2012 news event in Ireland where a female dentist requested an abortion but was repeatedly denied, ultimately leading to her death.


The story, tinged with quiet sorrow, follows Autumn, an ordinary teenage girl from a small town in Pennsylvania, juggling school and part-time work, who discovers she is pregnant and, accompanied by her cousin Skylar, takes a bus to New York to have an abortion. Two adolescent girls, while mocking their own insignificance by calling themselves "prostitutes," must make transactions to earn enough money for their return trip. Fortunately, at this crossroads in life, they lean on and care for each other.


In this film, there are two scenes featuring women holding hands, portrayed with great delicacy. This reminded me of a religious account: during the persecution of Manichaeism, two Manichaean followers would extend their right hands upon meeting, signaling their willingness to redeem one another.


After watching the film, I excerpted three online comments:

01 

What probably gives this otherwise mundane and conventional subject a glimmer of light is the subplot introduced by the man who hits on them on the bus. It is precisely the mixture of power and economic motivation behind seemingly ambiguous emotions that gives rise to the "second sex" in male-female relationships, along with the social structures attached to it—the complex reality of emotional power dynamics between men and women.

02

The pervasive sense of oppression created by numerous close-ups and extreme close-ups is like an invisible wall everywhere, This is precisely the dilemma women face: one day, they suddenly have to face the world, only to realize they are utterly powerless to respond. Although the overall mood remains calm and subdued, the moment "never, rarely, sometimes, always" appears, one still feels struck. From now on, I will never mispronounce this film title again.

03 

"Juno" exists only in movies; this film is the real teenage abortion story. The director's sense of cinematography is excellent, and the climax arrives unexpectedly with the words "never, rarely, sometimes, always." I love the girls' quiet bond and their pinky promise.

*Godard: Image, Sound, and Politics*

These viewing notes and comments also reminded me of a scene in Godard's film *Breathless*, where Patricia asks the writer: "Do you think women have a place in modern society?" We know this is a common story, but for Patricia in the film, it is a real dilemma: should she pursue her career as a journalist, or allow herself to be absorbed into Michel's world, co-starring in a crime drama combining violence, his love for her, fleeing from the police, and the promise of freedom and wealth in Italy? The writer's reply is both brief and incisive. He slowly removes his sunglasses, leans forward, gives Patricia a meaningful look, and says, "Yes, if she is extremely attractive, wearing a stripper's outfit, and dark sunglasses." This seems to suggest: a woman's contribution to modern society can be measured by her sexual appeal. Godard perceived the forces that stereotype women and suppress them into weakness and helplessness, exposing how women are deprived when transformed into images within a consumer society. For Godard, the image of women is the true foundation of consumer society.—*Godard: Image, Sound, and Politics*


Perhaps we don't fully understand, nor can we avoid the ever-present "other," but I have still decided to resist it. I hope my female friends can forgive the bluntness in the above words, because I sincerely want to point out the suffering women face, so they may realize: gentle words, sensitive emotions, elegant dresses, and narcissus-like love—these are fragile and cannot last. Women should strive to gain strength in both mind and body, for beyond the words of demands, rules, and duties lies a place of joy, brilliance, and beauty. Without even thinking, I've already decided I will do the same.
