" There's Still Tomorrow

The film There's Still Tomorrow uses only black, white, and gray throughout, a simple and restrained color palette that evokes memories of the spirited Princess Anne riding wildly through the streets of Rome, instantly transporting viewers back to mid-20th century Italy.


Director Paola Cortellesi is a national treasure of Italian cinema and also one of the film's lead actresses. In 2024, she won the David Giovani Prize for Progress and the Best Actress Award at the 69th Italian David di Donatello Awards for this film. That same year, the film became the top-grossing release in Italy and entered the top ten highest-grossing films in Italian cinematic history.




In China, the film also achieved notable success, quickly entering the Douban Movie Top 100 after its release.


The story unfolds through the eyes of Delia, a woman from the lower class. D elia endures each day under her husband Ivano's unpredictable emotional and physical abuse while caring for their family of six. In her repetitive and monotonous life, only her daughter Marcella, her close friend Marisa, and the brief moments of freedom walking alone through the streets offer her slight comfort and relief. Yet, the arrival of a mysterious letter is about to completely change Delia's life…


As a housewife: numbness after discipline


"Slap", A sharp slap opens the entire film—and Delia's ordinary, monotonous day.


Italy in 1946, like a seed sprouting from ruins, begins to grow and awaken. In the early postwar period, people in Italy were experiencing famine, material shortages, and the complete collapse of social order. A wave of unemployment swept the nation, and like other impoverished working-class families, Delia took on the dual burden of work and household care to ease financial pressure.




In the film, Delia begins her day at dawn, managing the household: opening windows for ventilation, preparing and setting the table, serving meals like a restaurant waiter, standing aside while the family eats, ready to serve at any moment. During this time, she must also care for her bedridden father-in-law. Beyond domestic duties, she works odd jobs outside the home to earn a meager income to support the family. Yet, her daily hard work brings her no reward or respect. Under the strong oppression of patriarchy, Delia is like a "domestic slave", burdened by the heavy shackles of social identity, subjected to the discipline and scrutiny of both society and family, a submissive wife, a gentle mother, a tireless caregiver—anything but herself.

Discipline as a concept in power and social control can be traced back to French philosopher Michel Foucault's analysis of the operation of power in Discipline and Punish. He argued that discipline is not merely a form of punishment, but a technique that shapes individuals by controlling their bodies and consciousness—an omnipresent mode of power in modern society.


In the film, Ivano constructs the household as a small patriarchal society centered on himself. Delia, as the primary object of subjugation, numbly obeys Ivano's authoritarian rule, endures his violent tyranny, and is gradually disciplined into a "human tool" sustaining his power. Over time, discipline flows like a silent river, seeping into Delia's self-perception, making her accustomed to maintaining the existing power structure and order through self-surveillance and restraint, treating the shadow of violence as part of daily life. A seemingly rational but actually cruel process of self-discipline is unconsciously completed. Countless small patriarchal family units, like grains of sand, pile up and converge, ultimately creating a collective, numb human desert—silent and still.




When Delia suffers domestic violence, neighbors sitting in the yard attribute the brutality to "Ivano's temperamental nature"; when Marcella forgets the potatoes on the stove, Delia nervously whispers warnings, vividly revealing her fear of her husband and her internalized discipline. "Occasionally, just once, beat her hard so she understands. That's how I treated your mother—have you ever seen her talk back?" The conversation between Ivano and his father about how patriarchal society enslaves women across generations resembles a solemn transmission, tightening the gears of patriarchy, link by link, ever tighter.


The director's handling of the father-son dialogue is extremely skillful. The scene blends ironic music with a duet dance, using black humor to soften the bloodiness of "domestic violence" and reduce viewer discomfort. Yet, this expression does not diminish the severity of "domestic violence"—the contrast created by the light rhythm still fills this "discipline" with a suffocating, fearful atmosphere, seeping in like poison gas.


A s a mother: awakening of self-awareness and mutual empowerment


In interviews, director Paola Cortellesi shared that her original motivation for making this film was to let her daughter understand the history of women's growth in their country, to know where their rights came from, and to remember the fierce struggles people once waged to defend those rights.


In Italy at the time, many families hoped their children—especially daughters—could improve their family's situation and achieve social mobility by marrying into wealthy, respectable gentlemanly households. Marcella, Delia's daughter, is a typical embodiment of this belief.



Delia initially believed such an arranged marriage would spare her daughter from living as she had, but the convergence of memory and reality, along with her fiancé Caliò's domineering and forceful attitude, leads her to re-examine her daughter's marriage. "Marriage is a good thing, but marriage is for life—there's no turning back." Delia's sigh goes unanswered by her daughter. Raised under patriarchal order, Marcella has already unconsciously inherited the gender role discipline; invisible intergenerational trauma has unknowingly passed the mother's shackles to the next generation.


Vivian Rakoff first proposed the "intergenerational trauma" concept in the 1960s. Intergenerational trauma refers to unresolved psychological trauma within a family that is unconsciously passed from one generation to the next through behavioral patterns, emotional responses, or parenting styles. Marcella's fear of male power and indifference toward women's rights under patriarchal society are indirect reflections of this trauma acting upon her.




In the end, mother Delia personally "destroys" her daughter's marriage, actively voicing opposition by "blowing up" Caliò's café, leaving her saved money for her daughter's education, and personally participating in voter registration. At that moment, Delia and Marcella truly mirror each other, letting her become her past, letting her move toward her future. Any small change is like a breeze, gently smoothing the sand-piled patriarchal desert.


As women, she and they: there's still tomorrow


At the film's end, the story returns to the mysterious letter mentioned at the beginning. It is a voter eligibility notice.



Beyond Delia, the director also portrays many other female characters. Though their objective conditions and life circumstances may differ from Delia's, their positions within family and society are highly similar. They too lack agency, choice, and even the right to look men in the eye. They are restrained under patriarchy, motionless, yet unresisting. The only woman living with self-awareness is the tailor shop owner, who always mends clothes and insists, "I decide for myself." Her presence, though as small as a firefly's glow, is like the ballot in Delia's hand—just one, yet so significant.




One ballot may not immediately transform a long-suppressed life, but it means women's subjectivity is no longer "chained," no longer imprisoned in the dark corners of the mind. For countless women like them, this ballot is like a tiny spark, igniting inner voices that will surely gather into a torch of liberation in pursuit of selfhood.


"I have no shield to protect myself,

no weapon to fend off enemies,

no helmet to hide myself,

no saint to intercede for me,

all I have is the tongue in my mouth,

even if you cut off my tongue, I will not stop,

sorry, I will still sing—with my mouth closed."
