Abstract:

Belgium has a generally gender-friendly work environment. According to the World Bank's 2023 Global Gender Pay Index, Belgium ranked among the top 10 countries with smaller gender pay gaps, scoring 79.6%. Despite this overall positive trend, existing research has failed to adequately present and analyze the working conditions of women from an ethnic perspective. To address this gap, this article focuses on Asian women—one of the immigrant groups with relatively strong growth potential in Belgium—as the primary research subject. Through qualitative research methods such as literature analysis, this study examines the general situation of Asian women in Belgium, their workplace treatment and rights protection, and working conditions. It aims to help Asian women currently in or planning to move to Belgium understand Belgian labor policies, clarify existing and future difficulties and challenges, advocate for fair workplace rights and well-being for Asian women, and promote the enhancement of Asian women's influence in the workplace.



I

Overview of Asian Women in Belgium



Classification and Characteristics


Academic definitions of Asian women vary, primarily categorizing them by country of origin and immigrant generation. Overall, Asian women in Belgium are commonly defined as "immigrants." By country of origin, Asian women in Belgium mainly refer to women of Asian descent from East Asia—primarily China—India and broader South Asia, and Southeast Asia. By immigrant generation, they are divided into first-generation and second-generation immigrants; among immigrants, there is also a distinction regarding whether they hold Belgian citizenship.


These definitions and classifications reflect the following characteristics of this group:

(1) The group is racially, culturally, and linguistically diverse, with complex composition and uneven levels of education;

(2) They are identified as outsiders due to external characteristics such as appearance and language;

(3) Career choices and professional development are easily constrained by the acquisition of citizenship;

(4) The population is growing, the group is expanding, and educational attainment is continuously rising.


These characteristics also reflect, to some extent, the challenges faced by Asian women in Belgium, such as language barriers hindering career advancement, racial discrimination, gender discrimination, and low social participation due to strong family dependency.



Education Level


There is a clear generational difference in the educational background of the Asian female immigrant population in Belgium. First-generation female immigrants generally have lower levels of education due to limitations in socioeconomic development conditions, and due to their upbringing, they still face obstacles in using Belgium's official languages (such as Dutch, French, German, etc.). Second-generation female immigrants have generally higher levels of education than first-generation immigrants, influenced by improved economic conditions, better adaptation to the host environment, internet access, and family expectations and emphasis on education. As second-generation immigrants, they possess higher language proficiency and have established a strong foundation for "localization" through early business experiences (Dongen, 2018). Moreover, many second-generation female immigrants' parents tend to plan high-status careers for their children in advance—such as doctors, accountants, or teachers—aiming to enhance their children's social mobility (Pang, 1998). At the same time, deepening international friendly exchanges, the spread of high-quality educational resources, and relatively smooth talent mobility channels have also attracted more international students to pursue higher education in Belgium and become part of the new generation of immigrants.



Employment Situation


Influenced by education, economic, and other factors, the employment situation of Asian women also shows generational differences. Overall, research indicates that compared to native-born individuals, first-generation immigrants not only face significant disadvantages in employment, but their descendants also encounter serious difficulties when entering the labor market. In other aspects, the employment gap for first-generation immigrants is more pronounced than for second-generation immigrants (Piton & Rycx, 2020).


First-generation female immigrants are primarily engaged in small-scale, family- or relative-run individual businesses. These businesses are characterized by long working hours, tedious and stressful tasks, weak sense of self-fulfillment, and low self-worth, barely sustaining basic family livelihoods (Pang, 1998). These businesses are mainly in the service sector, with typical examples including family-run restaurants, Asian supermarkets, massage parlors, hair salons, caregiving, and domestic work. With increasing competition in individual enterprises, rising numbers of new immigrants, and emerging issues such as racial discrimination, Chinese immigrants in Belgium have also attempted to start businesses in other fields, such as garment manufacturing and distribution, import-export businesses, and wholesale distribution of Chinese-made products (Dongen, 2018). Overall, the number of first-generation female immigrants is relatively higher than that of male immigrants of the same generation—for example, the female-dominated trend observed among first-generation immigrants from Thailand (Fresnoza-Flot, 2022). Asian women often bear greater family responsibilities, such as being full-time homemakers, which means first-generation female immigrants must dedicate more of their personal time or remain further removed from the workforce. It is noteworthy that in Belgium, even domestic work is categorized as formal or informal, documented or undocumented. Formal and documented work refers to domestic work that is officially recorded by the government and protected by relevant laws and policies. Informal and undocumented work refers to the phenomenon of starting work without a work visa, often through the black market or intermediary agencies. Such work typically falls outside the scope of legal protection and is not recorded by the government; it is the primary way for many first-generation immigrants or those who have not yet obtained a work visa to find employment (Magalhães, 2017).


In contrast, the new generation of female immigrants has more career options and is no longer confined to family-run businesses or restricted by traditional social gender roles. Their career choices reflect characteristics of modern women and contemporary workplaces, such as free career selection, higher pay and benefits, more comprehensive institutional protections, and a more ideal work-life balance and so on.


II

Workplace Treatment and Rights Protection




Labor Protection


Belgium's labor market for immigrants is subject to strict regulation, yet disparities with native workers persist. Although Belgium provides immigrants with formal pathways into the labor market and social security systems, it has one of the most strictly regulated and institutionalized labor markets in Europe (OECD, 2013; 2016). Relevant laws and regulations limit the number of people employed in relatively low-wage jobs, thereby reducing job opportunities for foreign immigrants.


Furthermore, compared to standard employment, these low-wage jobs require almost no formal training and offer little to no additional benefits or job security. This is particularly true for service-sector or family-run jobs, which are commonly held by many Asian women. This creates a fundamental issue: the employment gap between foreign-born and Belgium-born individuals is among the highest in the European Union (Lens, Marx & Vujic, 2018). In-work poverty has become a long-term condition, especially among Asian female workers engaged in low-quality jobs (Liu, 2019).


Despite various integration policies, Belgium performs poorly in labor market integration for immigrants. In international comparisons, Belgium is one of the countries with the lowest immigrant employment rates globally (Lens, Marx & Vujic, 2018; Timmerma et al.), and the situation for non-EU immigrants is even worse (Timmerma et al.). Given these policy and regulatory conditions, Asian women—especially those without Belgian citizenship—face significant challenges in labor market integration and labor protection in Belgium.


However, Belgium does have numerous organizations and initiatives dedicated to strengthening labor protections for immigrants. For example, the European Network of Migrant Women provides legal and social support to female migrant workers, including assistance in accessing healthcare, education, and employment opportunities. Additionally, other organizations such as the International Labour Organization (ILO) work more broadly to promote the rights of migrant workers.



Healthcare Protection


Although the European Union has consistently advocated for improved health policies targeting immigrants, Belgium has only addressed racial health inequities to a limited extent. Belgium has incorporated all European directives aimed at improving immigrant health into its legislation, but its commitment to diversity remains weak. Data on racial health disparities are extremely scarce. Moreover, to avoid exacerbating long-standing racial tensions and political conflicts, and to prevent public institutions from infringing on individual privacy rights through data collection, use, and dissemination, Belgium has enacted specific legislation—the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)—prohibiting public institutions from collecting racial data. As a result, few public health policies truly recognize the importance of cultural diversity or the needs of immigrants and ethnic minorities (Dauvrin et al., 2012).


Moreover, most public health programs (such as the National Cancer Plan) or blueprints in Belgium do not address the specific situations of immigrants, resulting in inadequate provision of health services, prevention, and health promotion. Healthcare organizations have no formal obligation to consider diversity, leading to implicit denial of healthcare discrimination. Although laws against racism and discrimination exist, the Equal Opportunities and Anti-Racism Centres serve only an advisory role and are not regarded as enforcement bodies. Furthermore, neither of the two most important health executive agencies has made a strong commitment to advancing equity and diversity. Finally, state-funded cross-cultural mediators are limited to hospitals, and there is almost no interpreting service available in outpatient settings (Dauvrin et al., 2012), creating barriers for Asian women with limited language proficiency to access medical care. Therefore, within racialized and gendered public health frameworks, Asian women are likely to face unjust healthcare coverage.



Social Security


According to the introduction to Belgium's labor and employment system by the Ministry of Commerce of the People's Republic of China: Any employee hired by a company must participate in Belgium's national social security system, unless an applicable international treaty provides otherwise. For example, workers from European Economic Area member states may remain covered only by their home country's social security system for a limited period (up to a maximum of 24 months). Belgium has also signed bilateral agreements with several other countries, including Argentina, Chile, Israel, Poland, Switzerland, Turkey, and the United States. All enterprises must register with the Federal Social Security Agency (known in Dutch as "RIZIV"). Companies are required to pay social security contributions quarterly. Employers are also obligated to withhold and remit employees' social security contributions to the social security administration. The social security system is primarily funded by contributions from employers and employees. Employers must contribute approximately 32.44% of white-collar workers' total income and approximately 38.44% of blue-collar workers' total income. Employees contribute 13.07% of their income (Ministry of Commerce of the People's Republic of China, 2011).


Belgium's social security system includes healthcare, sick leave, unemployment benefits, family allowances, maternity leave, and statutory pensions. This system provides significantly broader welfare coverage than in most English-speaking countries. For Asian women living in Belgium, foreign workers and their employers are categorized into two main types: temporary secondment or long-term employment.


In the case of temporary secondment, the employment relationship remains with the foreign company that temporarily sends employees to Belgium due to organizational, restructuring, or operational control needs. The employee remains subject to the foreign company's arrangements—he or she continues to receive instructions from and report to the management of the foreign company. Seconded employees cannot be staff members of a Belgian company. The seconded employee's salary continues to be paid by the foreign company. In addition, the seconded employee continues to be covered by the social security system of the country where the foreign company is based.


In the case of long-term employment, the foreign worker becomes an employee of the Belgian company and is listed on the Belgian company's payroll. The employee follows instructions from the local Belgian company and performs work under the authority of the Belgian company's management. The employee also becomes covered by Belgium's social security system. (Ministry of Commerce of the People's Republic of China, 2011)


However, for the social security contribution status of Asian women employed in small or micro businesses, there is currently no clear data available, nor are there specific policies in place to ensure social security coverage for this group. In most parts of the world, female workers who are long-term engaged in low-paid, insecure work (i.e., informal employment), or who lack independent economic capacity, are difficult to fully cover under social protection measures.



Leave arrangements: taking maternity leave as an example


Currently, there is insufficient data on maternity leave for Asian women employed in small or micro businesses in Belgium. The following provides a general overview of maternity leave policies in Belgian enterprises.


Female employees in Belgium are entitled to a maximum maternity leave of 15 weeks during their employment. However, one week before childbirth and eight weeks after childbirth are mandatory by law, while the additional six weeks can be taken either before or after childbirth at the employee's discretion.


Under certain circumstances, maternity leave can also be replaced by paternity leave. Employers do not pay wages during maternity leave or during paternity leave that replaces maternity leave. Allowances during maternity leave are paid from a special fund within the social security system. If an employee is pregnant, the employer may only terminate the employment contract for legitimate reasons unrelated to the pregnancy. Both parents—mother or father—can take three months of unpaid parental leave within four years of the child's birth or adoption (eight months if the child has a serious disability).


Additionally, fathers are entitled to a special 10-day "paternity leave" within 30 days after the child's birth. The first three days are fully paid by the employer, while the majority of wages for the remaining seven days are paid from a special fund within the social security system.



Legal protection of Asian women's workplace rights


1

Relevant provisions

Like most other countries, Belgium provides protections for certain groups of workers. For example, once an employer becomes aware of a female employee's pregnancy, the employee gains certain special rights. These rights include a prohibition on the employer terminating the employment contract due to pregnancy, from the time the employer learns of the pregnancy until one month after the end of maternity leave. If there are indeed serious grounds for terminating the contract, and these grounds are unrelated to the employee's health condition, then this special protection does not apply. If such grounds cannot be proven and the termination is based solely on the employee's pregnancy, the female employee has the right—besides exercising other rights—to compensation equivalent to six months' total salary.


Similarly, during paternity leave, the employment contract cannot be terminated for the aforementioned reasons. If these prohibitions are violated, the employee has the right—besides exercising other rights—to compensation equivalent to six months' total salary.


2

WBL Index

The WBL Index was developed by the World Bank and is composed of three dimensions: Women, Business, and Law Its purpose is to examine how laws affect women at various stages of their working lives (The World Bank, 2023). The 2023 Women, Business and the Law report presents an index covering 190 economies, structured around the life cycle of a professional woman. A total of 35 questions are scored across eight indicators. The overall score is calculated as the average across all indicators, with 100 representing full points (data based on laws and regulations). The index applies to major business cities (such as Brussels). Different rules may apply in other jurisdictions, so local legislation should be reviewed.


Using this methodology, Belgium scored 100 out of 100. Belgium's overall score is higher than the regional average for high-income OECD economies (95.3). In the high-income OECD region, 14 economies scored 100 out of 100.

This data indicates:


(1) Relative advantages: Belgium achieved full marks in areas involving restrictions on freedom of movement, laws affecting women's ability to work, laws affecting women's pay, marriage-related restrictions, laws affecting women's return to work after childbirth, restrictions on women's entrepreneurship and business operation, gender disparities in property and inheritance, and laws affecting the amount of women's pensions.


(2) At the same time, this data has certain limitations: the surveyed groups and questions do not include situations of foreign immigrants and foreign workers, data on Asian women remains missing. For a long time, international female migration has been considered driven by marriage and family factors, while their contributions in labor markets and economic activities have not been included in research. As some researchers have pointed out, "Although the presence of female migrants is acknowledged, they are always seen as secondary, as people who migrate for family reunification. Their contributions to the economy and society of the host country are therefore ignored" (Li Qirong, 2008)..


Three

Workplace Marginalization and Discrimination


Asian women in Belgium face multiple forms of marginalization and discrimination in the labor market. Marginalization manifests concretely in the invisibility of their voices and labor, while discrimination can appear as blocked promotions or violated rights due to the non-dominant status of their gender, language, culture, and racial characteristics. These two factors intersect and jointly affect the workplace survival of Asian women in Belgium. For example, Asian women engaged in small or micro businesses are often officially registered as "housewives." Their contributions are broadly regarded as those of their husbands or other family members (Pang, 1998); some Asian women employed in enterprises or government agencies often have their voices weakened due to their physical appearance. Notably, these difficulties are characterized by their long-term nature.



Discrimination and Marginalization from an Ethnic Perspective


Since the 1990s, many studies have revealed this from a racial perspective. A 1994 study found that among immigrant groups, Chinese people are relatively less identifiable, especially Chinese women. They seem to be absent from public discourse on migration and national policies in Belgium, lacking legal recognition or a voice of their own. They are often categorized as "them" in policy-making processes. This "us/them" dichotomy is not unique to Belgian scholars and policymakers but exists in social sciences (Allen, 1994) and migration policies across many European countries (Lutz, 1994; Ramakers, 1996). Notably, academic research on racial discrimination has primarily focused on Moroccan and Turkish communities, leaving the experiences of Asian women largely invisible. Despite differences in racial backgrounds among research subjects, findings reflect common challenges faced by migrants in Belgium. For example, in education, migrant youth perform worse than native youth. Migrants also face disadvantages in employment opportunities. A 1997 study by the Centre for Equal Opportunities and Opposition to Racism showed that in hiring for semi-skilled jobs, local candidates were prioritized over (male) migrant applicants.


The emphasis on identity differences often reinforces xenophobia and nationalism in Belgium, while the physical appearance and language differences of Asian women often make them victims of structural injustice. First-generation Asian women, due to language barriers, are frequently treated differently by Belgians. However, even second-generation Asian women who meet formal integration criteria—such as speaking Belgium's official languages in public and adhering to similar behavioral norms—are constantly reminded of their perceived differences, such as being exoticized, thereby exaggerating the differences between Chinese and Belgian women. Moreover, many Chinese women continue to be stereotyped as foreigners, denying their reality of settlement. For instance, according to Pang (1998), some second-generation Asian migrants who speak Belgium's official language as their mother tongue are still treated as outsiders and praised for their language proficiency.



Discrimination and marginalization from a gender perspective


In legal and formal work environments, Belgian women remain underrepresented in management and senior positions. Nine out of ten CEOs in Belgium are men. The number of women in managerial roles is among the lowest in Europe. The reasons behind this are not related to job wages but point to broader socioeconomic factors such as overall levels of socioeconomic development and distribution systems. Unaffordable childcare costs, insufficient childcare facilities, and lack of flexible working hours are some of the main causes (Warburton, 2019). Combined with the aforementioned ethnic issues, the dual identity of being both "Asian" and "female" presents significant barriers to career advancement for many professional women. Although each year the Institute for the Equality of Women and Men publishes new data on Belgium's gender pay gap and welcomes individuals who believe they have experienced discrimination to file complaints with the Institute for the Equality of Women and Men, this otherwise comprehensive legal and complaint system only offers Dutch- and French-language options, making it unfriendly to non-native speakers such as Asian women and increasing the difficulty of seeking help.


Furthermore, many Asian women in Belgium work in informal or illegal sectors. These women are often victims of human trafficking primarily involving economic and sexual exploitation, with Thai women being particularly vulnerable. A survey by the European Commission on Migration and Home Affairs found that while overt prostitution has decreased in Belgium, there has been a rise in hidden forms of prostitution, with women forced to work in private homes, caregiving services, and massage parlors (Belgian EU Commission, 2024). This reinforces public stereotypes of Asian women as "white men's accessories," "submissive to money," or "easily accessible." Trapped within highly organized human trafficking and labor black markets, these Asian women are labeled as "illegal residents," existing outside legal protection and policy oversight, suffering from rights deprivation, hardship, discrimination, and violence (Li Qirong, 2008).



Initial efforts to address discrimination and marginalization


Fortunately, as awareness and empowerment grow within the Asian women community and academic research deepens, solutions to these long-standing challenges are gradually becoming clearer.


An increasing number of Asian women are taking action to combat discrimination. For example, during the pandemic, Asian women launched the hashtag #IAmNotAVirus on social media platforms like Twitter, sharing their daily lives and thoughts to counteract racial biases against Asians (Times, 2020). At the same time, academic research is beginning to focus on this group, covering topics such as religious beliefs, female genital mutilation, dietary habits and nutritional intake, and social invisibility among Asian women in Belgium, providing a foundation for future research and policy development.


Four

Conclusion


Overall, Belgium has a relatively robust and humane labor, healthcare, and social security system, yet women still face difficulties breaking through the "glass ceiling" in the workplace, and disparities in work environments and professional experiences persist due to differences in race, identity, and occupation. These disparities are particularly evident among Asian women. In our research, we found that due to legal restrictions on collecting racial data and systemic discrimination, data on minority women, disabled women, and working-class women remain difficult to obtain. The absence of gender and race-disaggregated data silences Asian women's voices beyond mainstream socioeconomic activities. Therefore, how to collect as much data as possible on the workplace experiences of Asian women in Belgium, depict their working lives, and address the challenges they face due to gender, race, and legal status within the current legal framework could become a long-term direction for our efforts.
