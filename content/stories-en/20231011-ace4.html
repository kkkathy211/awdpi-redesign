Stepping Out of the Trap, Building Our Own Ark—Reading *Beyond Limits*



*Beyond Limits: A Feminist Correspondence* is a collection of exchanged letters between Japanese scholar Chizuko Ueno and writer Ryōmi Suzuki. From different perspectives, the two women engage in a dialogue on topics such as love and sex, marriage, work, independence, and freedom.


Through continuous intellectual confrontation and reflection, they reveal how gender oppression and social structures constrain women. At the same time, they demonstrated how she overcame and transcended these limitations through self-empowerment. Every woman has the strength to change her own destiny, break free from societal constraints, escape the traps she faces, and build her own ark.


01

The fear of weakness: being in a disadvantaged position yet refusing to identify as a victim


Refusing to acknowledge that one is experiencing harm, refusing to admit being a victim—this mindset is called "fear of weakness" (kowaku). This is a concept sharply introduced by Professor Chizuko Ueno in her discussion with Ryōmi Suzuki on "erotic capital" in this book.



Ryōmi Suzuki grew up in a wealthy, highly educated family; her parents were renowned professors and translators. She excelled academically from a young age and was admitted to the prestigious Keio University. Behind this façade, however, she secretly sold used underwear at a fetish shop at age 16, worked as a hostess at a cabaret club during university, and later chose to enter the adult film industry, starring in over 70 pornographic films.


Suzuki's letters are deeply contradictory: on one hand, she says that being in this industry has allowed her to experience the profound suffering of being a woman; on the other hand, as a well-educated woman, she attempts to find theoretical justification for her actions.


"I once strongly identified with Hakim's concept of 'erotic capital,' believing it could help women escape the victim identity and potentially serve as an auxiliary line that makes us more complex and powerful."


In her letters, she not only tries every way to rationalize her choices but also wraps them in sophisticated academic terminology and masks them with a cold, indifferent attitude. She speaks of "self-determination and self-responsibility," defending her participation in the industry as a personal choice, arguing that using erotic capital for fair transactions is no different from other market exchanges.


In her reply, Professor Ueno sharply criticizes this view:


"Calling something 'capital' when its owner has no control over it is clearly wrong. Capitalism is fundamentally tied to private ownership, yet it is questionable whether the bearers of erotic capital—women—actually possess ownership over it."


The concept of erotic capital reminds the author of organ trafficking: some argue that if one party is willing to buy and the other to sell, what's wrong with a fair market transaction? This rhetoric closely resembles the defenses made by those who justify "erotic capital," echoing the same rebuttals used to deny their own "fear of weakness." However, while organ trafficking exploits the poor, erotic capital typically exploits women.


This "fear of weakness" stems from elite women who refuse to acknowledge their disadvantaged position. By insisting on their subjectivity and self-determination, they seem to shed the identity of victimhood along with the social shackles they carry, as if the harm caused by sex work never occurred. This is a passive coping attitude.



As Professor Ueno puts it, the more an individual insists on "self-determination" as a subject, the more the structure can evade responsibility. Clearly, the long-term effects of this "fear of weakness" are detrimental to progress.


"Those who are structurally disadvantaged may indeed benefit from their disadvantage in the short term, but in the long run, this leads to the reproduction of the structure."


02

"Self-determination" pushing some women away from feminism


Chizuko Ueno once mentioned in her writing that sex work is an economic activity for women. Male consumers are well aware that this act cannot truly be measured by money, so they shift this psychological burden onto the other party in the transaction—the woman.


The most common and powerful defense used by men is "self-determination." .


In some narratives, sex workers' accounts are highly professionalized—put differently, they carry the attributes of commodities. They emphasize women's agency and their voluntary choice to become sexual objects. This is a cliché of the sex industry, as women's agency serves to absolve men of their sexual desires.


However, these narratives do not represent all sex workers. This makes the concept of "self-determination" somewhat distorted and misleading, because this choice is not one that everyone can truly have. Some women narrating their experiences may have more options—even the option to leave—while women with no other choices and forced into sex work cannot escape the night-time world. Here, "self-determination" may appear to grant women the power of choice, but it can also be used by men to exploit and justify their actions, thus concealing the real difficulties and inequalities women face in sex work.


The more an individual insists on "self-determination" as a subject, the more the structure can evade responsibility. Those who are structurally disadvantaged may indeed benefit from their disadvantage in the short term, but in the long run, this leads to the reproduction of the structure.


When we place greater emphasis on individual freedom, autonomy, and the right to make decisions about one's own life, responsibilities based on social gender structures and hierarchies may relatively weaken. In more severe cases, this could be seen as a way of evading responsibility, allowing structural inequalities to persist while placing blame entirely on the individual. Even if some disadvantaged groups leverage their social roles to gain attention, neglecting long-term social structural changes may unintentionally reinforce and reproduce existing structures of inequality.



This "self-determination" exists not only in sex work but in all aspects of life.


A concept that should have been feminism's pride and weapon has instead become a cunning justification—a form of exploitation and oppression under the name of "voluntariness." This has caused many women to miss the opportunity to embrace feminism. In their view, they have choices; their circumstances are the result of self-determination, unrelated to oppression by social structures. In the workplace, women may choose to accept lower pay and fewer career advancement opportunities in exchange for more family time and the ability to care for their families. This choice is made by themselves and appears to embody "self-determination."


Yet, behind this choice lies pressure from gender stereotypes and traditional gender roles, which allows the structure of workplace gender inequality to be maintained and reproduced. This will be elaborated in detail in the next section.


03

The struggle to balance family and career


Women are caught in a dilemma between pursuing a career and fulfilling family responsibilities: facing competitive pressures at work, they must work harder to prove their abilities in order to achieve equal status and opportunities with men; yet at the same time, they also bear the traditional responsibilities of caring for family and children. This dual role often forces women to make sacrifices, making it difficult for them to achieve balance between career advancement and family happiness.


The book reveals the negative impact of social structures and gender norms on women. For a long time, society has generally believed that women should prioritize family, treating childcare and household care as their primary responsibility, while downplaying the pursuit of a career. Ms. Ryōmi Suzuki shares her observation: "In the overall social structure, even women in relatively privileged positions deeply desire family life. This makes me realize that independence gained through work has its limits. Even if one appears independent enough, can build confidence through work, enjoys financial comfort, and clearly feels social recognition, some still cannot firmly embrace being a single elite. The immediate solutions people around them can offer are either finding a partner with marriage in mind or developing eccentric hobbies or keeping pets. To be honest, I have no idea what else could stop them from longing for the security marriage brings..." Beyond marriage, women lack too many safeguards.


Traditional beliefs lead to gender discrimination and career bottlenecks for women in the workplace, making their professional development more challenging. At the same time, these beliefs also affect women's own values and aspirations to some extent, making them more prone to doubt their abilities and impose self-limitations.



Ryōmi Suzuki asks Chizuko Ueno: When you entered a male-dominated workplace as a woman and achieved accomplishments unprecedented for women, did you ever feel that men became excited simply because 'this is written by a woman' or 'a female scholar wrote about a sexy girl'? While we passionately articulate our views, we are consumed by misplaced attention. Even if a girl's work is highly successful, men still gaze at her full breasts—this vulgar fascination also stems from rigid gender perceptions.


The issues revealed in the book are widespread in modern society. Promoting gender equality awareness and policy change is undoubtedly important; only by transforming public perception can women's dual roles in the workplace and at home be fully understood and respected. This book has sparked reflection on gender equality and family values.


04

Conclusion


Women still have a long way to go.


Thanks to feminist movements over the past few decades, women today have developed a certain level of awareness when facing gender discrimination. However, gender inequality does not only manifest as overt discrimination and oppression. It is deeply rooted in our social structures, cultural traditions, and even in our language and ways of thinking. These hidden forms of gender inequality are even harder to detect.


The essence of this book lies precisely here: Feminism does not tell us, "You can choose not to be a victim," but urges us to understand, "You should never have had to be a victim in the first place."


The former shifts responsibility onto individuals, making women feel that as long as they work hard to avoid problems, they can escape victimization. Ultimately, this mindset may become a sharp blade, directing blame and pressure back onto women themselves, leaving them to struggle alone when confronting gender inequality, without societal support or understanding. The latter, however, reveals a harsher truth: no one is exempt from the oppression of patriarchal society.


We should learn to see the world from other women's perspectives, understand their pain and struggles, and collectively search for solutions and ways forward. Women must cultivate empathy, solidarity, and take action.
