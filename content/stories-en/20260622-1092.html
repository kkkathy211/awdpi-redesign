Many Asian women experience similar moments while living and working overseas.


Wanting to speak up in a meeting,

but choosing silence due to the high language cost;

being "kindly overlooked" in professional settings,

with the assumption that "you probably don't need to take on that role";

in the process of constantly adapting,

gradually beginning to wonder—

Am I truly seen?


We rarely discuss these moments in depth.


Because they don't seem "serious," and often come with a certain politeness and reasonableness.


Yet it is precisely these subtle moments that constitute the real experiences of many Asian women in international environments:


expected to be "excellent," yet assumed "not quite suitable for leadership";

praised as "gentle and reliable," yet overlooked for "strong expression";

allowed to exist, but not necessarily permitted to define the rules.


This time, we want to bring these issues together for discussion.


On June 28, AWDPI (Asian Women Development Plan International) will host its June online roundtable forum.


We have invited three Asian women from different fields and countries:


They come from the fields of art, business, and life sciences..


Their career paths differ, but all have been exploring the same question in their respective fields:


How to find one's place and establish one's voice within a system not designed for us.




The three guests:



Yuki

Art agent | UK immigration planning expert | Pole dancer

A postgraduate student at the University of Cambridge, active in the UK and European art ecosystem, long involved in cross-cultural art projects and exhibition curation, while also working in UK immigration and identity planning.


Her career spans art, institutions, and bodily expression—a real-life practice of how "multiple identities can coexist."


Miki

Tax advisor at one of the Big Four accounting firms | Women's community advocate

Currently working in tax in Ireland, while consistently organizing women-friendly events and focusing on building social support systems in elderly care and disability assistance.


Within a highly structured professional system, she strives to preserve warmth in life and the possibility of choice.


Amy

Founder of the Chinese Health and Life Sciences Community

Long dedicated to connecting innovators and scholars across health, technology, and business, committed to building cross-industry collaboration networks and advancing Chinese influence in the global life sciences sector.


In a highly specialized, male-dominated field, she explores the path "from participant to rule-shaper."



What will we discuss together?



This is not merely a conversation about career development alone.


It is more deeply a conversation about "how to become oneself." of the conversation.


We will explore the following questions:


 On speaking up 

  • When English is not our native language, how do we express ourselves in the workplace?

  • Are those moments when we think "never mind, I won't say it" really just about language?

 On invisible boundaries 

  • Do Asian women truly face an "invisible ceiling"?

  • Or have we already internalized our own boundaries within a structural narrative?

 On leadership 

  • Must leadership equal assertiveness, extroversion, and fast-paced expression?

  • Or can it take different forms?

 On identity 

  • When we no longer define ourselves by job titles,

  • what other words can we use to describe ourselves?

 On belonging 

  • As overseas Asians, how do we build communities that genuinely support one another?

  • Is belonging more important, or the freedom to "leave at any time"?



Why is this conversation important?



Often, Asian women occupy a delicate position:


We are encouraged to work hard,

but rarely have serious discussions about "how to be seen";

We are expected to adapt,

but rarely encouraged to define the rules;

We are considered "capable enough,"

but not necessarily assumed to be "fit for leadership."


These structures are not always explicitly present,

yet they genuinely affect every career choice, every time we speak, every opportunity we receive.


What we hope to leave behind is not just answers,


but something softer yet firm:


a possibility that "I don't just have to adapt";

a belief that "I can redefine myself";

and a sense of connection that "I am not alone."
