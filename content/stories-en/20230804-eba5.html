Becoming Jane




The film *Becoming Jane* is adapted from the biography of author Jane Austen. While we feel sorrow for Jane and Tom's love story in the film, we are even more moved by Jane's courage and rationality. At the end, Jane writes her own regrets and those of her sister into *Pride and Prejudice*: "All my novel characters will have happy endings." Perhaps the author wished to write down the ideal romance she longed for in life, or perhaps the happy endings in her novels were meant to compensate for the shortcomings of reality.


In Austen's vision, an ideal marriage includes not only equality and respect, but also freedom and understanding. She hoped this would help people break free from the constraints of traditional thinking, find themselves, and realize their true selves. May everyone also feel the unique charm and powerful strength of the women in her writings.

Legally Blonde




Legally Blonde is a light-hearted, uplifting film full of female empowerment. The protagonist is a typical "blonde beauty" — popular, yet often dismissed by others as a "dumb blonde." After being dumped by her boyfriend, she works hard to gain admission to Harvard Law School, earns a position as a law intern through perseverance, and ultimately graduates with top honours. While everyone assumes that serious and rigid individuals make the best lawyers, Elle stands out by wearing bright, fashionable outfits, keenly identifying flaws in the case, stepping in as acting attorney after her supervisor is accused of sexual harassment, and ultimately winning the trial triumphantly.


As a traditional blonde beauty, Elle enjoys male admiration but is also treated as a male accessory, causing people to overlook that she is an outstanding and warm-hearted professional.

The Lullaby




The "slope" symbolizes the arduous journey every woman faces after becoming a mother—helplessness when the child cries, the husband's absence when the child falls ill, anxiety triggered by delayed child development, and the difficult choices between parenting and work.


Rieko has given up her job to care for her daughter Ayaka full-time, yet this feels like a solitary battle. She must take care of her daughter's daily needs alone, and at night still cook for her husband, turn on the TV, and bring him his dishes—acting like a meticulous nanny. When she is selected as an alternate juror, she feels anxious, as it means entrusting her daughter to someone else's care, something Rieko, who insists on doing everything herself, finds hard to accept.


However, serving as a juror also gives Rieko a chance to observe her own life from a third-party perspective, allowing her to step back from the chaos of childcare. By reflecting on the experiences of the mother in the child abuse case—someone who shares her own parenting anxieties—she begins to examine whether her marriage and life have similar problems. Other women of various ages also serve as jurors, and the inclusion of female perspectives may help promote fairer trials in cases involving female defendants.

" The Worst Person in the World "




*The Worst Person in the World* is a comedy directed by Joachim Trier, telling the story of 30-year-old Julia's struggle between love and self-worth. Though she and successful cartoonist Axel love each other, they eventually part ways due to differing views on having children.


In the end, Julia chooses to pursue her true self and begin a new life. Through comedic form, romantic style, and delicate storytelling, this film portrays the real struggles of urban women in their thirties in Europe, resonating deeply with women around the world.
