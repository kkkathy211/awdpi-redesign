Reader's Letter 

Blooming Tulip:

I don't like interacting with men, because as a child I often saw my father being violent toward my mother, and sometimes I myself was abused by him. In adulthood, I've repeatedly witnessed male supervisors emotionally manipulating women. The memories from childhood are filled with my father's negative energy. I often didn't want to see my father or mother, and wanted to stay far away from them. But in reality, I couldn't distance myself. This repeated internal conflict keeps replaying in my mind—exhausting!

Avoice Response 

First, we deeply understand your past experiences and the conflicting feeling of wanting to escape an environment you despise, yet having to survive within it. Since we may have to adapt to this environment for now, perhaps we can try to understand the motivations behind their behaviors—knowing both ourselves and the other party—focusing on enhancing our abilities to overcome difficulties, rather than fixating on the anxiety these challenges bring. Learning professional knowledge about PUA (Pick-Up Artist tactics) is a good starting point.


When you enhance your capabilities and bridge the information gap that prevents problem-solving, you not only become stronger, but the key to solving the problem will naturally appear in your hands. The phenomenon of "not liking to interact with men" may not, at its core, mean a genuine dislike of men, but rather a fear of being hurt again through interactions with men due to past trauma—an act of self-protection. Bridging the information gap on how to interact with men is precisely the key to resolving this issue.


PUA: Real Tiger or Paper Tiger??


1. Why Most Manipulators Are Men


Some men attempt to gain a sense of control and power through emotional manipulation, perhaps because they are unsuccessful or even failing in their careers, but at least they can fully control their partner. Others strongly identify with gender stereotypes, believing that men should hold authority while women must obey. Manipulators often justify themselves with "love": "I'm doing this for their own good. I'm truly a good person." In reality, however, they seek tangible benefits from emotional manipulation—whether money, time, power, or status. Through covert, skillful use of threats, humiliation, manipulation, and domination, they are able to continuously benefit from such behavior.


1

Gender Stereotypes:

Male Authority, Female Obedience

From a young age, girls are taught to become "caregivers" and "sacrificers"—expected to learn cooking, housework, caring for siblings, and to be gentle and compliant in character, becoming the "virtuous wife and wise mother" for their partners. If they fail, they are labeled "unvirtuous." Meanwhile, boys are encouraged to compete and win; even when men make mistakes, they are more easily forgiven.


In adulthood, women continue to prioritize others' feelings in work and life, trying to please others to avoid disapproval. In family life, for example, women not only prioritize the couple's interests but also ensure their husband and children are satisfied. Men, on the other hand, focus more on personal needs and development, bringing this mindset into family and daily life.


Three factors contribute to men using emotional manipulation in intimate relationships. First, they have a tendency toward domination. Second, they do not respect their partner as an independent, whole person. Third, they accept the role of manipulator, and social systems silently provide a favorable environment for such behavior.



If a man was severely punished by his father during childhood, he may treat his wife and children the same way in adulthood.


2

Social and Cultural Foundation:

Male Dominance and Control

To this day, men still dominate in various areas of society and enjoy more privileges than women. This leads men to perceive the world very differently from women, making it difficult for them to understand how challenging it is for women to survive amid various forms of discrimination and injustice in this "game of life."


In the hope of securing a safe living environment, some women come to believe that only by obeying and relying on male power can they gain loyalty and happiness. But in reality, if a woman abandons her own life goals to please her partner, she will fall into endless isolation should the marriage fail. A better approach is for men and women to respect and appreciate each other, moving forward together toward shared goals within a healthy support system.


3

Manipulation is merely a mask for inner cowardice

When some men face setbacks in work or life, they try to regain a sense of power by manipulating their partner, compensating for the sense of powerlessness they feel elsewhere.

2. Why Most Victims of Manipulation Are Women Gender


1

Deceived by Illusions:

Manipulators skillfully use brief moments of tenderness to win their partner back

In the American drama *Maid*, there is a story where the female protagonist leaves her violent boyfriend and moves into a women's shelter with her daughter. Suddenly, the boyfriend changes his attitude completely, becoming extremely gentle and pleading with her to return home. After observing him for a while, she believes he has truly changed and returns with him. But the next morning, she wakes up in horror to find herself imprisoned by him. Only after great hardship does she manage to escape this "home" again.



This is known as the "cycle of violence"—after an episode of abuse comes a "honeymoon phase," designed to win back the injured woman. Then, the man gives her a glimmer of hope, as if the violence never happened. When the woman begins to trust him again, she re-enters another "abuse phase." This repetitive emotional cycle is merely a skilled form of emotional manipulation. If a woman fails to recognize it, over time she may fall into loneliness and low self-esteem, becoming increasingly dependent on the manipulator and unable to leave.


2

Excessive "Savior Complex":

Manipulators often exploit women's sympathy

Manipulators portray themselves as victims, seeking women's compassion. Women, in turn, see themselves as "saviors," believing that through love they can help the man become a better person, thus unconsciously sacrificing themselves for his sake. It must be understood that helping a man become better does not require a woman to sacrifice herself.


3

Social Environment:

Economic and public opinion pressures make women accept manipulation

If women consider breaking up or divorcing, they not only face immense social pressure but may also suffer from rumors and gossip. Some full-time mothers, due to financial pressure and children, have no choice but to continue living with the manipulator.
