It also applies to Asian women and Asian communities overseas who are concerned about or wish to support the work of the Asian Women Development Plan International (AWDPI). This indicates that the charitable sector is not only a key component of the social support system but also an important force in advancing public interest, promoting community wellbeing, and strengthening social cohesion.

Australia has a mature and highly trusted regulatory framework for charities and not-for-profits. According to ACNC's Australian Charities Report (11th edition), Australia's charity sector generates approximately AUD 222 billion in annual revenue, employs around 1.54 million people, and engages approximately 3.77 million volunteers annually. This demonstrates that the charity sector is not only a vital part of Australia's social support system, but also a major force in advancing public benefit, community wellbeing, and social cohesion.

1. Who regulates charitable organizations in Australia

Swipe to view the English version


In Australia, the primary regulatory body for charitable organizations is the Australian Charities and Not-for-profits Commission (ACNC). The ACNC is a national independent charity regulator responsible for registering charities, maintaining a public register of charities, and monitoring ongoing compliance obligations, including annual information statements and financial reporting requirements. At the same time, the ACNC promotes sector transparency and strengthens public trust by issuing guidance and applying proportionate compliance measures.


Depending on the specific activities of an organization and the state or territory in which it operates, Australian charities must also comply with other relevant laws and compliance requirements. The Australian Taxation Office (ATO) assesses and determines whether an organization qualifies for tax concessions under federal tax law, including eligibility for income tax exemption and endorsement as a deductible gift recipient (DGR) (see Chapter 6).


Fundraising activities are primarily regulated by state and territory laws. Charities must comply with relevant regulations in the jurisdiction where fundraising activities are conducted, which may include registration, licensing, or reporting obligations, and these requirements vary across jurisdictions (see Chapter 5).


In addition,, the *Australian Consumer Law (ACL)* also applies to charitable organizations. Although it is not a law specifically designed to regulate fundraising, it prohibits misleading or deceptive conduct and false statements, including those made in fundraising campaigns and external communications.

1. Who Regulates Charities in Australia


In Australia, charities are primarily regulated by the Australian Charities and Not-for-profits Commission (ACNC), the independent national regulator of charities. The ACNC is responsible for registering charities, maintaining the public Australian Charities Register, and overseeing ongoing reporting obligations, including annual information statements and financial reports. It also provides guidance and takes proportionate compliance action to support transparency and public trust in the sector.


Charities in Australia also need to comply with other laws and compliance obligations, depending on their activities and where they operate. The Australian Taxation Office (ATO) is responsible for determining eligibility for Commonwealth tax concessions, including income tax exemption and deductible gift recipient (DGR) endorsement (see section 6 below).


Fundraising is not regulated by the ACNC. Instead, it is primarily regulated by state and territory laws, and charities must comply with the requirements in each jurisdiction where they raise funds, which may include registration, permits, or reporting obligations (see section 5 below).


In addition, the Australian Consumer Law (ACL) applies to charities in the same way as other organisations. While it does not specifically regulate fundraising, it prohibits misleading or deceptive conduct and false representations, including in fundraising appeals and public communications.




2. Basic requirements for registering a charitable organization

Swipe to view the English version


In Australia, organizations seeking to register as a charity must meet the requirements set out in the *Charities Act 2013 (Cth)* and related regulations, and be eligible for registration with the Australian Charities and Not-for-profits Commission (ACNC). Generally, this includes:


  • having purposes that are exclusively charitable and serve the public interest

  • being not-for-profit in nature

  • not having any purposes that are legally disqualifying (e.g., engaging in or promoting illegal activities or serious criminal conduct)

  • complying with ACNC Governance Standards

  • complying with ACNC's External Activities Standards if operating overseas (External Conduct Standards)


The *Charities Act 2013 (Cth)* also defines the main categories of charitable purposes, including advancing health, education, social welfare, human rights, culture, and environmental protection. Therefore, organizations working in areas such as women's rights protection, anti-discrimination, anti-violence, support for vulnerable groups, and other public interest activities may align with recognized charitable purposes under Australian charity law, provided their purposes are appropriately structured and governed.

2. Core Requirements for Charity Registration


In Australia, an organisation seeking registration as a charity must meet the requirements set out in the Charities Act 2013 (Cth) and relevant regulations, and be eligible for ACNC registration. Broadly, this includes:


  • having only charitable purposes for the public benefit;

  • being not-for-profit;

  • not having any disqualifying purposes (such as engaging in or promoting unlawful activities or serious offences);

  • complying with the ACNC Governance Standards; and

  • complying with the ACNC External Conduct Standards, if the charity operates outside Australia. 


Charities Act 2013 (Cth) also sets out the principal categories of charitable purpose in legislation, including the advancement of health, education, social welfare, human rights, culture, and environmental protection. As a result, organisations working in areas such as women's rights protection, anti-discrimination, anti-violence initiatives, and support for vulnerable communities may, if properly structured and governed, align with charitable purposes recognised under Australian law.



3. Ongoing compliance obligations after registration

Swipe to view the English version


In Australia, charitable organizations must continue to meet compliance obligations after registration. All registered charities are generally required to submit an Annual Information Statement (AIS) to the ACNC; medium and large charities are usually also required to submit annual financial reports. Generally, these submissions should be completed within 6 months after the end of the organization's reporting period.


The ACNC currently classifies charities into three categories based on annual revenue:

  • Small: annual revenue below AUD 500,000

  • Medium: annual revenue from AUD 500,000 to below AUD 3 million

  • Large: annual revenue of AUD 3 million or more


Medium charities typically require their financial reports to be reviewed or audited, while large charities usually require an audit. This tiered arrangement reflects the principle of "larger size, higher transparency and regulatory requirements," while also helping to reduce compliance burdens for small charities.


In addition to ACNC reporting obligations, charities in practice often need to comply with broader compliance requirements. These may include other regulatory rules (such as fundraising laws), industry standards, international frameworks (for cross-border activities), internal policies, and requirements set by funders or donors. Managing these requirements is an important part of daily operations. The next section briefly illustrates this using fundraising and public communication as examples.


3. Ongoing Compliance Obligations After Registration


In Australia, registration is only the beginning of a charity's compliance journey. All registered charities are generally required to lodge an Annual Information Statement (AIS) with the ACNC, and medium and large charities are generally also required to submit annual financial reports. In most cases, these filings are due within six months after the end of the charity's reporting period.


The ACNC currently classifies charities by annual revenue as follows:

● Small: under AUD 500,000

● Medium: AUD 500,000 to under AUD 3 million

● Large: AUD 3 million and above


Medium charities are generally required to have their financial reports reviewed or audited, while large charities are generally required to have them audited. This tiered system reflects a proportionate regulatory approach: larger organisations are subject to higher transparency expectations, while smaller charities face lower compliance burdens.


Beyond ACNC reporting, charities often need to meet a wider range of compliance obligations depending on their scope of work and how they operate. These can include other regulatory requirements (such as fundraising laws), industry standards, international frameworks (for cross-border work), internal policies, and requirements set by donors or grant-makers. 4. Compliance requirements for fundraising and public communication

Swipe to view the English version


When conducting fundraising, project promotion, public statements, or selling goods or services, charitable organizations must, in addition to complying with ACNC governance requirements, also comply with the *Australian Consumer Law (ACL)*. Under the ACL, charities must not:


  • engage in misleading or deceptive conduct

  • make false or misleading statements

  • exert unfair or unreasonable pressure on the public

  • misrepresent the use of funds, organizational identity, or the impact of donations


This means that transparent, accurate, and responsible public communication is not only a matter of brand and reputation for charities, but also part of their legal and compliance obligations. For organizations relying on public donations, establishing clear fundraising descriptions, disclosure of fund usage, and complaint handling mechanisms is essential.

4.Compliance in Fundraising and Public Communications


When charities engage in fundraising, public campaigns, representations about their work, or the sale of goods and services, they may be subject not only to ACNC governance requirements but also to the Australian Consumer Law (ACL). ACL makes clear that charities must not:

  • undertake misleading or deceptive conduct

  • provide any false or misleading representation

  • use unfair or unconscionable pressure tactics

  • misrepresent the use of funds, their identity, or the impact of donations

This means that, for charities, transparent, accurate, and responsible communication is not merely a matter of reputation; it is also part of legal and compliance practice. For organisations seeking donations from the public, it is especially important to maintain clear fundraising explanations, proper disclosure of fund use, and effective complaint-handling mechanisms.



5. Tax concessions and Deductible Gift Recipient (DGR) status

Swipe to view the English version


Eligible Australian charitable organizations may be entitled to various tax concessions, including income tax exemption, goods and services tax (GST) concessions, and in certain cases, fringe benefits tax (FBT) concessions.


These tax concessions are administered by the Australian Taxation Office (ATO) and require a separate endorsement process. While registration with the ACNC as a charity is often one of the prerequisites, it does not automatically confer all tax concession entitlements. That is, the ACNC handles charity registration, while the ATO assesses eligibility and approves tax concession applications under tax law.


Furthermore, not all registered charities have Deductible Gift Recipient (DGR) status. DGR status is a separate endorsement granted by the ATO, allowing donors to claim an income tax deduction for eligible donations, making it particularly important for organizations reliant on public donations. DGR status is assessed and approved based on specific categories and legal criteria. Typically, ACNC registration is one of the prerequisites for DGR endorsement, but it does not guarantee that an organization will meet or obtain this status.

5. Tax Concessions and Deductible Gift Recipient Status


Eligible Australian charities may be entitled to certain tax concessions, including income tax exemption, GST concessions, and, in some cases, fringe benefits tax (FBT) concessions. These concessions are administered by the Australian Taxation Office (ATO) and require separate endorsement by the ATO. 


While ACNC registration as a charity is generally a prerequisite, it does not automatically result in entitlement to all tax concessions. Separately, charities must apply for endorsement to access these concessions, and eligibility is assessed under ATO requirements. The ACNC is responsible for registering charities, while the ATO determines eligibility and administers tax law, including endorsement decisions.


In addition, not all registered charities are endorsed as Deductible Gift Recipients (DGRs). DGR endorsement is a separate ATO status that allows donors to claim income tax deductions for eligible gifts made to the organisation. It is subject to strict eligibility criteria based on specific DGR categories under tax law. ACNC registration is generally required for DGR endorsement, but it does not guarantee eligibility or approval.

7. Conclusion

Overall, Australia's charitable system is centered on clear rules, tiered regulation, information transparency, and public trust. For organizations wishing to carry out long-term public welfare work in Australia, compliance is not merely an administrative requirement, but a crucial foundation for building social trust, expanding collaboration opportunities, and achieving sustainable development.

7. Conclusion


Overall, Australia's charity system is characterised by clear rules, proportionate regulation, transparency, and a strong emphasis on public trust. For organisations seeking to undertake long-term public-interest work in Australia, compliance is not merely an administrative requirement; it is a foundation for credibility, partnership-building, and sustainable development.
