When you are studying in another country, disability is not always easy to see. Sometimes it is one morning after another when depression makes it hard to get out of bed, or a mind that suddenly goes blank from anxiety in the exam room. It can also mean working several times harder than others because of differences in reading, writing, or attention. As the number of international students grows, these visible and invisible physical and mental disabilities are increasingly being recognized on campuses and in workplaces abroad. They include not only physical and sensory disabilities but also mental health conditions (such as depression and anxiety) and neurodivergent conditions (such as attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder and dyslexia), which are especially common among international students.


Yet cultural pressures mean that many Asian students carry a heavy sense of stigma about physical and mental disabilities. They worry about being seen as "not good enough," about "causing trouble for the school," and about "bringing shame on the family." So they put off facing their situation and find it even harder to ask for help. We wrote this handbook to help you put that burden down. Requesting reasonable accommodations has never been a privilege. It is about having an equal chance to take part in study, work, and everyday life. We also hope to support you through a change: from quietly putting up with things and passively receiving help, to actively learning about and claiming the rights you are entitled to, recognizing discrimination, and having the confidence to stand up to it.


Disability is a complex and varied subject. Types of impairment, legal systems, and institutional arrangements differ enormously, and no single handbook can cover everything. This handbook therefore takes as its reference point the places where Asian students most often study. It follows mainly the U.S. system of disability rights, centered on the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 (ADA) and Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973, and compares key points with the UK's Equality Act 2010. That Act applies in England, Scotland, and Wales, but the courts and complaint bodies differ from one part of the country to another, so where this handbook discusses the UK, it follows the position in England and Wales. This choice does not mean that readers in other countries or regions are left out. The core principles of disability protection, such as the prohibition of discrimination, the duty to provide reasonable accommodations, and the prohibition of retaliation, along with the basic approach of keeping records and filing complaints, are shared by many countries and regions governed by the rule of law. What may differ are the names of the bodies involved, the application procedures, and how these interact with your visa status. So if you are studying in Canada, Australia, the European Union, or elsewhere, the approaches and routes set out in this handbook are still worth drawing on, but before you take any specific action, remember to check them with your school's disability services office (Disability Office / Student Support) and with local legal resources.


This handbook covers, in turn: the definitions and main types of disability and disability discrimination; how to recognize disability discrimination around you; ways to respond and seek remedies on and off campus; and the risks you may face in standing up for your rights, with strategies for dealing with them. At the end is a list of on- and off-campus sources of help in the U.S. and the UK. Wherever you are right now, you can turn straight to the part closest to your situation.

 NO.1 

Persons with Disabilities and Disability Discrimination: Definitions and Types


1.1

Definition of Persons with Disabilities and Main Types of Disability



Under the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, persons with disabilities include those who have long-term physical, mental, intellectual or sensory impairments which in interaction with various barriers may hinder their full and effective participation in society on an equal basis with others.


Domestic laws usually go into more detail. The ADA, for example, covers three groups of people: those with a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities, those with a record of such an impairment, and those regarded as having such an impairment. "Major life activities" include tasks such as caring for oneself, walking, learning, reading, thinking, and working, as well as the operation of major bodily functions, such as functions of the immune system, neurological functions, and brain functions. In the UK, the Equality Act 2010 defines disability as a physical or mental impairment that has a substantial and long-term adverse effect on a person's ability to carry out normal day-to-day activities.

Disabilities can be classified in many ways, for example by medical pathology, by legal criteria, or by whether they are visible, and you will need to check the laws of the country you are in. The U.S. Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), for example, lists 14 categories of disability (one of which, "developmental delay," applies only to young children; see the table below). Note that IDEA applies mainly to public education from preschool through grade 12 in the U.S. and does not extend directly to higher education. For international students at U.S. colleges and universities, the main laws are the ADA and Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973. Both use a functional definition rather than a list of specific categories. Even so, IDEA's categories are a useful guide to common types of disability. In addition, under the regulations issued by the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC), the following conditions should generally be found to be disabilities: amputation, autism, bipolar disorder, blindness, cancer, cerebral palsy, deafness, diabetes, epilepsy, HIV or AIDS, intellectual disability, major depressive disorder, mobility impairments requiring the use of a wheelchair, multiple sclerosis, muscular dystrophy, obsessive compulsive disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder, and schizophrenia. The list is meant to make the assessment simpler, and a condition that is not on it can still be protected. ADHD, dyslexia, and other conditions can still be disabilities; you just need to show, based on your own circumstances, how they substantially limit a major life activity.


The UK's Equality Act 2010 uses a similar functional definition, but Schedule 1 to the Act and regulations made under it list several conditions that automatically count as disabilities: cancer (including pre-cancerous conditions), HIV infection, multiple sclerosis, and blindness or sight impairment certified by a consultant ophthalmologist. Severe disfigurement is also generally treated as a disability, and progressive conditions (such as Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease) are protected from the point at which they begin to affect day-to-day activities. Compared with the EEOC list above, the UK's "automatic" coverage is narrower, and other conditions (such as diabetes, depression, and ADHD) still have to meet the functional test.

1.2

Definition of Disability Discrimination


Under the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, discrimination on the basis of disability means any distinction, exclusion or restriction on the basis of disability which has the purpose or effect of impairing or nullifying the recognition, enjoyment, or exercise, on an equal basis with others, of all human rights and fundamental freedoms in the political, economic, social, cultural, civil, or any other field. It includes all forms of discrimination, including denial of reasonable accommodation.

 NO.3 

Exercising Your Rights and Responding to Discrimination: On Campus


3.1

Protection in Advance: Applying for Accommodations



Under the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, reasonable accommodation means necessary and appropriate modification and adjustments not imposing a disproportionate or undue burden, where needed in a particular case, to ensure to persons with disabilities the enjoyment or exercise on an equal basis with others of all human rights and fundamental freedoms.


Domestic laws often set out more detailed rules. In the UK, for example, the Equality Act 2010 imposes a "duty to make reasonable adjustments." Where a provision, criterion or practice, a physical feature, or the absence of an auxiliary aid puts a disabled person at a substantial disadvantage, the organization must take reasonable steps to avoid that disadvantage (for example, by changing its rules, removing physical barriers, or providing auxiliary aids), and it may not require the disabled person to pay any of the costs. Under the U.S. ADA, reasonable accommodation mainly includes making existing facilities used by employees readily accessible to and usable by individuals with disabilities, and adjusting jobs and procedures, including job restructuring, part-time or modified work schedules, reassignment to a vacant position, acquisition or modification of equipment or devices, appropriate adjustment or modifications of examinations, training materials, or policies, and the provision of qualified readers or interpreters.


Accommodations usually apply only to academic work after your request is approved, and they are not retroactive. In other words, requesting accommodations is about protecting your future academic performance, not correcting past grades. So even if you are doing fine right now, it is best to register early so that you are eligible: having accommodations you never use is far better than trying to fix things afterward. If you wait until after an exam or after submitting an assignment and then ask for a grade change, a retake, or a chance to resubmit because of your health or because you had not requested accommodations, the university can usually refuse based on this principle.

(1) Documentation: Medical Diagnosis and Disclosure


Medical documentation is the foundation of any request for reasonable accommodations. The university's disability services office is not a medical provider, so it has to rely on the medical documentation you provide to decide whether you are eligible. Documentation that a DSO in the U.S. or the UK will readily accept usually has to be written by a qualified professional (a doctor or psychologist) and include three key elements:


a. A clear diagnosis: ideally including the specific medical name or code (such as a DSM-5 or ICD-10 code) rather than a vague description.


b. Functional limitations: this is the part DSOs care about most. Your doctor should not only say what condition you have but also explain exactly how it gets in the way of your studies.

  • Weak example: "The student feels anxious."

  • Strong example: "Due to generalized anxiety disorder, the student experiences cognitive freezing and panic attacks under the high pressure of timed exams, which prevents the student from demonstrating their actual level of knowledge."


c. Recommended accommodations: the support your doctor, based on clinical judgment, believes you need. For example: "Extended exam time of 50% is recommended" or "A separate, low-distraction exam room is recommended."

  • Privacy tip:  under laws such as the U.S. Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act and the UK's Data Protection Act 2018 and UK General Data Protection Regulation (UK GDPR), you do not have to tell your professors the specific cause of your condition or the details of your diagnosis. Your original medical documentation goes only to the DSO, which keeps it confidential. Professors have no right to see it.

  • Diagnoses from another country: many international students arrive with a diagnosis from a hospital back home, only to be told it does not meet the requirements. To avoid spending thousands of dollars on a new local evaluation, pay close attention to the following:

⩥Translation and notarization:

    ∆No DIY translations: universities generally do not accept medical records translated by the student.

   ∆Certified translation: you must provide a copy of the original plus a translation by a qualified translator. We recommend having it translated and notarized by a licensed notary office in China that handles foreign-related matters, or translated abroad by a certified translator (for example, through the American Translators Association or the UK's Institute of Translation and Interpreting).


⩥Check how recent it is:

    ∆Mental health conditions (such as depression and anxiety): because symptoms change over time, universities usually require a diagnosis from within the past 6 to 12 months.

   ∆Learning disabilities or neurodivergent conditions (such as ADHD and dyslexia): universities usually require a comprehensive evaluation report from within the past 3 to 5 years.


⩥Avoiding the risk of reassessment:

    ∆U.S. and UK universities set very high standards for diagnoses of ADHD and other learning disabilities, usually requiring a psycho-educational evaluation report of a dozen or more pages, including intelligence and cognitive testing.

   ∆Recommendation: if your documentation from home is old or thin, we strongly recommend getting a new, up-to-date evaluation report in English that meets U.S. or UK standards from a qualified top-tier (Grade A tertiary) hospital or specialist institution in China before you go abroad. Otherwise, after you arrive you may be asked to be reassessed locally, which takes time (the wait can be months), is expensive, and may not be covered by insurance.


(2) Registration and Intake


a.Submit an application: fill out the application form and upload your medical documentation through the online system on the DSO website.


b.Intake interview: this is a one-on-one meeting with a DSO advisor. The advisor looks at your records but pays more attention to how you describe your situation. Don't just describe your condition; explain in detail how it affects you functionally and what reasonable accommodations you would like. For example, instead of saying only "I have depression," say "Because depression saps my motivation in the mornings, I find it very hard to make 8 a.m. classes. Could I get priority registration for afternoon classes?" or "My medication makes my hands shake and I write very slowly. Could I take exams on a computer?"


c.Finalize the plan: after the interview, the advisor will approve a formal list of accommodations.


(3) Notification and Implementation


a.Get your accommodation letter: the DSO system will generate an official letter listing all the accommodations the university has approved for you (for example, Exam: 50% extended time). It leaves out your specific diagnosis and states only what needs to be done.


b.Send emails and confirm: we recommend emailing to confirm your accommodations in the first week of each semester. Accommodations are generally not retroactive, and if you wait until after final exams to show your accommodation letter, it is usually too late to fix anything. For accommodations that need your professor's cooperation (such as extended exam time), after emailing, it is a good idea to confirm the details with the professor during office hours, in person or over Zoom. For example: Where do I take the exam with extended time? At the university's testing center, or somewhere the professor arranges?

3.2

When Your Rights Are Blocked: Remedies Within the University


When your disability rights are blocked (for example, a professor refuses to follow what your accommodation letter requires, citing academic standards; classmates in a group project sideline a student with a disability because they find the student slow to communicate or resent the need for a sign language interpreter, so the student cannot earn the group grade; or university administrators delay approval and the window for exam adjustments is missed), you can consider the following approaches:



(1) Stage 1: Keep Evidence and Get It in Writing


When a professor turns down your accommodation request verbally, your first step is to confirm it and create a record.


Send an email right after the conversation to turn the verbal refusal into written evidence. If you need to file a complaint later, this will be your key evidence.


Here is a template you can adapt:

Dear Professor [Name],


Thank you for meeting with me earlier today to discuss my DSO-approved accommodations. I truly appreciate the time you took to explain the course structure and your expectations.


I'm writing to ensure I have a clear understanding of our discussion. My takeaway was that you have concerns regarding the implementation of [Specific Accommodation], as you feel it might fundamentally alter the nature of the [e.g., timed assessment / participation].


Could you please confirm if this accurately reflects your view? I would like to consult with my DSO advisor to see if there are alternative tools or creative solutions that could support my medical needs without compromising the essential academic integrity of your course.


I value your expertise and hope we can find a path forward that works for both the department and the learning objectives.


Best regards,

[Your Name]

Note: in some U.S. states, recording a conversation without the other person's consent is illegal. Where possible, rely on email confirmation as your evidence, so that the way you gathered it is not unlawful and does not put you on the back foot.


(2) Stage 2: Bringing in the DSO or DOS


This is the heart of informal resolution. The disability services office (DSO) has administrative authority and a say on compliance. You don't need to argue the law with your professor yourself; let the DSO step in.


U.S. universities also commonly use the Dean of Students Office (DOS), or a student care unit within it (Student Care and Outreach / Case Management), as the first step of intervention and coordination. The name varies from university to university, but the core role is the same: case managers give students nonclinical support by coordinating resources and intervening administratively. Stanford's DOS, for example, expressly lists responsive care and case management services among its core responsibilities.


a. Contact your DSO advisor or DOS case manager


Forward the professor's refusal email with your request, and point out that the professor refused without going through the interactive process and without proposing any alternative.


b. Official intervention: the compliance check


The DSO advisor or DOS case manager will usually contact the professor directly and explain that because the university has already approved the accommodation, a professor who wants to refuse to implement it must give a reason that holds up, such as that the accommodation would fundamentally alter the nature of the course (fundamental alteration). Otherwise, the university faces compliance and litigation risks.


(3) Stage 3: The Tripartite Meeting


If the professor still refuses after the office intervenes, a three-way meeting is usually arranged between the student, the professor, and the DSO advisor (or a DOS representative). For example:

  • Student: describes the difficulty rather than asking for special privileges. For example: "I process auditory information slowly and need recordings to review."

  • Professor: explains the academic concern. For example: "Recordings would leak the proprietary content of our case analyses."

  • DSO / DOS (mediator / decision-maker): looks for a solution both sides can accept. For example: "Professor, would you accept it if the student signs a Lecture Recording Agreement, promising to use the recordings only for personal review, never to share them, and to delete them at the end of the semester?"


(4) Stage 4: The Ombuds (an Alternative Route)


If intervention has not worked, or if you feel the professor is refusing you out of personal bias rather than academic standards, communication has broken down, emotions are running high on both sides, or you are worried the professor will retaliate if you complain and want someone to mediate behind the scenes, you can resort to the university ombuds. As a neutral third party, the ombuds represents neither the university nor the student, only a fair process. Ombuds have no enforcement power and cannot order a professor to change a grade; they can only make recommendations.


Offices such as the DSO are administrative units that act for the university. Your communications with them and the decisions they make are on the record and kept in the university's official files. They have the authority to require professors to provide the reasonable accommodations the law requires, whose bottom line is making sure the university complies with federal law and avoiding litigation. However, talking to an ombuds is informal, confidential, and off the record. Ombuds are independent of the interests of academic departments, have no power to issue orders, and can exert influence only through neutral advice and by pointing out compliance risks.


University ombuds offices are fairly common in the U.S. but are not usually found in UK, where independent review is mainly the job of outside institutions. If you face a similar situation at a UK university, you can first ask your students' union advice service for help. For routes outside the university, see Part 5 of this handbook.

When informal efforts are in vain, or you face serious discrimination or harassment, you can consider starting the university's formal internal complaint procedure.


(1) Complaints and Reports


  • Formal Complaint

    Most powerful. Once you file, the university will open a full investigation, and the outcome is binding. It can provide individual remedies such as a grade change, a retake, or removal of a disciplinary record. Under the principle of due process, the respondent is usually entitled to know who filed the complaint and the contents.

  • Anonymous Reporting

    Less powerful; usually useful only for passing on information. Because the respondent has the right to confront the accuser, an anonymous report usually cannot change your own grades or situation; at most it can prompt the university to make general improvements.

  • Third-Party Reporting

    You can ask a third party, such as the students' union disability officer or a student advocate, to submit the complaint for you. This suits students who find it too difficult to take part in an investigation in person because of psychological trauma.


(2) The Investigation Process


Once you file a formal complaint, the university will begin a quasi-judicial internal administrative investigation.


a. Stage 1: Initial Assessment


The investigator decides whether the allegations fall under the ADA or Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973. If they do, the investigator will open a case and notify the respondent.


For international students, there is also an important protection during the investigation: interim measures.


Investigations usually take 60 to 90 days, and international students can request interim measures during that time. For example, a course you are failing can be marked "Incomplete" rather than "F" until the investigation concludes, so that your visa status is not affected, or you can ask to switch sections to avoid contact with the instructor involved.


b. Stage 2: Fact-Finding


The investigator interviews the student, the professor, and witnesses separately, and may obtain emails, feedback on assignments, and syllabuses. Some universities hold a hearing, where both sides can cross-examine each other through an advisor.


c. Stage 3: Standard of Evidence


University complaint procedures use the preponderance of the evidence standard: if the probability of discrimination or harassment is above 50%, the student's complaint will be upheld.

(1) The U.S.: Medical Reduced Course Load (Medical RCL)


In the U.S., F-1 students who cannot keep up a full course of study because of a temporary physical illness or mental health condition can apply for a Medical Reduced Course Load (RCL). With prior approval from the designated school official (not the same as the disability services office discussed above), a student can take fewer credits and, if necessary, may be allowed to take no credits in a particular term. While the RCL is approved, the student keeps lawful F-1 status, and their SEVIS record usually stays active. An RCL can be used for no more than 12 months in total at each program level (for example, while studying for a master's degree).

Note: On July 17, 2026, the U.S. Department of Homeland Security published a final rule that would end "duration of status" for F-1 students from September 15, 2026, and replace it with a fixed period of admission, shown on Form I-94 as an admit-until date, of no more than four years. Higher education groups and unions then sued, and on September 14, 2026, the U.S. District Court for the District of Massachusetts ordered that the rule not take effect while the case is pending, with nationwide effect, so duration of status still applies for now. That order is preliminary relief during the litigation, not a final judgment. The case is still being heard and the government may appeal, so whether and when the rule takes effect remains uncertain. Keep a close eye on developments. If the rule does take effect, it will have three direct consequences for students who reduce their course load or take a leave of absence because of illness.


First, if medical reasons prevent you from finishing your program within the period of admission on your I-94, an extension approved by your designated school official and an updated SEVIS record will no longer be enough. You will also have to file Form I-539 with USCIS to request an extension of stay before your period of admission ends. A documented illness or medical condition is one of the grounds for an extension that the rule recognizes, but USCIS makes the final decision.


Second, unlawful presence will begin to accrue from the day after the date on your I-94 expires, without any need for a formal finding.


Third, the grace period after you finish your program or practical training will be cut from 60 days to 30 days. Transition rules apply to students admitted in duration of status before September 15, 2026, who are in the U.S. on that date: they may stay until the program end date on their I-20 or the end date of their OPT or STEM OPT employment authorization, whichever is later, but no later than November 14, 2030, and they keep a 60-day grace period. Once such a student leaves the U.S. and reenters, or receives an extension approved by USCIS, the new system applies. If you need to reduce your course load or take a leave of absence because of illness, be sure to check with your university's international student office before you act: confirm the rule's current status, check the date on your I-94, and find out whether you need to file a separate extension request.

Students must give the designated school official medical documentation from one of the following U.S.-licensed professionals: a medical doctor (M.D.), a doctor of osteopathy (D.O.), or a licensed clinical psychologist. Documentation from a counselor, therapist, nurse, or practitioner of traditional Chinese medicine usually cannot by itself meet the documentation requirement for a Medical RCL unless the author also holds one of the licenses the regulation requires.

  • How to apply:

1.Contact your university's international student office.


Before dropping any classes, contact your university's international student office (e.g., ISSS, OISS, or Visa Team) to confirm the university's Medical RCL application form, the required format for medical documentation, and the deadlines.


2.Get medical documentation that meets the requirements.


The documentation should confirm the student's physical or mental health condition and support a recommendation to reduce the course load or temporarily stop taking classes.


3.Submit your application to the designated school official.


Following the university's requirements, submit the Medical RCL application form and current medical documentation.


4.Wait for the designated school official to approve the request and update SEVIS.


Before the student actually reduces their course load, the designated school official must enter information in SEVIS such as the reason for the authorization, the date of the authorization, and the start date of the next term.


5.Drop classes only after approval.


Only after receiving clear approval from the designated school official may the student drop below a full course of study or, where approved, take no credits.


6.Reapply every new term.


Even if the condition continues, the student usually has to submit updated medical documentation, and the designated school official has to reauthorize the RCL each new term, session, or semester.


(2) The UK: Authorised Absence and Interruption of Studies


As licensed student sponsors, UK universities must monitor the attendance and academic engagement of students on Student visas. When a student cannot study normally for health reasons, there are usually two options:


  • Option A: Authorised absence

    • When it applies: a short illness, where the absence will not stop you from completing your course on time or take you beyond the end of your current visa.

    • How to apply: following your university's requirements, submit a fit note, a medical letter, or other medical evidence to your department or the international student office.

    • Effect on your status: if the university approves the absence and continues to sponsor you, your Student visa is usually unaffected and you can stay in the UK.

  • Option B: Interruption of studies

    • When it applies: a more serious illness that requires a longer break from study, such as a term or an academic year. A university may continue to sponsor a student whose studies are suspended for no more than 60 days, provided the student can still complete the course within their current visa after returning. If the break is longer than 60 days, the university should in principle withdraw sponsorship; but in exceptional circumstances such as serious illness or injury, if the student can still complete the course within their current visa, the university may use its discretion to continue sponsoring them.

    • Effect on your status: after withdrawing sponsorship, the university must report it to UKVI. UKVI can cancel the student's remaining permission and will state the date the permission ends in a cancellation notice. Students are not automatically entitled to a 60-day grace period; the date in UKVI's notice is what counts. Students usually have to leave the UK as required. When they have recovered and return to their studies, the university will issue a new CAS, and the student will need to apply for a Student visa again.

 NO.4 

Exercising Your Rights and Responding to Discrimination: Internships and Job Hunting


When you apply for internships or full-time jobs, job seekers and workers with disabilities are also protected by local anti-discrimination laws. This protection usually applies at every stage of employment, from recruitment and hiring to terms and conditions of employment, promotion, and dismissal.


4.1

Disability Rights at Work and the Scope of Legal Protection



(1) The Qualification Principle

Job seekers must have the skills, experience, and qualifications the job requires and be able to perform its essential functions with reasonable accommodations. The law does not require employers to lower their basic qualification standards, but it does prohibit them from setting up barriers unrelated to the job because of disability.


(2) The Reasonable Accommodation Principle

Employers must adjust the recruitment process, work arrangements, or work environment to meet actual needs, unless doing so would cause what the law calls undue hardship.


(3) The Right to Privacy and Nondisclosure

Before making a job offer, employers are strictly prohibited from asking about a job seeker's health or the details of any disability. Job seekers have the right to choose when, where, and to whom they disclose.


>>Click to go back<<



(1) Scope


Employer size: federal anti-discrimination law (such as Title I of the ADA) applies to private employers with 15 or more employees, state and local governments, employment agencies, and labor unions. (Note: most companies able to sponsor H-1B visas meet this threshold.)


Stages covered: the whole process, including recruitment, interviews, medical examinations on hiring, internships, full-time work, and promotion or dismissal. For interns in particular:

  • Paid interns: whether you are on CPT or OPT, if you are paid and work as an employee rather than an independent contractor, you are generally covered by Title I of the ADA.

  • Unpaid interns: if your internship is entirely unpaid and done for academic credit (CPT), you are usually not protected by Title I of the ADA because you receive no real compensation. But be sure to check your state's law. To close this gap in federal law, a growing number of states have passed laws expressly giving unpaid interns the same anti-discrimination rights as regular employees, including the right to request reasonable accommodations. State and local laws against workplace discrimination are often stricter and more specific than federal law.


(2) Protections


First, protection of essential functions: an employer cannot reject you because you cannot perform nonessential or marginal functions. For example, suppose a law firm assistant position requires the ability to "carry 20-pound file boxes." If you use a wheelchair and cannot carry them but can perform the essential functions of legal research and drafting, the employer must excuse you from carrying boxes or have someone else do it, and cannot refuse to hire you on that basis.


Second, the interactive process: an ongoing, two-way discussion between employee and employer. Once you request a reasonable accommodation, the employer is obliged to start this process. Large employers, such as big law firms and leading tech companies, usually won't refuse to buy you a screen reader or other assistive tools on grounds of cost.


Third, the ban on pre-employment medical inquiries: before giving a candidate a conditional job offer, HR is strictly prohibited from asking health-related questions, such as about disability status or how often the candidate has taken sick leave. For example, "Do you have a disability?" and "How many sick days did you take last year?" are both unlawful questions.


>>Click to go back<<



(1) Scope


  • No size limit: unlike U.S. law, the Equality Act 2010 applies to all employers.

  • Who is protected: all employees, workers, contract workers, interns, and job applicants.

  • Definition of disability: you are protected by law as long as a physical or mental impairment has a substantial and long-term (having lasted or expected to last at least 12 months) adverse effect on your ability to carry out day-to-day activities.


(2) Protections


  • Duty to make reasonable adjustments: employers have a duty to make reasonable adjustments so that disabled people are not put at a substantial disadvantage in recruitment or at work, including:

        a. Changing policies or ways of working: such as allowing flexible hours or working from home.

        b. Improving the physical environment: such as installing ramps or improving lighting.

        c. Providing auxiliary aids or services: such as specialist keyboards or sign language interpreters.

  • Access to Work: a UK government employment support program that can pay for specialist equipment, assistive software, support workers, and extra travel costs arising from disability. It does not replace the employer's duty to make reasonable adjustments, and it does not guarantee to cover all costs.

    People who live and work lawfully in England, Scotland, or Wales and are in, or about to start, eligible paid work can apply. People on the Graduate Route or a Skilled Worker visa may be eligible, but this still depends on the nature of the job and their individual circumstances. Ordinary unpaid internships and volunteer work are generally not covered.

4.3

Disclosure: Strategy and Timing


Legally, job seekers and employees have no obligation to tell their employer about a disability on their own initiative. But if your condition could pose a substantial risk to your own or others' safety at work, or you need reasonable accommodations, it is advisable to disclose the necessary information. If you can safely perform the essential functions of the job without any adjustments, you can usually choose not to disclose.

 NO.5 

Exercising Your Rights and Responding to Discrimination: Other Channels


5.1

Administrative Complaints


If your university's or employer's internal complaint process has not solved the problem, or you believe the internal investigation was biased, you have the right to complain to an outside government agency or nongovernmental organization.

  • Key advantages:

  • No cost: most administrative complaints involve no filing fee.

  • Neutrality: investigators are assigned by the government and have no stake in your university or employer.

  • Enforcement power: once a U.S. federal agency finds discrimination, it can require the university to sign a resolution agreement and, if necessary, start proceedings to cut off federal funding. The UK's ombudsman bodies mainly make recommendations; these are not enforceable, but institutions usually comply.



The U.S. administrative complaint system has strong enforcement powers. A university that violates the ADA or Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 is, in effect, taking on federal law.


(1) Department of Education, Office for Civil Rights (OCR)


OCR covers all educational institutions that receive federal funding, which includes almost all public and private universities. It investigates whether a school has violated Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 or Title II of the ADA. You must file your complaint within 180 days of the discrimination. If you first go through the university's internal complaint process, you must file within 60 days after that process ends. OCR will not seek large damages for emotional distress on your behalf, but it will push the university to sign a resolution agreement, for example to change an unreasonable leave policy, require professors to attend training, or reinstate your enrollment.


(2) Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC): Internships and Jobs


The EEOC handles employment discrimination by employers (including universities acting as employers, for example in RA or TA positions). Before you can sue your employer, you must first file a charge of discrimination with the EEOC. The EEOC will investigate or mediate. If mediation fails, the EEOC will issue you a Notice of Right to Sue, and only with this notice can you go to court.


(3) Department of Justice (DOJ)


DOJ mainly handles public universities or state government agencies that violate Title II of the ADA, or cases involving the broad public interest, such as a campus where none of the buildings comply. Cases DOJ takes on often set legal precedent.


>>Click to go back<<



The UK system focuses more on dispute resolution and independent adjudication. A student who disagrees with the outcome of a university's internal investigation can, once the internal procedures have been exhausted, complain to an external independent body, and which body depends on the part of the UK. In England and Wales, it is the Office of the Independent Adjudicator for Higher Education (OIA). In Scotland, it is the Scottish Public Services Ombudsman (SPSO), which is the final stage for complaints about Scottish universities. In Northern Ireland, it is the Northern Ireland Public Services Ombudsman (NIPSO), under the Public Services Ombudsman Act (Northern Ireland) 2016. None of these bodies reassesses academic judgment itself (the grade awarded, for example); instead, they look at whether the institution followed its own regulations and handled the matter fairly. The sections below on higher education complaints use the OIA as the example.


(1) Office of the Independent Adjudicator for Higher Education (OIA): The Final Stage for Student Complaints


The OIA covers higher education providers in England and Wales that are required by law to join its scheme, which includes the great majority of universities. You must first complete all of your university's internal complaint procedures and obtain a Completion of Procedures Letter (COP Letter), then submit your complaint within 12 months of receiving it. The OIA can make recommendations. Although these are formally only recommendations, universities almost always follow them. Common outcomes include having work re-marked, being offered an uncapped resit, and a payment for distress and inconvenience (usually £250 to £5,000).


(2) Acas (Advisory, Conciliation and Arbitration Service): The Required First Step in Workplace Disputes


Acas covers discrimination disputes with employers (including internships). Before you bring a claim in an employment tribunal, the law requires you to contact Acas first for early conciliation. An Acas conciliator passes messages between you and your employer to try to reach a settlement agreement and avoid a court battle. Acas covers England, Scotland, and Wales; in Northern Ireland, the equivalent body is the Labour Relations Agency (LRA), and cases are heard by an industrial tribunal.


(3) Equality and Human Rights Commission (EHRC)


Although it does not directly handle every individual complaint, the EHRC will provide legal funding or intervene directly in cases of major strategic importance (strategic litigation).

5.2

Using Social Media and Public Opinion to Assert Your Rights


This is a high-risk and high-reward route outside the usual channels. We recommend considering it only when all formal channels (the DSO and internal complaints on campus, and OCR or the OIA outside the university) have failed and you have clearly been treated unfairly.



Universities, especially elite private ones, value their reputations. When different offices keep shifting responsibility onto one another, public scrutiny can often break the deadlock. The purpose of applying public pressure is not to vent your frustration but to strengthen your hand in negotiations.



>>Click to go back<<



(1) State Only the Facts


  • Wrong: "This professor is a pervert who discriminates against disabled people. He failed me on purpose!" (Opinions and insults can easily get you sued for defamation.)

  • Right: "Although I had an accommodation letter approved by the DSO, Professor X refused to provide extended exam time on [date Y], saying he would 'not give special treatment.' As a result, I could not complete the exam." (This states objective facts that can be checked.)


(2) Protect Your Anonymity


As an international student, you are advised to post through a confession page or a burner account, and not to post your student ID number or real name unless you are fully prepared to fight under your own name.


(3) Redact Your Evidence


When posting screenshots of emails, black out private information such as the other person's personal phone number or home address to avoid being accused of doxxing. It is generally acceptable to leave in their work email address and job title.

The U.S. and the UK protect free speech to different degrees, and both impose strict limits.



>>Click to go back<<



Besides the law, you are also bound by university rules, and the university may discipline you on the following grounds:


Damaging the university's reputation: this is a broad catch-all provision. Be sure to emphasize that your aim is to push the university to improve, not to destroy it.


Cyberbullying: if your post leads people online to dox the professor, be sure to add a clear note at the end of the post: "Please engage respectfully and do not harass anyone involved."

If you have solid evidence, such as highly discriminatory recordings or emails, the media are a safer channel than social media, because journalists will have the story legally reviewed for you.


(1) Student newspapers


Student newspapers, such as The Crimson at Harvard and Cherwell at Oxford, are a good first stop. Student journalists are students themselves, so they tend to be sympathetic to student concerns, and many have been trained in journalism ethics.


(2) Specialist higher education media


  • U.S.: The Chronicle of Higher Education and Inside Higher Ed.

  • UK: The Tab and Times Higher Education (THE).


(3) How to pass on a tip


Contact journalists from a personal email account such as Proton Mail, not your university email, which the university may be able to access.


Before sharing anything, agree with the journalist which information is on the record (can be published and attributed to you), on background (can be used but not attributed to you by name), and off the record (not for publication).

5.3

Taking Legal Action



Before filing a lawsuit, you need a professional legal assessment. Don't try to take on your university's or employer's legal team alone. Here are the places to look, from lowest to highest cost:


(1) Pro Bono Resources


The wait can be long, but these services are completely free, and the people behind them are highly experienced.

  • Law school clinics:

    Many law schools (such as those at Harvard, Yale, and UCL) run clinics where upper-year law students work under faculty supervision. They are free, and they know very well how the education system works. Avoid your own university's law school (there is usually a conflict of interest) and look for a law school at another university in the same city.

  • Disability rights organizations:

    • U.S.: every state has a federally funded Protection and Advocacy (P&A) system (for example, Disability Rights California), with statutory investigative powers. They are experts in handling ADA cases.

    • UK: the Disability Law Service or Law Centres, which provide free legal advice to people on low incomes.


(2) Contingency Fees and Lawyer Referrals


If pro bono services cannot take your case, you can look for a private lawyer, but keep costs under control. Search the website of your local bar association (such as the New York City Bar Association); many offer 30-minute consultations at low cost (often just $25–$50) for an initial assessment of your chances of winning.


Choosing a fee arrangement:


a. Hourly rate: very expensive; avoid if possible.


b.Contingency fee (no win, no fee): the lawyer covers the costs up front and, if you win, takes 30–40% of the award. Lawyers will take such cases only when there is clear potential for financial compensation.


c.UK only: conditional fee agreements (CFAs). You pay no legal fees if you lose, but you may have to pay the other side's legal costs (you can buy insurance to cover this).


(3) Preparing for the Consultation


To improve your chances of being accepted for a free consultation, prepare the following:


a. Timeline: list all events in reverse chronological order (date – event – evidence). (Example: January 15, 2026 – emailed to request extended time – professor replied refusing – Attachment 1)


b. Key evidence: print the 3–5 most important emails, doctor's letters, and your transcript or dismissal letter. Don't hand the lawyer a pile of disorganized documents.


c. Clear goals: what exactly do you want? (Money? Reinstatement? An apology?)


>>Click to go back<<



Litigation is a last resort. In common law systems such as those of the U.S. and the UK, a lawsuit usually means a complete breakdown of the relationship; it takes a long time, costs a lot, and is public. We recommend it only if you have suffered major financial loss, physical or psychological harm, or serious injustice, and internal and administrative remedies have completely failed.


(1) Pre-Action Stage: Demand Letter (US) / Letter Before Claim (UK)


Your lawyer may send the university or employer a formal letter setting out the facts, the legal basis of your claim, and the specific remedies you are seeking, such as compensation, a remark of your work, or an apology. To avoid expensive litigation and reputational damage, institutions often bring in their legal team at this point and may offer to settle out of court. Before accepting any settlement, check whether it includes a confidentiality clause, as this may prevent you from speaking publicly about your case.


(2) Filing Complaint & Service


If the other side refuses to settle, your lawyer will formally file the court papers (a complaint or claim form). The body that hears a discrimination case and the time limit for bringing it vary by country and by area of discrimination. The U.S. and the UK are shown below as examples:

Note that for continuing acts of discrimination, the limitation period usually runs from the date of the last discriminatory act. In practice, a court may also extend the limitation period because of continuing discrimination or other specific factors, such as a party being a minor. Because calculating time limits is legally complex, the deadline for any particular case should be assessed in detail by a lawyer.


(3) Discovery / Disclosure


Both sides must exchange all relevant evidence. The university can demand all your medical records, private diaries, and even social media messages, and you must be prepared for your privacy to be completely exposed. You also have the right to demand the university's internal emails between professors, such as any emails mocking you. Many universities choose to settle at this stage to keep a scandal from coming out.


(4) Trial and Judgment


Very few cases get this far. A judge or jury makes the final decision.

6.2

Guide to Gathering Evidence


  1. Whether or not you decide to file a formal complaint, getting into the habit of keeping a paper trail is your best protection.

  2. Keep your medical records: keep all original diagnoses and doctors' letters recommending accommodations.

  3. Keep a communication log:

    (1) Email archive: set up a dedicated folder for all emails with the DSO, professors, and advisors, especially emails containing refusals or evasions.

    (2) Put verbal communication in writing: if a professor will not reply in writing and will only meet in person, send a follow-up email immediately after the meeting: "Dear Professor, thank you for meeting with me today. To avoid any misunderstanding, I would like to confirm that we concluded [list what was refused]. Please let me know if I have misunderstood anything."

  4. Record the details of violations: for environmental barriers (such as a broken elevator) or verbal harassment, record the specific time, place, witnesses present, and what was said. If you can, take photos or make recordings, but pay attention to local laws on recording.


6.3

General Advice


  • Safety first: when facing heated conflict or harassment, make sure you are physically safe first, avoid direct confrontation, and file a complaint through the proper channels afterward.

  • Make good use of third parties: when communication with a professor reaches a stalemate, don't try to persuade them on your own. DSO advisors know the systems and procedures; ask them to negotiate for you, which brings the issue back to the level of policy rather than a personal feud.

  • Look after your mental health: standing up for your rights is a long fight that can easily wear you down. Consider seeking counseling at the same time so that the process itself does not cause you further trauma.



As globalization advances, Asian international students studying abroad face not only cross-cultural challenges but possibly also the double test of physical and mental health and institutional barriers. For people with physical and mental disabilities in particular, the stigma that comes from cultural background and the lack of accessibility in institutions often form invisible walls that are harder to overcome than open discrimination.


Disability is not a personal misfortune or defect but part of human diversity. In the U.S., the UK, and other major study destinations, access to reasonable accommodations (known as reasonable adjustments in the UK) and accessibility support is the foundation for success in your studies and career. It is also a right protected by law, not charity or a favor granted by universities or employers. By breaking the silence and practicing self-advocacy, and by making full use of the law and the resources available on and off campus, Asian international students can turn passive endurance into actively standing up for their rights.


We hope this handbook will give Asian international students the support and guidance they need, close the information gap, and help break down stigma, so that no one has to face physical or mental challenges alone. Wherever you are and whatever you face, remember: seeking a level playing field is your right. Each of us can find the courage to speak up for ourselves and for one another. Through every request for reasonable accommodations and every challenge to unfairness, we not only defend our own dignity but also tear down walls for those who come after us, and together build a more inclusive and accessible international community.
