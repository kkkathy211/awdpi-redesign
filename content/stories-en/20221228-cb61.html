Introduction

Introduction


(I) Background and Definition


In all sectors of employment in Indonesia, particularly in the formal sector, women's employment rates are significantly lower than men's. The civil service system promotes gender equality and contains no discrimination. However, women in other jobs still face discrimination in income, benefits, job opportunities, and career advancement. Statistical data show that women earn less than men across all sectors. Recent official unemployment figures continue to show higher numbers of unemployed women. Even when women are employed, they often serve as unpaid workers (71.5%).. In 2005, the male unemployment rate was 9.3%, while the female rate was 14.7%. The situation improved in 2007, with male unemployment at 8.1% and female unemployment at 10.8%. In reality, women actively strive to enter the labor market, but registered job vacancies are predominantly reserved for men (88.67% for men versus 69.38% for women). In 1990, women's income accounted for only 29.2% of total income; in 2002, this figure was 28.3%; and in 2009, it reached 33%. The Millennium Development Goal of achieving gender parity in income (50% each) by the end of 2015 appears to be a distant target, requiring substantial effort to achieve.


Many women work in the informal sector for various reasons, such as lacking the educational qualifications or access to education required for formal sector employment. Additionally, informal sector jobs offer greater flexibility and fewer rigid restrictions, making them more suitable for women still constrained by traditional gender roles. Due to limited domestic employment opportunities, many women become migrant workers in the informal sector. Nevertheless, work in the informal sector carries much higher insecurity, with no guarantee of continuous employment or other protections. Moreover, wages are lower than in the formal sector, and working hours are often longer. Many women in the informal sector work as domestic workers, making them vulnerable to exploitation and violence (UN, 2009).


Image source: ILOSTAT


According to statistics from Indonesia's "National Domestic Workers Network," there are currently 3 million female domestic workers in the Greater Jakarta area, accounting for 25% of the total number of domestic workers nationwide. In the Jakarta region, a domestic worker's monthly salary typically ranges from 1.5 million to 2 million Indonesian rupiah. Almost every middle-class household hires a domestic worker to manage household chores and care for young children, while wealthier families may employ two or three. Leslie Thirtabrata, a woman living in South Jakarta with her husband and two children, hired a temporary domestic worker during the Eid al-Fitr holiday because her three regular domestic workers had returned to their hometowns for the festival. She said that without this temporary worker's help, she would not have known how to get through the holiday. However, this temporary worker hired during Eid cost Leslie four times the usual salary she pays her regular domestic workers (Huaxia, 2014).

 

(II) The Emergence of an International Issue


Due to Indonesia's disadvantaged position in negotiations with destination countries, many current policies fail to effectively protect Indonesian migrant workers. Despite ongoing improvements in policy and management, female-majority Indonesian migrant workers continue to face exploitation and violence, and their human rights are violated both within Indonesia, during transit, while working in destination countries, and upon returning to Indonesia. In 2004, Malaysia carried out a large-scale deportation of undocumented migrant workers. The Coordinating Minister for People's Welfare reported that as many as 261,789 Indonesian migrant workers were deported in 2004. Unfortunately, although it is widely known that most of those deported were women, there is no exact data disaggregated by gender. Without strategic, comprehensive preventive measures, legal protection for migrant workers who suffer exploitation, rights violations, and violence remains minimal.

 

(III) Current Situation


Efforts to advance women's status in the formal sector follow Law No. 13/2003 on Labor (Right to Work). The Indonesian government, based on International Labour Organization Conventions No. 100 and No. 111, has published a reference manual titled "Equal Employment Opportunity," which has been disseminated to companies and employers. In protecting women's reproductive rights, the Ministry for Women's Empowerment, the Ministry of Health, and the Ministry of Labor have jointly issued regulations on providing breastfeeding facilities in workplaces (Regulations No. 48/Men.PP./XII/2008, No. Per.27/MEN/XII/2008, and No. 117/Menkes/PB/2008), ministerial regulations on the certification of reception facilities for Indonesian migrant workers, and regulations on maternity leave. Monitoring of night shifts is being promoted to ensure the protection of women working at night. Other activities include raising awareness among companies and workers on key issues such as workers' rights and responsibilities, risks of sexual harassment in the workplace, and discrimination in wages and benefits.


Law No. 23/2004 on the Elimination of Domestic Violence provides protection for domestic workers, but compared to other family members, domestic workers have a much weaker bargaining position and remain highly vulnerable. When wages are unpaid and other rights are unsecured, domestic workers are susceptible to psychological, physical, sexual, and economic violence. A common practice is that domestic workers' rooms cannot be locked, and they are not allowed to leave the house, as the employer holds the keys. They are also threatened not to report incidents to the police. Domestic workers are often unfamiliar with urban environments and find it difficult to seek help from outside their employer's household. Isolated living conditions combined with inhumane working conditions cause many domestic workers to live under extreme stress.


Image source: rawpixel


Due to Indonesia's large population and vast territory divided by sea, the country faces highly complex challenges. The number of job seekers each year is enormous—approximately 500,000 (Indonesian Central Bureau of Statistics, 2008), though the actual number is likely even higher. Therefore, it is projected that the economy must grow at a rate of at least 5% annually to absorb this volume of job seekers. The formal sector still faces significant problems, and the informal sector is even worse. In terms of resources and understanding of the issue, the formulation, monitoring, and enforcement of regulations remain weak. Human rights violations such as denial of maternity leave and wages below standard levels are not penalized. The new phenomenon of "outsourcing" has not brought security or benefits, and sometimes results in even lower incomes. Most companies use contract labor mechanisms to cope with adverse economic conditions, but this makes workers more vulnerable. It is difficult for workers to become permanent employees, as companies hire based on fluctuating needs. To extend temporary employment, job descriptions are constantly changed—a practice that is actually illegal. For example, women working in palm oil plantations reportedly earn very low wages, perform physically demanding labor, and face a high risk of sexual harassment in their work environment.


In the informal sector, domestic workers also lack legal protection, despite ongoing efforts in this area. Although sexual harassment occurs frequently, it is difficult to combat sexual harassment in the workplace because the law does not provide protection for addressing this issue. Measures intended to protect women are sometimes viewed as restrictions on women's freedom of movement and fundamental freedoms—for example, certain regional regulations prohibit women from going out at night or require them to wear "Muslim" attire. Family problems and the rising trend of divorce resulting from spousal separation are another issue affecting migrant workers. To address this, the Ministry of Religious Affairs and local religious affairs offices have established marriage counseling institutions empowered to advise migrant workers and their families. Other ongoing improvements include better services for distressed migrant workers, especially health services, strengthened control over population movement in border areas, and empowerment of potential migrant workers at the source, including providing jobs through regional development programs as an alternative to overseas employment. The education of migrant workers' children, especially those without household registration, is often neglected. To address this issue, the National Ministry of Education, in cooperation with other ministries, has established services for the children of Indonesian migrant workers working in Malaysia and living in border areas.

Life: Indonesian Women's Firsthand Accounts

Life: Indonesian Women's Account


🔹 Sukma: I have to wake up at 3:30 a.m. If I wake up at 4:30 a.m., I get scolded: "Sleeping so late? Do you think this is your own home?"


Three years ago, an Indonesian girl named Sukma was deceived by an illegal agency into working as a domestic worker in Malaysia. Fearing she might run away, her employer confiscated her phone. For two years and seven months, Sukma had no way to contact her family back in Indonesia. It was a painful memory: Sukma woke up at 3:30 every morning to prepare breakfast for the employer's family, clean the house, and care for the elderly and children, not finishing all her work until 10 p.m. Since she lived in the employer's home, Sukma was constantly under surveillance, with almost no privacy. A Muslim, Sukma would change into a full-body prayer garment during her rest time to perform prayers, but this clothing frightened the employer's children, so the employer banned her from praying. As a "foreigner" living abroad, harsh employers, heavy workloads, and unfamiliar cultures left Sukma feeling deeply lonely and sad, yet she could neither contact her family nor seek solace in her faith.


Although the work was exhausting and emotionally draining, domestic workers could not show too much negativity. In addition to completing tedious household chores and caregiving duties, they were also expected to perform extensive emotional labor. During training at an Indonesian domestic worker school, instructors repeatedly warned trainees: even if you miss home or feel overwhelmed by the work, never show sadness or distress in front of your employer. This would provoke suspicion, and suspicion would erode trust. Because they lived under the same roof, the work and personal lives of employer and worker were deeply intertwined, with no clear boundaries. A worker's negative emotions could affect the employer's household, making them feel their home was no longer comfortable or free, and strained relationships or resentment could put the worker at risk of losing their job.


Image source: pixabay


At the same time, the crisis of trust caused by suspicion places foreign domestic workers in a vulnerable position. If an employer wishes to terminate a domestic worker, they may forcibly end the employment by paying one month's salary in lieu of notice. Some employers impose unreasonable work demands to force the worker to resign voluntarily. In an article by an Indonesian employer (Wu Xin, 2021), the author wrote: "When friends or agencies learned we were going to terminate Nina, they specifically warned us not to tell her in advance. They said many workers, once they know they're being let go, stop working well during their final month. If you pay the notice compensation, you can require the worker to leave the house the same day." It is difficult to simply judge the employer's actions, but imagine how helpless and disoriented a foreign worker—whose food, shelter, and daily life all depend on the employer's home—would feel being suddenly cast out in an unfamiliar city with no support. Not to mention, unemployed domestic workers who fail to find a new employer within three months face deportation (regulations vary by region; this example refers to Hong Kong law). Even in cases where the law prohibits employers from dismissing domestic workers—such as during pregnancy—employers can still find numerous online methods to pressure workers into "voluntary" resignation. For example, buying rice, going shopping, hosting parties at home, changing the worker's rest schedule, making her rest in areas without phone access, or restricting her leisure activities. Employers can legally create deliberately uncomfortable working environments and impose excessive workloads to force domestic workers to quit.


Illegal agencies, harsh employers, mutual distrust between people, and legal gray zones with little protection—all are risks that foreign domestic workers must face.


🔹 Muji: I'm working abroad, but my husband is cheating on me.


Foreign domestic workers not only need to carefully manage their relationships with employers and employer families, but also face great difficulty maintaining intimacy with their husbands, children, or boyfriends back in Indonesia.


Muji is an Indonesian domestic worker employed in Taiwan, caring for a grandmother suffering from dementia and Parkinson's disease. Every morning, Muji helps the grandmother move her limbs in bed, changes her diaper, helps her get up, transfers her to a wheelchair, and feeds her. Since the grandmother eats very slowly, Muji often spends two or three hours helping her finish a meal. Because the care recipient is completely unable to care for herself, Muji is essentially working non-stop, except when sleeping. Before working in Taiwan, Muji had also worked as a domestic worker in Malaysia. Having left her homeland multiple times to do demanding labor, Muji's entire purpose was to provide a better life for her family in Indonesia. Yet, while Muji was striving to create better living conditions for her children and family, her husband back in Indonesia was using the money she had earned through hardship to "get involved with another woman."


Indonesia is a country with traditional gender norms. When a wife works abroad to support the family, her husband's infidelity is often socially excused—or even justified. While wives and girlfriends work hard in a foreign land, cutting back on food and clothing to save money, they are still expected to put in 100% of their effort to maintain long-distance love and family bonds; otherwise, they may be blamed—by others, or even their own families—for not trying hard enough. In the documentary *Guarding from Afar*, a male agency staff member tells a group of women who have just completed training and are about to leave for overseas work: "I feel sorry for your husbands. You must understand, this is difficult for them." Everyone sees the "hardship" of the left-behind husbands—without a wife to manage the household or care for the elderly and children—but no one speaks of the hardship faced by women who carry such heavy burdens. It could be said that, to some extent, women's increased economic contribution has not significantly improved their status in gender relations.


For foreign domestic workers, maintaining long-distance relationships is difficult; building stable intimate relationships with men in the host city is even harder. Teachers at domestic worker training schools repeatedly warn their students before graduation: "Don't be fooled by men's sweet talk. Men at factories will whistle at you, but what they really want is your money." Tari, an Indonesian domestic worker employed at a nursing home in Taiwan, also says: "Indonesian men in Taiwan are all scoundrels. They claim to be single, but actually have wives back in Indonesia. After sending their salaries home, they look for side income by targeting us." Female migrant workers, far from home, struggle to receive emotional support from their husbands or boyfriends in Indonesia, yet are easily drawn into the deceptive charm of other men. How can foreign domestic workers, already vulnerable in both labor and intimate relationships, establish a stable life abroad?


🔹 Tari: I want to bring my diploma back and prove to my father that I've done it.


Beyond earning money to support their families, many Indonesian domestic workers choose to work overseas and endure years of homesickness because they see greater possibilities in their host cities. They hope to earn their first fortune through domestic work, using years of hard labor to gain both savings and professional skills, compensating for the resources they lack at home.


Tari is an Indonesian domestic worker employed at a nursing home in Tainan. She was born into a poor family and did not receive a good education in her youth. Therefore, she sees Taiwan not just as a workplace, but as a place to learn—such as how to professionally care for the elderly—and she is also studying in the Department of Communication at a community college. "We are ordinary people. We can earn respect through knowledge, not wealth. Through education, we can become useful individuals."


Image source: pixabay


Tari uses her work and vacation time to study hard and enrich herself. In Taiwan, she has gained opportunities she once missed and that were scarce in her youth. She sees Taiwan as a platform for growth and a blessed land where dreams come true. For Indonesian women, being born into poverty often means limited access to quality education, and staying in their hometowns usually means early marriage. Although working overseas is undoubtedly difficult, it also offers them opportunities for independence and self-fulfillment.


Hayaria, an Indonesian domestic worker who graduated from high school, has worked abroad for ten years. She used her earnings to build a house for her family and buy motorcycles and a car. She is very proud of this—helping her family escape poverty proves her ability and worth. Hard work has not only brought wealth to improve her family's living conditions, but also earned her self-recognition and affirmation.

Government and agencies: help or exploitation?

Government and Intermediaries: Help or Exploit?


Aside from the Philippines, Indonesia is currently the country sending the largest number of female domestic workers overseas (Lan Pei-chia, 2011). Most work in countries such as Singapore, China, and Malaysia, providing domestic services for employers. The economic disparity between Indonesia and these countries is the main reason driving the flow of female domestic workers into overseas markets. However, the lack of legal protections for domestic workers in Indonesia means many female workers—constrained by education, language, employment conditions, and agency fees—must rely heavily on agencies.


(1) The dual nature of the Indonesian government: dependence on overseas domestic workers and undervaluation of domestic labor


From the late 20th century to the early 21st century, many Asian economies experienced sustained growth. This not only expanded the size of the middle class but also provided local women with more and better employment opportunities in state-owned or private enterprises—meaning the number of local women working in domestic service began to decline (Chin, 1997).


At the same time, the 1997 Asian financial crisis hit Indonesia especially hard. To relieve financial pressure, many Indonesians began seeking overseas employment opportunities. There are approximately 4,500,000 Indonesian workers employed overseas, the majority of whom work in domestic services (Britton, 2018); among them, 84% are women and 16% are children (Putri, 2021).


Despite the large number of Indonesian domestic workers abroad, the Indonesian government has not established corresponding laws to protect their rights and interests. In fact, as early as 2004, Indonesia's legislative body began drafting a domestic worker protection bill known as "RUU PRT," but this bill has yet to be passed (Britton, 2018). Moreover, Indonesian society itself does not value domestic labor, and domestic workers are rarely seen as workers (pekerja); in most cases, they are simply referred to as helpers (pembantu) (ILO, 2006).


Image source: rawpixel


Regardless of whether their rights are protected, since the 1980s, overseas domestic workers have become an important force in Indonesia's national development. The impact of the 1997 Asian financial crisis mentioned earlier, along with the resignation of Indonesia's second president, Suharto, in 1998, led the Indonesian government to dismantle monopolies and deregulate the labor recruitment market, resulting in a sharp increase in the number of Indonesian recruitment agencies (Lindquist, 2010). Furthermore, the Indonesian government issues special tourist visas for domestic workers, such as the Sijil Perjalanan Laksana Paspot—a visa valid only for travel between Indonesia and Malaysia (Chin, 1997).


Although they are an important contributor to economic development, the Indonesian government itself does not strongly support the large-scale deployment of women to work in domestic services overseas. In 2015, Indonesian President Joko Widodo stated that he wanted to stop sending female domestic workers abroad because the nation "must have pride and dignity" (Nazeer, 2015).


(2) The Dual Role of Recruitment Agencies: Both Assistance and Exploitation


The absence of legal protections for domestic workers means that the rights of Indonesian overseas workers can only be safeguarded by the laws of their host countries. Formal recruitment agencies play a relatively important role in this process.


Taking Hong Kong, China as an example—by the end of September 2020, there were approximately 155,000 Indonesian domestic workers employed in Hong Kong (HLC). A recruitment agency established in 1991 primarily assists workers in finding employers, provides pre-employment training and handles visa procedures; offers consultation services to clients seeking domestic helpers and helps them find suitable candidates; mediates minor labor disputes between domestic workers and employers; and assists workers with various administrative procedures such as obtaining a Hong Kong ID card (HLC). Of course, the employer pays all costs associated with the agency's services, including visas, passports, insurance, training, and round-trip airfare for the worker.


Meanwhile, on the website of this Hong Kong-based agency, it states "Both employer and worker must sign a contract with reference number I.D.407", which specifies the scope of work, salary, working hours, rest periods, and holidays for female domestic workers (HLC).


Image source: pixabay


On another website in Singapore called "Helper Choice," the required training content for Indonesian domestic workers is listed. While still in Indonesia, they must learn how to clean, cook various dishes, and care for children or the elderly. After completing domestic service training, they must pass a competency assessment before being allowed to work overseas. Before officially starting work, they must also complete 600 hours of vocational and educational training, mainly focused on introducing them to the culture of the host country and basic language communication. Most importantly, they need to understand their duties and related rights as domestic workers (Helper Choice).


Correspondingly, agencies charge female workers a high recruitment fee—even though the average monthly salary for Indonesian female domestic workers in Singapore is 597 USD (Helper Choice) (approximately 3,800 RMB)—a fee that may take workers several years to repay. For example, a recruitment agency in Suzhou, China, earned over 10 million RMB in illegal income in less than a year, from May 2015 to March 2016, by placing more than 200 foreign domestic workers (Zuo, 2017).


By 2020, due to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, many female domestic helpers returned to Indonesia, while various countries tightened their entry policies for foreign laborers. Taking Singapore as an example, a female domestic worker seeking entry must not only have a visa but also approval from Singapore's Ministry of Manpower and a valid pre-departure COVID-19 test result, and must undergo a period of observation at a designated location. Starting January 1, 2021, employers are also required to purchase insurance for female domestic workers before their arrival in Singapore, covering medical expenses related to COVID-19 (Ho, 2021).

The Future: Where Do We Go From Here?

The future: Where is the way?


For female domestic workers from Indonesia, their lives are filled with contradictory uncertainties. Indonesian society does not recognize the value of domestic labor, yet their work overseas is a major source of foreign exchange income for Indonesia (Li Guozhang, 2015); recruitment agencies help them find jobs but charge exorbitant fees that take years to repay. Not to mention, while working in employers' homes, they are more vulnerable to disrespect and even abuse (Britton, 2018).


Exploitation is widespread, and within the informal sector, international female migrants often become targets of manipulation (UN, 2009); migrant women often do not know their rights or the obligations of their sponsors, whether they migrate as family members or through labor channels. How should we improve the living and working conditions of Indonesian female domestic workers, and even more broadly, how can we change the societal—beyond just Indonesia—disregard for the value of domestic labor? These are questions we must reflect upon.

▶ International Perspectives: The Case of Hong Kong


People have begun to pay attention to the treatment of 325,000 domestic workers in Hong Kong, who mainly come from Indonesia and the Philippines. Media cited Hong Kong Police Commissioner Tsang Wai-hung as saying that abuse of domestic workers is rare. However, activists, charities, and academics oppose this view, stating that Indonesian maids are particularly vulnerable. Amnesty International published a report in November stating that thousands of Indonesian women are smuggled into Hong Kong and face "slavery-like" living conditions, and that neither the Jakarta nor Hong Kong governments adequately protect them from abuse and exploitation.

The charity "Pastoral Care Centre for Migrant Workers" surveyed over 3,000 domestic workers in April 2013, finding that 58% reported verbal abuse, 18% physical abuse, and 6% sexual assault.


Professor Hans Ladegaard of Hong Kong Baptist University has studied abuse of domestic workers since 2008 and says the situation is serious and widespread. He believes Indonesian workers face higher risks than Filipino workers because they are younger, less educated, and have weaker English skills. He says some families prefer hiring Indonesian workers because they are more willing to accept lower wages and are described by agencies as more "obedient (compliant)." He adds: "That's what agencies tell employers. I think this amounts to condoning their exploitation and abuse." Unlike Filipinos and other international workers, Indonesians can only work abroad through government-registered employment agencies. Amnesty International says: "They fear contract termination, which would prevent them from finding new jobs and force them to pay recruitment fees a second time, thus compelling many Indonesian domestic workers to endure continued abuse and exploitation." Ladegaard says it is uncommon for abused domestic workers to take legal action against their employers. He explains: "In Hong Kong, yes, there are laws to protect them if abuse can be proven, so Hong Kong is ahead of many other Asian and Middle Eastern countries in this regard. However, the problem is that most cases are never reported." Based on his experience, most abused workers are unwilling to file complaints because legal proceedings often take over a year, during which time they cannot work.


Hong Kong Labour Department Director Tang Chi-keung said he disagrees with the assessment that abuse of maids is widespread, stating that most employers treat their employees well. The Labour Department was shocked when it learned of the case of Erwiana. Erwiana left Hong Kong in January without reporting to the police. She only shared her experience after arriving back in Indonesia.


In light of this case, the Labour Department launched an awareness campaign this week at gathering spots for Indonesian domestic workers, informing them of their rights.


Tang Chi-keung also said: "We also talk to employers, making them aware of the importance of treating domestic helpers well, otherwise there will be serious criminal consequences." However, he said the government has no plans to change the rule requiring all foreign domestic workers to live with their employers.


Activists say the cohabitation rule increases the risk of physical and sexual assault and may lead to workers being forced to work non-stop.

 


▶ On Thursday, November 21, the Hong Kong Labour Department issued a statement responding to a critical report previously released by Amnesty International.


The Labour Department's statement said the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region government is committed to protecting the rights of about 150,000 Indonesian domestic workers in Hong Kong, "In fact, Hong Kong is one of the few places in the world where foreign domestic workers enjoy the same legal protections as local workers."

The statement also noted that foreign domestic workers are further protected by employment contracts, entitling them to minimum wage, free food and accommodation, reasonable privacy, free medical care, and free round-trip travel to their home countries.


A report by Amnesty International criticized the exploitation of Indonesian maids in Hong Kong and accused the Hong Kong government of not doing enough.


The report states that Indonesian domestic workers are exploited by agencies during the recruitment process, which often involves confiscating their documents and charging high fees, while falsely promising them high salaries and good working conditions.


The report also alleges that this process often turns into human trafficking and forced labour, as these domestic workers, deprived of their documents and burdened with debt, are unable to escape their exploitative situations.


The report criticizes the inaction of the Indonesian and Hong Kong authorities as "inexcusable."


A statement from Hong Kong's Labour Department said that abuse of foreign domestic workers, including underpayment and denial of rest days, will not be tolerated. "Any act of abuse will be prosecuted once sufficient evidence is obtained."


In September this year, prior to the public release of the report, a Hong Kong couple was convicted on eight charges for abusing an Indonesian domestic worker, including burning her with an iron and beating her with a bicycle chain, and was sentenced to 3 years and 3 months, and 5 years and 6 months in prison respectively.


There are currently over 300,000 domestic workers in Hong Kong, the majority of whom come from Indonesia, the Philippines, and other South Asian countries.

▶ Indonesia delays implementation of a new policy that could make it more costly for employers here to hire its citizens as foreign domestic workers


Under the placement fee exemption policy, Indonesian migrant workers in 10 job sectors, including domestic workers, cleaners, and farm workers, will no longer have to pay their own placement and training fees. Tatang Razak, Secretary-General of Indonesia's Migrant Workers Protection Agency (BP2MI), told CNA that the implementation has now been postponed to mid-July due to "several factors."


"After observing and seeing the objective conditions on the ground, particularly the training infrastructure for (migrant workers) in regional areas (within Indonesia), it appears that many are not yet ready," said Mr. Razak. "If some regions are still unprepared, we need to devise a mechanism. Even if placement fees, such as training fees, remain, we could perhaps provide commercial loans to people at low interest rates."

 

Mr. Matthew Lee, manager of Raymond Maids, an agency specializing in Indonesian and Filipino domestic workers, said potential employers are concerned about having to pay higher costs. "I tell them—let's wait and see, because regulations in Indonesia seem to change quickly," said Mr. Lee. He noted that demand for Indonesian workers dropped temporarily due to the delay but has now rebounded. Mr. Lee added, "We cannot control what the Indonesian government wants or plans to do. We hope they can reconsider this policy and find a win-win, feasible solution."
