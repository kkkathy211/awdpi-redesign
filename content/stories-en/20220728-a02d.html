Sofie (Host): Let's first invite Annie to give us a scientific overview of the forms of domestic violence and its impacts on the individual psychologically.


Annie: I'll divide my presentation into two parts:


The first part is about how to identify domestic violence and its forms.



Domestic violence, also known as intimate partner violence, is an imbalance of power and control, where the abuser often uses intimidating and harmful language or behaviors to control their partner. Domestic violence can take many forms and is generally divided into these five categories. Psychological and emotional abuse, sexual abuse, physical abuse, economic control, and threat-based abuse. Threat-based abuse refers to when the abuser says, "If you don't do as I say, I will threaten you by doing X." This is also a form of domestic violence.


Domestic violence can occur in both heterosexual and same-sex relationships, and both men and women can be victims. However, since the purpose of this event and our organizations is to protect more Chinese women, and multiple statistical data show that domestic violence more frequently targets women, the content today will mainly focus on issues related to women.


Let's first become familiar with how to identify domestic violence. Based on my personal experience, many women may not be familiar with certain characteristics of domestic violence—for example, they may not realize that economic control or behavioral control also constitute abuse, and they might believe that only extreme violent acts qualify as domestic violence.


Often, abuse begins subtly and gradually escalates over time, so it may not be easy to identify in the early stages. Here, I've compiled some signs to help identify domestic violence: obvious verbal attacks; attempts to control whom you see, what you do, how you spend money, or your daily behaviors; expressions of extreme jealousy or possessiveness; constant accusations of disloyalty in the relationship; becoming irritable or angry after drinking or using drugs; threatening you with violence or weapons; or even carrying out violent acts against you, your children, or pets; forcing sexual acts against your will; or blaming all violent behaviors on you (saying that because of what you did or said, this is what you deserve). If you are experiencing any of these in a relationship, you may be experiencing domestic violence.


How to identify domestic violence:

1. Cursing, insulting, or demeaning you

2. Interfering with or preventing you from working or studying, or from seeing family or friends

3. Trying to control how you spend money, where you go, what medication you take, or what clothes you wear

4. Showing extreme jealousy or possessiveness, or constantly accusing you of disloyalty

5. Becoming angry after drinking or using drugs

6. Threatening you with violence or weapons

7. Hitting, kicking, pushing, slapping, choking, or otherwise harming you, your children, or pets

8. Forcing you into sexual acts or engaging in sex against your will

9. Blaming their violent behavior on you or telling you that you deserve it


A more specific situation is if you are in a same-sex relationship, or if you are bisexual or transgender and you may be experiencing domestic violence—if your partner threatens to disclose your sexual orientation or gender identity to your friends, family, colleagues, or others close to you, tells you that because of your sexual orientation or gender identity you won't be able to receive help from official authorities, or uses your sexual orientation or gender identity to justify their abusive behavior, these are also forms of domestic violence.


Additionally, since I'm not certain about the age range of our audience and previously expected a more diverse group of participants, I'd like to briefly discuss an important issue: the relationship between domestic violence, pregnancy, and children. Sometimes domestic violence begins or increases in frequency during pregnancy, and it can put both your health and your baby's health at risk. Even if children are not directly abused, merely witnessing the abuse can harm them—children raised in abusive households are more likely to experience abuse themselves or develop behavioral problems. As adults, they are more likely to become abusers or to view abuse as a normal part of relationships.


You may worry that speaking the truth will further endanger you, your children, or other family members, and could break up your family, but seeking help is the best way to protect yourself and your loved ones.


The next part I'd like to discuss is the psychological impact of domestic violence on victims.


1

First, mental health issues. According to U.S. research data, among women seeking care at mental health institutions, on average more than half have experienced or are currently experiencing intimate partner abuse. Common psychiatric diagnoses include PTSD (post-traumatic stress disorder), depression, or anxiety disorders. Additionally, traumatic events can cause profound and lasting changes in physiological, emotional, and cognitive memory functions. These changes may not necessarily lead directly to a mental illness diagnosis, but they can be a risk factor. A person's mental health can be affected even without the above-mentioned disorders. Whether someone develops PTSD due to domestic violence depends on many factors, and not everyone is affected in the same way. For example, someone might have sleep problems, physical pain (such as headaches), or reduced work performance. Psychological impacts on victims are common and may include difficulty caring for others, difficulty forming and maintaining healthy relationships, and difficulty adapting to change or coping with adversity.

2

Second, mental health coercion. This is a psychological term referring to an abusive strategy that specifically targets a partner's mental health, part of a broader pattern of abuse and control. It often involves using violence, force, threats, or manipulation to carry out mental health coercion, including deliberately attempting to undermine the victim's sanity, preventing the victim from receiving treatment, controlling their medication, using the victim's mental health status to discredit their sources of protection and support (such as family and friends), and then using their mental state to manipulate police or influence child custody decisions. I'd like to clarify this point—for example, the abuser might tell others that the victim has mental health issues. This claim could influence police judgment or child custody decisions. Additionally, they may exploit the stigma around mental health to make the victim believe that no one will believe them.

3

Third, gaslighting. It is a form of manipulation frequently occurring in abusive relationships and a type of covert emotional abuse. The second point may resemble behavioral or physical control, whereas this emotional abuse involves the abuser misleading the victim with false narratives, making them question their perception of reality. It includes a series of strategies such as lying, diverting attention, and minimizing (minimizing means downplaying their own actions, then denying or blaming the other person). Under this effect, people may begin to doubt themselves—questioning their memory, doubting recent events and their own cognition, wondering if they are truly at fault—until eventually, victims become uncertain about their own cognition and worldview, even questioning whether they are losing their mind. This is a form of psychological manipulation, and some abusers not only engage in overtly controlling behaviors but also exert psychological influence on their victims.

Sofie (Host): Thank you, Annie. Annie's sharing has offered insights into the individuals involved in domestic violence from a personal psychological perspective. When we expand our view to the level of social psychology, we may gain even more diverse interpretations. Next, we invite Li Zi, a researcher in social and cross-cultural psychology, to share with us how we should understand domestic violence from a social psychological perspective.


Li Zi: Annie just mentioned that domestic violence includes physical violence, sexual violence, psychological violence, and economic control. According to data from France and Belgium, nearly 200 people die each year in France due to domestic violence, including 3/4 homicides and 1/4 suicides. In Belgium, one in every eight couples experiences psychological or mental health issues due to domestic violence. The causes of domestic violence are extremely complex, involving a series of factors including individual, social, cultural, and economic aspects. Therefore, it is almost impossible to predict with 100% certainty whether someone will become a perpetrator of domestic violence—or never will.

To better understand what follows, let me briefly explain the definition of social psychology. Social psychology is a scientific discipline that studies the psychological behaviors and developmental patterns of individuals and groups. It is an interdisciplinary field situated between psychology and sociology, filling gaps between the two to some extent. Research areas in social psychology include psychological and behavioral aspects related to individuals, such as self-awareness, attitude change, and emotions, as well as interpersonal interactions, such as helping behavior, interpersonal attraction, and relationships. Additionally, it examines individual and group psychology from the perspectives of groups, macro culture, and social environments, such as racism and group dynamics.


I want to emphasize the difference between violence and conflict. Conflict between partners, for example, occurs when differing opinions arise and, during negotiation, both parties remain adjustable and respectful—meaning the issue itself is resolvable. In contrast, intimate partner violence reflects a vertical power relationship. For the abuser, differing opinions are perceived as a challenge to their authority. Typically, the abuser lacks empathy, negotiation skills, coordination abilities, and communication skills. Of course, this lack of communication skills refers specifically within the relationship; externally, they may appear exceptionally patient and skilled in communication.


Today, I'd like to introduce a concept called the Cycle of Violence Theory. It is a behavioral pattern identified by a psychologist in the late 1970s through interviews with 1,500 women experiencing domestic violence. This model consists of four stages:


01

The first stage is the accumulation of tension. During this stage, the victim often feels fear, communication becomes very strained or nearly nonexistent, and both parties internally accumulate emotional stress. This eventually leads to the second stage.

02

The second stage is the eruption of previously accumulated tensions. In this stage, both parties experience anger, and behaviors such as threats occur—this is domestic violence happening in real time.

03

Then comes the third stage, a reconciliation phase. Usually after the violence, the abuser will try every means—such as apologizing or giving gifts—to seek the victim's forgiveness, or may make the victim believe it was their own fault, leading into the fourth stage.

04

The fourth stage can also be called the honeymoon phase, a period where the violent behavior appears to be forgotten and calm seems to return. After this apparent calm, the cycle returns to a new phase of accumulating tension, ultimately perpetuating the cycle of violence.


According to statistical data, about 25% of the 270 million households worldwide experience domestic violence, with 90% of victims being women. On average, victims endure 35 incidents of abuse before choosing to report it, and the longest recorded duration of abuse for a victim is 40 years. People may ask, why don't victims leave? Why do they wait until so many incidents of abuse before reporting or taking remedial action?

First, the abuser repeatedly apologizes and repeatedly demonstrates sincerity. Or, the abuser may genuinely believe during the apology that they will never commit violence again. There are two possible scenarios here: one is that they truly believe it at that moment but fail to follow through, thus failing to break the cycle of violence. The second scenario is that these are simply lies fabricated by the abuser.


Second, not leaving may actually be a form of self-protection for the victim. On one hand, they may feel "family shame should not be made public," because their family dynamic differs greatly from the idealized family model held by society. If they were to say, "I am being abused; my husband is violent," they would worry about how others would perceive them.


Another reason is that the risks of leaving may be too high. These risks include various aspects, such as economic risk. If they leave the abuser, they may not be able to support themselves financially. There is also the possibility that custody of children will be awarded to the abuser.


Additionally, the victim may attribute the cause of the violence to themselves. They believe it is their fault, and that if they correct this fault, they will no longer be beaten.


Also, there is the investment and commitment they have made in the relationship. We will discuss this point next.


Another factor is the normalization of violence due to social and cultural influences. If you are in an environment with high tolerance for violence, people may think, "If someone is disobedient, just beat them." Women experiencing domestic violence may also think, "Everyone does it; every family is like this." So why should we even talk about it?


When thinking about why victims don't leave, I recalled a concept from a class I once took, called the Investment Model of Close Relationships. This model was not originally designed for domestic violence, but I find it interesting and would like to share it. I'm translating directly from French into English, so some terms will be in English. Simply put, commitment can be understood as loyalty or level of commitment, and is composed of three main factors: First, the satisfaction both parties feel in the relationship; second, the degree to which one desires to break up or leave; and third, the level of investment in the relationship.



In this intimate relationship, if one's gains outweigh their costs, their satisfaction with the relationship increases. The alternative option can be understood as a "backup" partner—if one has a very supportive backup partner, their satisfaction with the current relationship decreases. The amount of resources and time required to leave affects the victim's level of investment in the relationship. The greater the investment, the higher their loyalty to the relationship. This investment includes both tangible and intangible aspects, such as money and time. The internal aspect refers to the individual's direct investment in the relationship, such as time and money spent maintaining it; the external aspect refers to what the relationship brings, such as having a child together and the investment made for that child.


There are also mechanisms that maintain relationships, such as accommodation, willingness to sacrifice, forgiveness, and positive illusions. Positive illusions include believing the partner is perfect and that the two will have a wonderful future together. Finally, there is the deliberate devaluation of alternatives—actively looking down on the backup partner.

In fact, this is a general model of investment in close relationships. But personally, I believe it can be applied to cases of domestic violence. For example, the more a victim invests in the relationship, the more likely they are to stay in it. The satisfaction level and desire to leave may be very low. But if the level of investment exceeds the sum of these two factors, the victim will still remain. This is why victims usually do not easily leave their abusers.


I'd also like to talk about the impact of domestic violence on children. The Bobo doll experiment, a particularly famous experiment in social psychology, is based on social learning theory and the views of Bandura (Albert Bandura, American psychologist), who argued that children can learn and imitate behaviors by observing others.


The Bobo doll experiment, in short, involved researchers dividing children around four years old into two main groups. In the first group, an adult actor was placed in the same room with the child, and the room contained various toys. The child would play with their own toys while the adult played with others. The second group was the same, except the adult would physically attack the Bobo doll. After the adult left, researchers observed that children in the second group gradually imitated the adult's previous behaviors toward the Bobo doll—meaning, the children were seen hitting and verbally abusing the dolls.


Bandura initially had several hypotheses for this experiment: children who did not see the adult attack the doll would not attack it themselves. This was confirmed. Second, there were gender differences—boys were more likely to imitate the violent behavior toward the doll, which was also confirmed in later experiments.


Later variations of the Bobo doll experiment also confirmed that children can be influenced by reward and punishment systems. Children are more likely not to imitate violent behavior when they see adults who hit dolls being punished; conversely, they are more likely to imitate such behavior when they see those adults being rewarded. The conclusion is that these children, or people in general, are highly susceptible to being influenced by the behaviors of others around them.


Next, I would like to talk about domestic violence and attachment styles. There are roughly four dimensions of attachment styles, determined by two key dimensions— anxiety and avoidance. Based on these two dimensions, there are four types: secure, preoccupied (anxious), fearful-avoidant, and dismissive-avoidant. Aside from the secure type, the other three are considered insecure.


Children who grow up in a domestic violence environment are more likely to become one of these three insecure types in adulthood. A secure attachment style requires proper care and treatment during childhood, which is nearly impossible for children raised in a violent household. That concludes my sharing. Thank you.

Next, let's welcome Jackson to share with us the legal issues involved in domestic violence.


Jackson: The previous two guests shared with us, from a psychological perspective, the nature of domestic violence and its impacts. What comes to my mind now is: how can we provide solutions for victims of domestic violence? I know our audience comes from different regions, so I hope to offer a general legal framework outlining what rights victims have, and what kind of help we can offer if we encounter a victim. Of course, specific regulations require further research into local systems. Different countries or regions (such as different states in the U.S.) may have varying details. Therefore, when studying specific issues, it's necessary to return to local resources to find relevant legal information, and I'll also share with you how to locate such resources.

Legal systems can generally be divided into four levels.


The previous two guests have both explained what constitutes domestic violence. Simply put, legally defined domestic violence refers to violent acts between family members. Family members generally include spouses (current, former, or fiancé(e)s), intimate partners (such as romantic partners), relatives (siblings, adoptive parents, adoptive children, parents, children, step-parents, step-children, etc.), and those who jointly raise a child. In some regions, legal relationships formed through special civil agreements are also included within the definition of family relationships. In the United States, the law also defines dating violence, which is in fact very similar to domestic violence. However, in the legal systems of other countries, such behavior is typically included within the broader category of domestic violence. I hope everyone will not interpret the term "family relationship" purely based on its literal meaning. Domestic violence, beyond physical violence, psychological abuse, coercion (through force or language), economic control, and emotional control—already mentioned by the previous two speakers—also includes deprivation of freedom and persistent harassment.


Meanwhile, there are also legal justifications that may exclude certain acts from being classified as domestic violence. Generally speaking, we may have heard of self-defense or emergency avoidance in daily life. In such cases, although the acts objectively constitute violence, they are excluded from the definition of domestic violence. Additionally, due to differences in laws across countries or regions, certain acts may be excluded—for example, violent acts involved in child discipline.


Domestic violence may also overlap with other legal offenses. "Overlap" here means that a single act may be subject to multiple legal characterizations. For example, a person can simultaneously be a student at school, a child to their parents, and a friend to others. Similarly, legally, domestic violence can be both a civil tort and a criminal offense.


Generally, it may involve common criminal offenses such as: assault (intentional injury, intentional homicide, intentional injury resulting in death, negligent homicide), other personal crimes (threats by force, kidnapping, unlawful deprivation of freedom, abuse, abandonment, harassment, etc.), and sexual offenses (forced indecency, rape). Meanwhile, sexual offenses against children have special legal provisions because children are inherently in a more vulnerable position. In addition, economic crimes such as extortion, theft, and fraud may sometimes also be included within the scope of domestic violence.


How can domestic violence be legally addressed?


(1) Applying for a court order

Court orders are a common form of legal remedy in common law systems, including protection orders (including emergency protection orders), restraining orders, eviction orders, and counseling orders (in some countries). Protection orders can quickly free victims from ongoing threats and harm. Restraining and eviction orders can prohibit the abuser from engaging in certain behaviors or entering specific areas—for example, banning them from approaching a particular shelter, or even excluding them from shared property such as a home. This system aims to minimize the risk of contact between the abuser and the victim. Some countries or regions also have counseling orders, which require the abuser to attend cognitive-behavioral programs provided by the community, aiming to correct and educate them so they understand their behavior is wrong.


Why would abusers comply with court orders? Because compared to civil penalties or criminal imprisonment/detention, the consequences of violating a court order are relatively mild—but once an abuser violates the order, they may face harsher consequences such as fines or imprisonment. Therefore, they generally have motivation and reason to comply.


(2) Applying for shelter and financial assistance

When victims of domestic violence actively leave the abusive environment, they may lack financial resources or suitable housing. At such times, the state or region may provide shelters and financial aid. However, when applying, one must consider whether shelters and financial aid are available to non-citizens or overseas residents (some countries do not provide such support), and carefully review the eligibility requirements during the application process.


(3) Applying for mediation

Some theories suggest that domestic violence is a recurring behavior that is difficult to correct. However, from the victim's own perspective, they may still be unwilling to leave the abuser. In such cases, mediation can be used to impose behavioral restrictions on the abuser or secure financial compensation or commitments. While this is not a complete solution to leaving the abuser, and may not be ideal in some ways, it is relatively efficient and respects the victim's own wishes.


(4) Filing a lawsuit

Litigation is a final means of resolution. Since domestic violence involves civil torts or criminal offenses and also concerns familial relationships, such lawsuits may involve both civil and criminal (potentially) domains.

Host: What aspects are involved in changing marital status?


Jackson: First, one must determine the current marital status, especially overseas, where a marriage certificate alone does not necessarily guarantee that the abuser and victim are in a legally valid marriage.

Generally, valid marital statuses include: marriages without legal defects, de facto marriages, and in some countries or regions, cohabitation relationships that are legally treated as similar to marriage. Correspondingly, there may also be invalid or voidable marriages. For such "marriages," one should seek annulment or a declaration of invalidity rather than divorce.


Taking China as an example, the main grounds for invalid marriage are: bigamy, marriage due to disease or close kinship, or marriage below the legal age. In such cases, the marriage itself is not legally recognized and is considered invalid.


Voidable marriages are those entered into with procedural defects or under duress. If a victim married the abuser under coercion, such a marriage can be annulled.


(2) Divorce procedures

There are generally two types of divorce procedures: litigation-based divorce and mutual agreement divorce. However, not every country or region offers mutual agreement divorce, due to cultural differences—some jurisdictions only allow litigation-based divorce, so this must be assessed on a case-by-case basis.


(3) Grounds for divorce

The most liberal divorce condition stems from individual liberalism, meaning that as long as both parties express a desire for divorce, regardless of the reason, the government may issue a divorce certificate and dissolve the marriage upon request by one party. However, some countries or regions impose certain requirements for filing for divorce, such as a minimum duration of marriage (e.g., three years) or a required period of separation (e.g., two years). In cases involving domestic violence, there is generally an exception clause that treats domestic violence as a special circumstance, in which case the application for divorce is not subject to the standard divorce conditions. The most common condition is the breakdown of the marital relationship; when the court determines that the couple's relationship has irretrievably broken down, the marriage may be dissolved..


Besides domestic violence, let me introduce a few other situations that are relatively special and allow one to bypass standard divorce conditions, including abandonment and adultery.


(4) Special Damages System

Regarding domestic violence, there is a special damages system under marriage law. Different from the damages system in tort law, this requires the abuser or the at-fault party to provide certain compensation to the victim or the innocent party, in addition to property division. It is another compensation mechanism beyond marital property division and serves as a special penalty against the at-fault party.


(5) Spousal Support

For a fixed period or number of years, the financially stronger partner pays a certain amount of support to the relatively economically weaker partner to help them transition and better reintegrate into society.

Host: How is property divided after a change in marital status?


Jackson: First, one must determine the applicable property regime. The most common regime is the community property system, under which pre-marital property remains separate, while post-marital property is considered jointly owned. There is also the separate property system, under which both pre-marital and post-marital property are kept entirely separate. In addition, some countries or regions have a special form of community property regime established through specific agreements or negotiations. Once the marriage is established, all property—whether acquired before or after marriage—becomes jointly owned; however, this is not a very common arrangement.


Property division is generally approached from three perspectives . The first is equal division by amount, which is a relatively simple method. The second is division based on each party's contribution to the family. This contribution is not measured solely by financial input, but also includes each party's overall contribution to the household and support for the other's career—it is a comprehensive assessment.


Compared to a simple 50-50 split, dividing property based on contribution is generally more reasonable, but it requires a relatively well-developed legal system to implement. Moreover, since domestic violence is considered a fault, property division may be tilted in favor of the victim. Additionally, judges may exercise discretion based on the economic circumstances of both parties.

Host: How are child custody issues handled after a change in marital status?

Jackson: Child custody generally involves determining who will have guardianship. It should be noted that the parent without custody still has a duty to support the child, because they remain the child's father or mother. Guardianship merely designates the primary caregiver; the other parent still bears support responsibilities. The non-custodial parent must pay a certain amount of child support. Likewise, even without custody, they retain visitation rights, ensuring both parents have the opportunity to maintain contact with the child.


Meanwhile, in some countries, a parenting plan for children is a mandatory requirement for divorce. Even if the marital relationship changes, the child's education must remain a top priority. If the couple cannot make appropriate plans for the child's future, the dissolution of their marriage may be restricted.


Determination of custody primarily considers the child's welfare—what is in the best interest of the child. Factors include the primary caregiver during the child's upbringing, current living arrangements, the child's own wishes, the child's age, the parents' willingness and ability to care for the child, and the parents' financial situations. Since some of our audience are overseas Chinese, I would particularly emphasize the issue of children leaving the country. Taking a child out of the country usually involves special requirements; for example, in some countries, taking a child abroad without the other parent's consent may be considered child abduction..

Host: How does a change in marital status affect residency status and visas?


Jackson: A change in marital status may or may not affect residency status. For example, if the victim already holds a work visa, the change in marital status may have no impact on their residency rights. However, if marital status does affect residency, it may involve visa changes. This is generally related to the duration of the marriage. In some countries or regions, if the marriage has lasted a certain number of years, one may qualify for a lower-barrier visa; otherwise, one may have to go through standard application procedures.

Both civil and criminal matters involve time limitations—called the statute of limitations in civil cases and the prosecution period in criminal cases. This is a reminder that if you or someone around you experiences such an issue, it should be addressed as soon as possible. The longer it is delayed, the higher the time and emotional costs, and you may even lose the ability to protect your rights.

(2) Application of Overseas Law

Abroad, we have a special cross-border status, so we must consider whether the local court has jurisdiction or authority, and which laws apply.

(3) Hiring a Lawyer

In some countries and regions, legal representation is mandatory, meaning a lawyer must be present in court. Legal aid may be provided for those with limited financial means, so we need to prepare in advance.

Additionally, these three groups may face special challenges.



Immigrants

Immigrants, international students, or those on work visas may hesitate to seek help due to concerns about their immigration status. At the same time, language barriers, financial dependence, and limited social support can increase feelings of isolation and reduce access to resources.


Older women

They may face challenges related to age and the sunk costs of long-term emotional relationships. Moreover, many older women were born in an era where domestic violence was never discussed, so they may not perceive it as a serious issue. Alternatively, their partner may be experiencing health problems, which can increase their sense of dependency and responsibility.


Same-sex relationships

Many individuals may not seek help after being harmed because they do not want to disclose their sexual orientation; they may even worry that they lack convincing arguments to convince others, such as "I was sexually assaulted by a woman," causing hesitation when seeking help.

1. Call the local women's shelter or domestic violence hotline for help. Remember to make the call at a safe time and place to ensure personal safety; 2. Prepare an emergency kit


2. Prepare a emergency kit Keep a bag packed with everything you need—choose a relatively inconspicuous bag and place it in a relatively safe spot, while keeping some essential items within easy reach so you can grab and go if trouble arises.


3. Think carefully before taking action plan—clearly know where you are going and how to get there; prepare all of this in advance to prevent panic or emergencies.


4, Protect your communications and your location. Some abusers are very meticulous and may attempt to monitor your behavior. Use phones and computers cautiously, check your home or car for hidden cameras or listening devices, and if any devices have GPS functions, disable them. Frequently change your online account passwords, phone passcodes, facial and fingerprint unlock settings, and remember to regularly clear your browser history.

Host: From a legal perspective, what self-protection measures can we take when facing domestic violence?

Jackson:


1, Preservation of documentation.

Keep important documents within easy reach so you can take them quickly if needed. These documents can be divided into three main categories:


1) Identity information: Passport, residence permit, driver's license, birth certificate, bank cards and bank statements, proof of address, and even files containing important personal information or phone numbers.
