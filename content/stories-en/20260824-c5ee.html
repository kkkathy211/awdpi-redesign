1.5

Civil Torts and State Compensation Laws, etc.


In addition to criminal liability, you may also seek compensation through civil litigation or apply for state-level compensation. Relevant laws include:


1.5.1 Provisional Relief Measures


  • Under Article 10 of the Domestic Violence Victim Protection Act:

    When implementing Article 8, the competent authority—authorized by the Minister and holding a rank no lower than that of a senior administrative or police officer as defined under the Criminal Procedure Code—may issue orders providing certain temporary measures or relief for victims of domestic violence, regardless of whether the victim has made a request.

    Such temporary measures or relief should be limited to what is necessary and appropriate. They may include requiring the victim to undergo a medical examination, requiring the perpetrator to provide financial support based on their living conditions, prohibiting the perpetrator from entering the victim's household or approaching any of its members, and arrangements for child custody.

  • Under Article 11 of the Domestic Violence Victim Protection Act:

    During investigation or trial, the court may issue injunctions under Article 10, directing certain temporary measures or relief, or any other injunctions it deems appropriate.

    If the circumstances of the person committing domestic violence or the victim change, the court may modify, alter, or revoke the injunctions directing temporary measures or relief, issue new injunctions, or impose additional conditions.


1.5.2 Welfare Protection Measures


  • Under the Promotion of Development and Protection of Family Institution Act B.E. 2562 (2019) (See: https://www.refworld.org/legal/legislation/natlegbod/2007/35475?__cf_chl_f_tk=KYVWSgRAuaWyDGbLd2lNtIHQNvkPRodyotn9tgPZ8Uk-1783254422-1.0.1.1-N1s8pZikSXAyckVU9QNf01lRnjDYa7gOllGDXilyCAM) Article 28: After receiving a report of domestic violence, the Family Promotion and Protection Center (FPPC) may file a motion with the Juvenile and Family Court to request a "welfare protection measure" order.


1.5.3 Welfare Protection Cases


  • Under Article 7 of the Domestic Violence Victim Protection Act and Article 172 of the Juvenile and Family Court and Procedure Act B.E. 2553: Victims or interested parties may directly file an application for a "welfare protection case" with the Juvenile and Family Court, requesting the court to order welfare protection measures. If the victim is unable to file the application personally, it may be filed on their behalf by another person, such as a relative, official, public prosecutor, or officer from a relevant child/women's protection agency.


If you want to understand how to file compensation claims in practice, prepare evidence, and navigate procedural details, please read Section 3.6.

How to Seek Legal Protection?


3.1

Emergency Measures


3.1.1 Emergency Hotlines


In Thailand, prioritize remembering these numbers:

  • Police: 191

  • Medical Emergency: 1669

  • Social Assistance Hotline: 1300 (Managed by Thailand's Ministry of Social Development and Human Security, used to report urgent social issues and request social assistance; also serves as a referral point for domestic violence, child/women protection, etc.)

  • Tourist Police: 1155

    For foreign individuals unfamiliar with Thai language or local procedures, calling the Tourist Police hotline (1155) or the Social Assistance Hotline (1300) is recommended: the former handles emergencies involving tourists and foreigners in Thailand, while the latter assists with referrals to social services, child/women protection, and temporary shelter resources.

Source: Thailand.go.th, List of emergency telephone numbers, https://thailand.go.th/issue-focus-detail/003_003?hl=en; Thang Rath, (1300), https://www.thangrath.go.th/feature/thangrat-hotline-1300/


3.1.2 Preserving Evidence


Evidence helps police, hospitals, courts, or legal aid organizations quickly understand what you have experienced. But remember: preserving evidence should never come at the cost of remaining in danger. If the perpetrator is present, your phone is being monitored, or searching your phone could provoke them, leaving the dangerous situation is more important than taking photos.


Without increasing the risk, you can preserve the following evidence: photos or videos of injuries, hospital diagnosis certificates and medical records, police reports, chat logs, call records, threatening text messages or voice messages, photos of damaged items, witness statements from neighbors/friends/colleagues, observations from children or other family members, and written or recorded apologies or admissions from the abuser after the violence occurred.


When preserving evidence, prioritize safety: back up photos, chat logs, and important documents to an email account, cloud storage, or a trusted friend's device that the abuser cannot access. When seeking medical care, clearly inform the doctor about the source of the injuries and request that the injuries and their cause be documented. When reporting or seeking help, you may also request a case reference number or written confirmation from the receiving party.


Why is this evidence important? Regarding electronic evidence, Section 11 of Thailand's Electronic Transactions Act B.E. 2544 (2001) states that data messages cannot be excluded as legal evidence solely because they are in electronic form; when assessing their evidentiary value, courts will consider the reliability of their creation, storage, and transmission methods, the integrity of the information, and the means of identifying the sender. Therefore, electronic evidence such as chat logs, text messages, emails, social media private messages, and location records should retain original records, timestamps on screenshots, account information, and backup files whenever possible. See: https://www.etda.or.th/en/ETC/strategy-law-standard/law.aspx



3.2

Protection Orders


3.2.1 Introduction


Under Thailand's current Domestic Violence Victim Protection Act, the measure closest to China's "personal safety protection order" is the "provisional relief measure." Authorized officials and courts may, in domestic violence cases, order the abuser to cease contact, vacate the shared residence, pay necessary support, arrange medical examinations/treatment, or make temporary arrangements for child custody. Source: Domestic Violence Victim Protection Act B.E. 2550 (2007), ss 10–11.


Its significance lies in enabling authorized officials or courts to make protective arrangements before the case is fully resolved, thereby reducing immediate risks as much as possible.


3.2.2 Eligibility Criteria


If you are a victim of domestic violence, you may seek help, file a complaint, or apply for protection yourself. If you are unable to do so personally due to injury, control by the abuser, language barriers, age, or other reasons, authorized agencies, relatives, prosecutors, child/women protection officers, or other interested parties may, under certain circumstances, assist or initiate the process on your behalf. In cases involving minors, legal guardians or relevant protection agencies may also intervene. Source: Domestic Violence Victim Protection Act B.E. 2550 (2007), ss 5–7; Baker McKenzie, Fighting Domestic Violence: Thailand, 4.1.2 and 4.1.6.


Protection is not limited only to legally registered wives. According to the definition of "family member" under the Domestic Violence Victim Protection Act, spouses, former spouses, unregistered couples living together or who have previously lived together as a couple, children, adopted children, and cohabiting family members who are dependent may all be included in the scope of protection. This means that "not being legally married," "having broken up or divorced," or "the abuser claiming it is a private family matter" do not necessarily prevent you from seeking help first.


If your relationship does not meet the criteria of "family member" or cohabitation—such as in a dating relationship—it may require case-by-case assessment whether the domestic violence protection mechanism applies directly. However, this does not mean there are no options: you may still consider reporting to police, filing criminal charges, pursuing civil tort claims, or addressing harassment, threats, or unlawful detention, and seek referrals through 1300, 1155, hospital OSCC, legal aid agencies, or NGOs.


3.2.3 Application Procedure


Step 1: Report or file a complaint. The victim, a person with knowledge of the abuse, or anyone who discovers domestic violence can report it to the police, authorized administrative/social protection agencies, the 1300 hotline, or relevant protection organizations.


Step 2: Request immediate protection and intervention by medical institutions or social workers. Upon learning of domestic violence, the authorized agency may enter the relevant residence or incident site to investigate, arrange for the victim to receive medical examination and treatment, and provide counseling by psychiatrists, psychologists, or social workers. If the victim wishes to initiate criminal proceedings, the agency should assist in filing the complaint; if the victim is unable to do so personally, the agency may file the complaint on their behalf.


Step three: Apply for interim measures or court protection. Competent authorities may issue interim measures within the necessary and appropriate scope; courts may also order interim measures, modify or revoke measures, and attach appropriate conditions during investigation or trial. If pursuing a welfare protection case, victims or interested parties may file an application with the Juvenile and Family Court; the court will typically schedule a hearing. When applying, bring identification documents/passport, address information, the abuser's identity information, photos of injuries, medical records, chat logs, police reports, and child-related materials if applicable.


3.2.4 Legal Effects


The purpose of provisional or protective measures is to address immediate dangers: ensuring the victim receives medical examination and treatment; ordering the abuser to pay initial support or necessary expenses according to their financial capacity; prohibiting the abuser from entering the family residence or approaching the victim and family members; arranging temporary child custody; and requiring the abuser to cease violent behaviors and undergo counseling, treatment, or behavioral control.


Provisional measures issued by authorized officials must typically be submitted to the court for review within 48 hours, or the court will decide whether to continue enforcement. The court may also modify, revoke, or add conditions. Violating provisional measures approved by officials or the court may result in up to 3 months' imprisonment or a fine of up to 3,000 THB; violating a court order may result in up to 6 months' imprisonment or a fine of up to 6,000 THB. Therefore, if the abuser continues to harass, threaten, stalk, or approach, preserve records and promptly inform the police, court, social worker, or lawyer.


3.3

Shelter


3.3.1 Introduction


Thailand does not have a single national shelter entry point exclusively for "domestic violence victims," but the Ministry of Social Development and Human Security and its affiliated agencies provide social assistance, child and family shelters, women's protection, and vocational development services. In emergencies, victims can first call the 1300 social help hotline, which will assess the situation and refer them to local offices of the Ministry of Social Development and Human Security, child and family shelters, hospital OSCCs, police, or NGOs. Source: Thang Rath, 1300 Social Help Center, https://www.thangrath.go.th/feature/thangrat-hotline-1300/


3.3.2 Eligibility Criteria


Shelter or safe referral is recommended if any of the following apply: you are being chased or threatened; the abuser possesses weapons; the victim or child has no safe place to stay; sexual violence or serious physical harm has occurred; the abuser controls your documents, money, or communication; or the victim is pregnant, a child, elderly, disabled, or a foreigner without stable residency or language support.


Foreign victims can first seek help from police, hospitals, the 1300 hotline, or Tourist Police at 1155. If your passport is withheld, your visa is at risk, or you face human trafficking or forced prostitution, also seek assistance from your embassy/consulate, Tourist Police 1155, the anti-trafficking hotline 1599, or specialized NGOs. Do not endure violence alone due to concerns about visa status, language, or immigration status—these are matters for later resolution and should not prevent you from seeking help immediately.


3.3.3 Application Procedure


Recommended Path One: In case of immediate danger, call 191 for police or 1669 for emergency medical help; simultaneously call 1300 to request social protection referral. When calling, clearly state: your location, whether anyone is injured, whether the abuser is present, whether there are children or elderly persons involved, whether you need temporary shelter, and whether you need interpretation.


Recommended Path Two: If you are already at a hospital, request referral to a social worker. Hospitals can not only provide treatment and injury documentation but also offer psychological support, assist with reporting to police, and support follow-up applications for protection orders.


Recommended Path Three: If you are temporarily safe but cannot return home, contact your provincial office of the Ministry of Social Development and Human Security, women's/children's and family shelters, or local NGOs. Note: Do not disclose the shelter's address to the abuser before going. When leaving, prioritize taking your passport/ID, phone, bank cards, some cash, medication, children's documents, and key evidence. If you cannot take everything, do not delay leaving a dangerous situation.


3.4

Free Legal Aid


3.4.1 Introduction


Thailand's legal aid resources are primarily provided by government or quasi-government agencies offering basic legal consultation and financial support, bar associations providing free consultations and volunteer lawyers, and NGOs offering case-specific support for domestic and gender-based violence. Victims of domestic violence may seek help with: reporting and criminal complaints, applying for provisional protection or welfare protection orders, divorce, child custody/child support, compensation for crime victims, witness protection, interpretation, and social service referrals.


3.4.2 Government Legal Aid Agencies


3.4.2.1 Office of the Justice Fund


Agency Overview: The Justice Fund is not an ordinary "legal consultation center" but a financial support mechanism. Its responsibilities include providing financial assistance to poor and vulnerable individuals needing access to legal proceedings, such as legal fees, court costs, transportation, and accommodation; it also supports temporary release, aid for human rights victims, and public legal education.


Eligibility Criteria: Officially defined as "the poor and vulnerable public." Required documents include copies of ID card/civil servant card, household registration, case files, income proof, expense documentation, and National Welfare Card. Whether foreign victims are eligible is not specified on public English pages; it is recommended to first confirm with the provincial justice office or the Justice Fund office. Official sources state that applications are processed within 21 days in principle.


Contact Information: Website https://jfo.moj.go.th/page/people.php; English explanation https://www.thailand.go.th/issue-focus-detail/001_01_230?hl=en


3.4.2.2 Office of Rights Protection and Legal Aid, Office of the Attorney General


Agency Overview: This is a rights protection and legal aid agency under Thailand's Office of the Attorney General, with service networks at both central and local levels. Services include legal consultation, rights protection, assistance with certain non-litigious matters and court applications, and access to volunteer lawyers. For victims of domestic violence, it may help you understand basic issues such as reporting abuse, criminal procedures, rights protection, and resource referrals. The official website clearly states: "Have a legal issue? Consult a prosecutor—hotline 1157."


Eligibility criteria: When contacting, you can directly state that you are experiencing domestic violence and need consultation regarding protective measures, criminal complaints, child arrangements, or divorce-related rights. The website does not list uniform eligibility criteria for whether the agency can represent or assist with specific types of domestic violence, divorce, or custody cases; this is determined by the local office based on its authority and the nature of the case.


Contact information: Hotline 1157, official website https://www3.ago.go.th/center/lawaid/

3.5

Pursuing criminal liability of the abuser


In Thailand, criminal liability related to domestic violence can broadly be divided into three categories: first, the specific offense of "domestic violence"; second, crimes involving physical violence, such as bodily harm or unlawful detention; and third, crimes involving sexual violence, such as rape or procuring someone for another's sexual purposes. It should be noted that a single act may violate multiple criminal provisions—for example, an abuser may simultaneously commit the crime of "domestic violence" and the offense of "bodily harm" under the Penal Code, and both can be pursued concurrently without conflict.


3.5.1 Possible criminal charges against the abuser


3.5.1.1 Crime of Domestic Violence


This is a criminal charge specifically established in Thailand to address domestic violence. According to Article 4 of the Domestic Violence Victims Protection Act, anyone who commits an act of domestic violence is guilty of the crime of domestic violence and may be sentenced to up to six months' imprisonment, or a fine of up to 6,000 THB, or both.


It should be noted that this offense is classified as "a crime that may be settled" (see 3.5.3.3 for details), but any settlement applies only to the domestic violence charge itself and does not affect the prosecution of more serious offenses under the Penal Code. In other words, even if the abusive act also constitutes a more serious crime under the Penal Code, you may still pursue liability for the more serious offense.


3.5.1.2 Crime of Bodily Harm


If the abuser punches, kicks, shoves, strangles, or attacks you with an object, causing physical or mental harm, this may constitute the crime of bodily harm under Articles 295 to 300 of the Penal Code, punishable by up to two years' imprisonment, or a fine of up to 4,000 THB, or both. The more severe the injury, the heavier the penalty.


This charge can be pursued alongside the crime of domestic violence. This means that even if the abuser attempts to downplay the incident as a "family matter" or "marital dispute," you can still pursue criminal liability under the Penal Code as long as actual harm has occurred.


3.5.1.3 Crime of Rape


According to Article 276 of the Penal Code, anyone who engages in sexual intercourse with another person against their will, by means of threat, violence, taking advantage of the victim's inability to resist, or by impersonating another person, is guilty of rape and may be sentenced to four to twenty years' imprisonment and fined between 8,000 and 40,000 THB.


Two points are particularly important for victims. First, following the 2019 amendment to the Penal Code (No. 27), the provision now uses gender-neutral language—the victim is not limited to women, nor is the perpetrator limited to men. Second, rape can occur within marriage; that is, "marital rape" is a crime in Thailand. Furthermore, the 2025 amendment to the Penal Code (No. 30), effective from December 30, 2025, further expands the definition: any act involving penetration with a sexual organ, another body part, or an object may be considered rape. Therefore, if you are forced into sexual acts within marriage or an intimate relationship, this is a prosecutable crime, not a "private matter between spouses."


3.5.1.4 Crime of Procuring for Another's Sexual Purposes


According to Article 282 of the Penal Code, anyone who entices, lures, or takes away another person (regardless of the person's consent) for the purpose of satisfying another's sexual desire and engaging in lewd acts may be sentenced to one to ten years' imprisonment and fined between 2,000 and 20,000 THB. If the victim is a minor, the penalty is significantly increased: if the victim is under 18 years old, the sentence is three to fifteen years' imprisonment; if under 15, the sentence is five to twenty years' imprisonment. Anyone who assists in committing such acts is punished under the same standard.


If the abuser not only abuses you personally but also "provides" or takes you to others, or coerces you into sex work, this provision can serve as a basis for prosecution; when minors are involved, legal protection is especially strict.


3.5.1.5 Crime of Unlawful Detention


If the abuser locks you in the house, restricts your movement, prevents you from going out or leaving, or otherwise deprives you of personal freedom, this may constitute the crime of unlawful detention under Articles 310 to 311 of the Penal Code, punishable by up to three years' imprisonment, or a fine of up to 6,000 THB, or both. If the detention results in death or serious bodily harm, heavier penalties under Articles 290, 297, and 298 will apply.


Such situations are actually common in domestic violence cases—for example, having your phone and identification documents confiscated, being locked in a room, or being prevented from seeking medical care or reporting to the police. These acts may already constitute crimes, not merely being "strictly controlled."


3.5.2 Public Prosecution


"Public prosecution" refers to the state (through prosecutors) initiating criminal proceedings against the abuser in the public interest. For victims, this means you do not have to bear the full responsibility of prosecuting the abuser alone—once a case enters the criminal process, it will be advanced by investigators and prosecutors. The following explains the conditions required to initiate public prosecution and the general process.


3.5.2.1 Standards for Public Prosecution


According to the Domestic Violence Victims Protection Act, criminal prosecution primarily involves three provisions.


Article 5 establishes a reporting obligation: victims of domestic violence, or any person who discovers or becomes aware of domestic violence, has a duty to report it to the competent authority; anyone making a report in good faith is protected by law and bears no civil, criminal, or administrative liability. This means that third parties such as neighbors, relatives, friends, or medical personnel can report on your behalf without fear of legal consequences.


Article 6 concerns initiating criminal proceedings: upon learning of domestic violence, the competent authority has the right to enter the relevant residence or incident site to investigate, interview relevant persons, and arrange for you to receive medical examination, treatment, and psychological or social work counseling. If you wish to initiate criminal proceedings, the competent authority shall obtain your complaint in accordance with the Criminal Procedure Code; if you are unable to file it personally, the authority may file it on your behalf.


Article 7 concerns the statute of limitations, which is particularly important: if you do not submit information or file a complaint within three months from the day you "were able or had the opportunity to report or complain personally," the right to pursue criminal liability expires. Therefore, please report as early as possible. Even if this deadline is missed, you may still apply for welfare protection under the Juvenile and Family Court Establishment and Procedure Act (see 3.2).


3.5.2.2 Public Prosecution Procedure


According to Article 8 of the Domestic Violence Victims Protection Act, if you file a complaint within the three-month limitation period, the investigator shall immediately begin an investigation and transfer the victim, investigation file, and opinion to the prosecutor; the prosecutor shall file public charges with the court within 48 hours of apprehending the abuser. If prosecution cannot be completed on time due to genuine necessity, the prosecutor may apply to the court for an extension, but each extension may not exceed six days, and no more than three consecutive extensions are allowed.


One safeguard during the process is particularly important for victims: when being questioned, you have the right to request the presence of a psychiatrist, psychologist, social worker, or someone you trust to accompany and support you; only in emergencies where such accompaniment is genuinely impossible may investigators question you in their absence, and the reason must be recorded in the case file. This means you have the right to participate in the investigation in a supported and accompanied environment, without facing it alone.


3.5.3 Reporting/Complaint Procedures


This section explains the practical conditions and steps for reporting and filing complaints from the perspective of "what you can do." It is based on the same laws as the "public prosecution" section above, but focuses more on the actual process you, as the affected party, need to follow.


3.5.3.1 Conditions for Reporting/Filing a Complaint


According to Section 5 of the Domestic Violence Victims Protection Act, the threshold for reporting is actually very low: not only you yourself, but any person who discovers or becomes aware of domestic violence has the right (and duty) to report it to the competent officer. Moreover, as long as the report is made in good faith, the reporter is protected by law and will not incur civil, criminal, or administrative liability as a result.


This means you do not need to wait until you have "complete evidence" or are "hurt badly enough" to report; if you are temporarily unable to come forward personally, you may also ask relatives, friends, neighbors, or social workers to report on your behalf.


3.5.3.2 Procedure for Reporting/Complaint


According to Section 6 of the Domestic Violence Victims Protection Act, reporting can be done in any convenient manner—verbally, in writing, by phone, or electronically—and is not restricted by form.


After a report is made, the responsible officer has the authority to enter the relevant residence or incident site, question the perpetrator, you, and any persons present, and may arrange for you to receive medical examination or treatment, as well as counseling from a psychiatrist, psychologist, or social worker. If you wish to initiate prosecution, the officer will assist you in filing a criminal complaint under the Criminal Procedure Code; if you are unable or do not have the opportunity to file personally, the officer may file on your behalf.


In practice, two suggestions: when reporting, try to bring materials that can prove your identity and the incident, such as identification documents or passport, photos of injuries, medical diagnosis certificates, chat or call records (for how to preserve evidence, see Section 3.1); at the same time, remember to request a report receipt or case reference number to facilitate follow-up on the case progress.


3.5.3.3 Special Case Disposition and Victim's Reconciliation


It should be specifically noted that reconciliation is possible in domestic violence cases, but this is entirely your choice—you are under no obligation to accept reconciliation.


According to Section 4 of the Domestic Violence Victims Protection Act, domestic violence is a reconcilable offense; however, reconciliation is limited only to the domestic violence offense itself and does not affect more serious offenses prescribed under the Criminal Code or other laws. According to Section 16, to facilitate reconciliation, the competent officer or court may appoint a mediator (such as both parties' parents, guardians, relatives, or suitable persons like social workers) to assist in communication; the mediation outcome must be reported to the competent officer or court, and if an agreement is reached, a reconciliation agreement will be established. Such an agreement will only be recognized and enforced if it does not violate the law, public order, or good customs.


We also want to remind you: in domestic violence cases, "reconciliation" or "forgiveness" should be approached with caution. The abuser or their family may sometimes exert pressure using reasons such as "I will change," "for the sake of the children," or "it won't happen again," but repeated domestic violence often escalates progressively. The decision on whether to reconcile, and under what conditions, always remains in your hands; if you have doubts, it is recommended to first consult a social worker, lawyer, or professional anti-domestic violence organization (see Section 3.4) before making a decision.



3.6

Civil Compensation and Relief


3.6.1 Ancillary Civil Compensation


A court's criminal judgment may include a compensation order.


Section 44/1 of the Thai Criminal Procedure Code states:


In a public prosecution, if the victim has the right to claim compensation because the defendant's criminal act has caused death, or bodily or mental injury, loss of personal freedom, damage to reputation, or property loss, the victim may file a motion with the court hearing the criminal case, requesting that the defendant be ordered to compensate for such losses.


A motion filed under Paragraph 1 must be submitted before evidence is taken; if no evidence is required, it must be submitted before the case is heard. Such a motion shall be treated as a complaint under the Civil Procedure Code, and the victim shall be the plaintiff. Therefore, the motion must contain reasonable details regarding the losses and the amount of compensation claimed. If the court finds the motion insufficient, it may order the movant to correct it.


A motion filed under Paragraph 1 must not include any request other than compelling the defendant to compensate for losses caused by their criminal act, and must not contradict or conflict with the charges brought by the public prosecutor. Provided that the public prosecutor has complied with the requirements of Section 43, the victim has no right to file a motion under Paragraph 1 seeking return of property or its value.


3.6.2 Independent Tort Litigation


You may separately file a lawsuit to claim compensation for bodily or mental harm.


Section 420 of the Civil and Commercial Code: Any person who intentionally or negligently infringes upon another person's life, body, health, freedom, property, or other rights, thereby committing a wrongful act, must compensate the victim for the damages suffered.


Section 446 of the Civil and Commercial Code: The victim may also petition the court for non-pecuniary damages (such as compensation for mental suffering). This right cannot be transferred to heirs.


3.6.3 State Compensation


Currently, Thai law does not have a widely operating "State Compensation Fund for Crime Victims" system (similar to the Victim Compensation Programs in many Western countries).


However, there are some provisions/laws that involve similar mechanisms:


3.6.3.1 Victim Compensation and Restitution for the Accused Act (B.E. 2544 / 2001)


This is a Thai law concerning compensation for criminal victims and compensation and expenses for the accused (English title: Damages for the Injured Person and Compensations and Expenses for the Accused in the Criminal Case Act, B.E. 2544 (2001)). The law aims to establish an institution/committee to determine compensation for victims in criminal cases (such as medical expenses, death compensation, loss of income, etc.), as well as compensation for acquitted defendants. It provides a formalized, state-level compensation mechanism, rather than relying solely on court judgments.


Note: The practical application and procedural details of this law require further consultation of the Royal Gazette or the official websites of the Ministry of Justice / Legislative Assembly. Some sources indicate that the system exists but is not as comprehensive or widely implemented as in certain Western countries, and formal application must be made according to the provisions of the law.


3.6.3.2 Victim Compensation and Restitution Board / Similar Mechanisms


Some legal opinions and unofficial sources mention that Thailand has a Victim Compensation Board, through which victims may apply for compensation (e.g., for medical expenses, loss of income, death benefits)—but there are strict regulations regarding application deadlines and eligibility.


3.7

Divorce


3.7.1 Types of Divorce


Two methods: mutual consent divorce (uncontested divorce), and litigation divorce (contested divorce)

Mutual consent divorce, also known as administrative divorce. Both spouses agree on all matters, including property division, child custody, and alimony, and can complete the divorce procedures quickly at the local district office (Amphoe) without appearing in court.

Litigation divorce is required when the spouses cannot reach an agreement, involving filing a lawsuit, potentially multiple hearings, submission of evidence, and legal representation.


3.7.2 Grounds/Conditions for Divorce


Section 1514 of the Civil and Commercial Code: Methods of Divorce: Divorce is effective only through mutual consent or a court judgment. Mutual consent must be in writing and certified by the signatures of at least two witnesses.


Section 1515 of the Civil and Commercial Code: Mutual Consent Divorce: For marriages registered under this law, mutual consent divorce is valid only when both spouses jointly register the divorce.


3.7.3 Divorce Procedure


Section 1516 of the Civil and Commercial Code: Grounds for Divorce: The grounds for divorce litigation are as follows:

1. If the husband supports another woman or treats another woman with the respect due to a wife, or if the wife commits adultery, the other spouse may file for divorce;

2. Even if such misconduct does not constitute a criminal offense, if it causes the other spouse to suffer any of the following harms, it constitutes grounds for divorce: a. extreme shame; b. humiliation or hatred from continuing the marital relationship with the offending spouse; or c. if the harm or trouble is excessive, considering the circumstances, status, and shared life of both spouses, the other spouse may file for divorce;

3. If one spouse causes serious physical or mental harm or torment to the other, or seriously insults the other spouse or their elders, the other spouse may file for divorce;

4. If one spouse abandons the other for more than one year, the other may file for divorce; a. (4/1) If one spouse is convicted of a crime and receives a final court judgment of imprisonment for more than one year, and the other spouse did not participate in, consent to, or know of the criminal act, and continuing to live together as spouses would cause excessive harm or trouble to the other spouse, the latter may file for divorce; b. (4/2) If the spouses have voluntarily lived apart for more than three years due to inability to live together in peace, or have been ordered by the court to live apart for more than three years, either spouse may file for divorce;

5. If one spouse is declared missing, or has left their residence or place of abode for more than three years, and their life or death cannot be determined;

6. If one spouse fails to provide proper maintenance and support to the other, or engages in serious conduct damaging to the marital relationship, causing the other spouse to face extreme hardship, considering the circumstances, status, and shared life of both spouses, the other spouse may file for divorce;

7. If one spouse has suffered from a mental illness continuously for more than three years, and the illness is difficult to cure, making it impossible to sustain the marriage, the other spouse may file for divorce;

8. If one spouse violates a good conduct agreement they have undertaken, the other spouse may file for divorce;

9. If one spouse suffers from an incurable and dangerous contagious disease that may harm the other spouse, the other spouse may file for divorce;

10. If one spouse is permanently unable to live together as husband and wife due to physical disability, the other spouse may file for divorce.


3.7.4 Child Custody


  • Section 1520 of the Civil and Commercial Code: Child Custody

In cases of mutual consent divorce, the spouses shall reach a written agreement on the exercise of custody over their children. If no such agreement exists or cannot be reached, the court shall decide.

If a court rules on divorce, the court hearing the divorce case must also determine which party shall have custody of the children. If during the proceedings it is deemed necessary to deprive a spouse of custody under Article 1582, the court may issue an order depriving that spouse of custody and appointing a third party as guardian, provided that the child's welfare and best interests are taken into account.


3.7.5 Child Support


  • Civil and Commercial Code Article 1522 Child Support

If both parties agree to divorce, the divorce agreement must include arrangements specifying whether one or both spouses will bear the cost of child support and the amount to be paid.

If the divorce is granted by court judgment, or if the divorce agreement does not include provisions regarding child support, the court will make the determination.


3.7.6 Spousal Support


  • Civil and Commercial Code Article 1523 Spousal Compensation

When a court grants a divorce based on the grounds specified in Article 1516(1), either the husband or wife has the right to claim compensation from the other party (if applicable).

A husband has the right to claim compensation from any person who commits adultery with his wife, and a wife has the right to claim compensation from any other woman who publicly engages in a relationship with her former husband. However, the right to claim compensation does not exist if the husband or wife consents to or tacitly permits the conduct under Article 1516(1), or allows the other party to act as provided in paragraph (2).


  •  Civil and Commercial Code Article 1524 Intentional Fault

If a divorce petition is filed under Article 1516 paragraphs (3), (4), or (6), and the grounds arise from the intentional misconduct of one party that renders the other party's life unbearable, thereby necessitating the divorce, the injured party has the right to claim compensation from the at-fault party.


  • " Civil and Commercial Code Article 1525 Determination of Compensation

Under Articles 1523 and 1524, the amount of compensation shall be determined by the court according to the specific circumstances. The court may order a lump-sum payment or periodic payments, as it deems appropriate.

If the person liable for compensation is the other spouse, the property share received by the former from the Sin Somros settlement upon divorce shall be taken into account.


  • Civil and Commercial Code Article 1526 Consideration of the Paying Party's Ability

In divorce cases where the grounds for divorce stem solely from the fault of one party and the divorce would leave the other party impoverished—unable to rely on property or income acquired during the marriage—the injured party may apply for a maintenance allowance from the at-fault party. The court may decide whether to grant such an allowance based on the payer's financial capacity and the recipient's living conditions, applying by analogy the provisions of Articles 1598/39, 1598/40, and 1598/41.

The right to claim a maintenance allowance is forfeited if the claim is not raised in the divorce petition or counterclaim.


  • Civil and Commercial Code Article 1527 Maintenance of a Mentally Ill or Ill Spouse

If divorce is granted on the grounds of mental illness under Article 1516(7), or on the grounds of a dangerous contagious disease under Article 1516(9), the other spouse shall provide a maintenance allowance to the mentally ill or ill spouse in accordance with Article 1526.


  • Civil and Commercial Code Article 1528 Remarriage of the Supported Spouse

If the spouse receiving the maintenance allowance remarries, the right to receive the allowance ceases.



 NO.4 

Visa Issues


If your visa was obtained based on your marital relationship, one of the most immediate concerns after experiencing domestic violence is: "Will I lose my legal status immediately if I report the abuse, separate, or divorce?"


Here is what you should know first: Whether you can continue to stay in Thailand is ultimately decided by the Thai Immigration Bureau based on the law and your individual circumstances—not by the abuser alone. Specifically, several visa types may help you remain legally in Thailand.


4.1

Visa Types and Risks


Common independent visas in Thailand, such as student visas, fall under the Education (ED) category; marriage-based visas are classified as Non-Immigrant O visas.

You should be aware that a marriage visa becomes invalid after divorce. Therefore, if you hold an O visa, we recommend that you promptly check your visa status and, depending on your situation, apply for an extension or switch to another visa type—rather than waiting until your visa expires.


Also, do not refrain from seeking help due to threats from the abuser such as "I will cancel your visa" or "You will be deported immediately." The abuser has no right to unilaterally cancel your visa. Whether a marriage visa remains valid is determined by the Thai Immigration Bureau based on legal provisions and visa conditions—not by the personal decision of the spouse.


If the Immigration Bureau determines that your visa needs to be re-evaluated, you will generally be notified through official procedures and asked to provide supporting documents. Before a decision is made, you usually have the opportunity to explain your situation, submit evidence, and legally protect your right to stay. Therefore, a change in marital status does not automatically result in immediate visa cancellation or immediate departure from Thailand.


4.2

(For dependent visas) Residence options after relationship breakdown


4.2.1 Applying for Visa Extension


If you need to report domestic violence, apply for a protection order, or participate in criminal or other judicial proceedings, the Immigration Bureau may, in practice, consider these circumstances as valid reasons to allow you to apply for a short-term stay extension or other appropriate visa arrangements to ensure your safety and the proper handling of your case.


If you are undergoing divorce or criminal proceedings, you may also apply for a visa extension, but you will typically need to submit documents proving your involvement in judicial processes, such as a TM.7 application form, passport copy, official case confirmation letter from an investigator, proof of participation in legal proceedings, or other official documents.


We also want to remind you: Visa extension applications usually require some processing time. If you have decided to seek legal assistance, we recommend preparing the necessary documents as early as possible, rather than waiting until your visa is about to expire, to avoid jeopardizing your legal residency status.


Source: https://www.thailand.go.th/issue-focus-detail


4.2.2 Applying for Other Visas/Residence Permits


If you wish to continue legally residing in Thailand after the termination of your marriage, you may consider switching to another non-dependent status, for example:


4.2.2.1 Short-term Tourist/Visit Visa


Includes single-entry or multiple-entry tourist visas. A single-entry tourist visa generally allows a 60-day stay, extendable by 30 days; a multiple-entry tourist visa allows multiple entries within six months, with a maximum stay of 60 days per entry, and can also be legally extended.


4.2.2.2 90-Day Non-Immigrant Visa


Suitable for business or educational purposes. Initially allows a 90-day stay, and upon meeting conditions, can be extended through the Thai Immigration Bureau into a one-year visa for specific purposes such as retirement or marriage.


4.2.2.3 Retirement Visa (Thailand Retirement Visa) (Non-Immigrant OA Visa)


Available to individuals aged 50 or older who meet financial requirements, unaffected by marital status. Holders may stay in Thailand for one full year and renew annually.


4.2.2.4 Work Visa/Business Visa (Type B)


If you have started a business in Thailand or have recently been hired by a Thai company, you may apply for this visa. After obtaining a 90-day Non-Immigrant B visa, you can apply for a work permit within Thailand. Once approved, you can convert your visa into a one-year Non-Immigrant B visa, extending your stay to one year.


4.2.2.5 Long-Term Resident Visa (LTR Visa)


Designed for individuals who meet certain income, asset, or professional criteria, such as highly skilled professionals, remote workers, or wealthy retirees, allowing up to ten years of long-term residence.


Wealthy Global Citizens: Must have at least USD 1 million in assets and USD 80,000 in annual income over the past two years, plus an investment of at least USD 500,000 in Thailand.


Wealthy Retirees: Aged 50 or above, with an annual income of USD 80,000, or USD 40,000 with a USD 250,000 investment in Thai government bonds, real estate, or a business.


Thai Remote Work Professionals: Remote workers from reputable foreign companies must have an annual income of USD 80,000 (or USD 40,000 if holding a master's degree, intellectual property, or Series A funding).


Highly Skilled Professionals: Professionals in targeted fields must earn an annual income of USD 80,000 (or USD 40,000 with a relevant master's degree) and be employed by a company in the designated industry.


4.2.2.6 Thailand Elite Visa


Requires a valid foreign passport, clean record (no criminal or bankruptcy history), and payment of membership fees ranging from 900,000 THB for a 5-year membership to 5,000,000 THB for a 20-year membership, with validity from 5 to 20 years.


4.2.2.7 Thailand Digital Nomad Visa (DTV Visa)


Designed for remote workers or individuals participating in Thailand-recognized "soft power" activities (e.g., Muay Thai training, cooking classes, long-term medical treatment, sports training, art festivals, or music festivals), allowing a maximum stay of 180 days per entry, with legal options for renewal.


4.2.3 Long-term Residence through Custody of Thai National Children


If you have children who are Thai nationals, even after divorce, you may apply for and renew a Non-Immigrant O visa to continue legally residing in Thailand to care for your children. This typically requires that the children are unmarried, live with you, and are under 20 years of age.



Finally, we want to remind you: While visa issues are important, your safety is always more important than your visa status. If you are facing ongoing personal danger, please prioritize your personal safety and promptly seek help from the police, social workers, or domestic violence support organizations.
