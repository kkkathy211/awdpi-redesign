Introduction:

On June 24 local time, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled to overturn the 1973 "Roe v. Wade" decision, eliminating constitutional protection for women's abortion rights. As a result, abortion rights will revert to "state jurisdiction," and conflicts among states over abortion rights have intensified—states banning abortion have already begun implementing abortion bans ahead of schedule.


The U.S. abortion bans

          are broadly divided into three categories, including:


1

"trigger laws"—once the Supreme Court overturns the "Roe v. Wade" decision, states that have passed such laws will automatically enact abortion bans;

2

pre-Roe abortion bans that were in place before the Roe decision;

3

and recently passed bans that anticipated the Supreme Court's ruling.



The Center for Reproductive Rights and its partners are working tirelessly to prevent abortion bans from taking effect across states. This week, they filed a series of lawsuits aimed at blocking abortion bans in Louisiana, Mississippi, and Texas, which has temporarily slowed the enforcement of these bans. Currently, the "trigger laws" in Louisiana and Utah, as well as Texas's abortion ban, have all been suspended, and abortion-providing facilities have reopened.


"Courts in Louisiana, Texas, and Utah have made fair decisions to block these bans from taking effect. In such a dark historical moment, these states' actions are undoubtedly good news for us, " said Jenny Ma, senior attorney at the Center. "This way, women seeking abortions can still access essential healthcare."


Louisiana and Texas held hearings on July 8 and July 12 respectively to discuss whether the bans should take effect. However, Texas also filed a motion requesting a stay on the restrictions against its abortion ban.



Florida's restriction on the ban's enforcement


Another case from reproductive rights organizations shows that on June 30, a Florida court ruled to block the state's abortion ban—which was set to take effect on July 1 as a 15-week restriction. The court found the ban violated Florida's constitution, which contains a clear provision—the right to personal privacy, which includes the right to abortion,.



U.S. Supreme Court

eliminating constitutional protection for women's abortion rights


The U.S. Supreme Court's decision to overturn Roe v. Wade arose from Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization. This case challenged Mississippi's law banning abortions after 15 weeks of pregnancy, requiring the Supreme Court to rule on the law's constitutionality. Ultimately, the nearly 50-year-old precedent set by Roe v. Wade was overturned, and the authority to legislate on abortion was fully returned to the states.


"I began my medical career before Roe v. Wade; but I never imagined our country would return to a place where doctors are prevented from helping women, " said Alan Braid, owner of Alamo Women's Reproductive Services. "Abortion is central to reproductive healthcare, and no one should be forced to cross state lines to exercise the most basic and urgent right to healthcare."


On June 24, the U.S. Supreme Court announced its decision to abandon a nearly 50-year precedent, stripping constitutional protection for abortion rights and paving the way for states to ban abortion. The ruling came in Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization, a case challenging Mississippi's law banning abortion after 15 weeks of pregnancy. This decision marks the first time in history that the Supreme Court has taken away a fundamental right..


Since the Supreme Court's ruling, many states have halted abortion services. Alabama, Arkansas, Oklahoma, Mississippi, Missouri, and South Dakota have banned abortion. In several other states, abortion care is not being provided due to unclear legal status. Due to the court's decision, it is estimated that up to half of U.S. states will ban abortion..


"I began my medical career before Roe v. Wade, and I never thought our country would return to criminalizing doctors and stopping us from helping women," said Dr. Alan Braid, an abortion provider and owner of Alamo Women's Reproductive Services in San Antonio. "Abortion is a standard and necessary part of maternal healthcare. No one should be forced to cross state lines to access basic, time-sensitive healthcare services." ".



Litigation update:

Blocking the enforcement of state abortion bans


After the U.S. Supreme Court eliminated constitutional abortion rights on June 24, anti-abortion states began implementing abortion bans. The Center for Reproductive Rights and its partners are working hard to prevent these bans from taking effect, to protect women's access to abortion..


Lawsuits filed on behalf of abortion providers are challenging states' "trigger" laws, pre-Roe abortion bans that were never repealed, and other laws that ban or severely restrict abortion.


This week, laws in Louisiana and Arizona were temporarily blocked..


"The impact of the U.S. Supreme Court's decision has been swift and severe, with abortion services immediately halting in many states," said CEO Nancy Northup. "We are witnessing the beginning of a public health crisis that will engulf the nation. Our top priority is to keep abortion services available for as long as possible in every state. Every day and every hour that clinics remain open is a victory for patients in the waiting room."


Jenny Ma, senior attorney at the Center, commenting on the Louisiana ruling allowing abortion services to continue, said: "This is incredible relief for people in Louisiana who need abortion care right now. Abortions can resume in the state today, avoiding further irreversible harm. Our work continues—we now look forward to Monday's hearing, where we will ask the judge to more permanently block these bans." ".


Below is a summary of ongoing cases:


South Carolina: On July 13, a lawsuit was filed in a South Carolina court to again block the state's law banning abortion after approximately six weeks of pregnancy. Although earlier this year a federal appeals court upheld a district court ruling blocking the law, after the U.S. Supreme Court revoked constitutional abortion rights, the federal district court granted the governor's emergency motion, suspending the injunction and allowing the ban to take effect. The lawsuit argues that the law (S.B.1) violates South Carolinians' constitutional right to equal protection because it bans abortion without providing adequate protections for patients' health, and conditions survivors of sexual assault's access to abortion on disclosing personal information to law enforcement..


Louisiana: On July 12, a state court in Baton Rouge blocked Louisiana's trigger ban, allowing abortion services to resume in the state. The judge granted the Center's request for a temporary restraining order against the ban and scheduled a hearing for July 18 at 8:30 a.m. CT to discuss longer-term relief. On July 8, the judge ruled that the case challenging the state's trigger ban must change venues, thereby halting abortion care. Two days earlier, the Louisiana Supreme Court had denied the state's request to allow abortion services to resume. That court's ruling temporarily blocked trigger bans in three states. The lawsuit argues that the state's trigger law is unconstitutional.


Arizona: On July 11, following a hearing on July 8, a federal district court granted a request to block Arizona's personhood law as it applies to abortion. The "personhood requirement," challenged in a case filed in August 2021, would grant fertilized eggs, embryos, and fetuses the same "rights and privileges" as "other persons" under all Arizona laws. The personhood law is one of several conflicting laws in the state, including pre-Roe bans, which have increased legal confusion.


North Dakota: On July 7, a lawsuit was filed in state court to block the enforcement of the state's trigger ban. The state attorney general said the ban would take effect on July 28. The case argues that the trigger ban is unconstitutional under the North Dakota Constitution, which guarantees the right to life, liberty, security, and happiness—all of which protect the right to abortion..


Florida: A Florida court announced on June 30 that it would issue an injunction to block the state's 15-week abortion ban, set to take effect on July 1. On July 5, the state appealed the ruling, triggering an automatic stay that allows the ban to remain in effect during the litigation. The case argues that the law (HB5) is unconstitutional under the Florida Constitution, which contains a clear provision for the right to privacy, ".


Texas: A lawsuit was filed in a Texas court to block officials from enforcing the state's pre-Roe abortion ban, which once completely prohibited abortion but had been interpreted as repealed. On June 28, the court granted a temporary restraining order blocking enforcement of the ban. On July 1, the Texas Supreme Court partially granted the state attorney general's request, allowing civil enforcement of the abortion ban. A hearing on a permanent injunction against the ban is scheduled for July 12.


Mississippi: A case filed on June 27 challenges Mississippi's trigger ban and six-week ban in state court, arguing that the state constitution protects the right to abortion. After a hearing on July 5, the Mississippi court denied the request to block the bans from taking effect.


Oklahoma: On July 1, a lawsuit was filed with the Oklahoma Supreme Court to block the state's pre-Roe abortion ban enacted in 1910, as well as a comprehensive abortion ban set to take effect in August. The lawsuit claims that criminal abortion bans violate the rights to personal and bodily autonomy, health, and substantive due process guaranteed by the Oklahoma Constitution..



Want to defend abortion rights?

Here are seven things everyone can do.


The U.S. Supreme Court has announced the removal of protections for women's abortion rights. If you want to help defend abortion rights, here are seven actions you can take. Starting today, the following seven actions can serve as practical steps for everyone to defend abortion rights.:


1

Donate to the National Abortion Funds. The National Abortion Funds provide direct assistance to women seeking abortion care, including financial support and practical help such as transportation, accommodation, and language interpretation services. The National Abortion Funds are a vital organization in safeguarding women's abortion rights. 2

Donate to "Keep Our Clinics," an independent U.S. abortion clinic network. Donate. In the United States, small community clinics provide abortion services, but these clinics may operate illegally within certain states. If clinics shut down, entire communities lose access to abortion, reproductive, and sexual health services.

3

Join protests and speak out for abortion rights legislation. You can participate in local demonstrations or rallies. Protests supporting abortion rights continue across the U.S.—find events near you and join in person.

4

Leverage your network to amplify your voice. Whether it's friends, family, colleagues, professionals, or community leaders—anyone you can think of—talk to them about the critical importance of abortion rights for people's lives and futures. Make full use of your social media accounts by posting your own content or sharing posts and hashtags like #AbortionIsEssential.

5

Learn more about abortion laws in your state. Click on the interactive section of the Human Rights Center's official website: "What if Roe Fell," to learn about abortion laws in your state and others. Understand which states support abortion access and which oppose it.

6

Clarify your state legislators' positions on abortion rights. Do you know the stance of your local, state, and federal lawmakers on abortion rights? Call or email them to ask about their position, and urge them to take immediate action in support of abortion rights and to advance abortion-related legislation.

7

Pass the Women's Health Protection Act (WHPA) to propose federal protection for abortion rights in Congress. Congress has the power and responsibility to immediately act to protect abortion rights across states. Even though the U.S. Supreme Court overturned Roe v. Wade, the Women's Health Protection Act would safeguard women's access to abortion from state-level abortion bans.


The U.S. Supreme Court's decision in Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization will harm the rights of countless people seeking abortion care, especially those already facing discrimination in healthcare—women, Black people, Indigenous people, other people of color, people with disabilities, rural communities, youth, immigrants, and low-income individuals.


Supreme Court Justices Stephen Breyer, Sonia Sotomayor, and Elena Kagan stated in their dissent: " From today onward, young women of this generation will grow up with far fewer rights than their mothers and grandmothers had… Most people refuse to even consider what life will be like after the overturning of Roe and Casey. This reality itself is a powerful indictment of the decision."
