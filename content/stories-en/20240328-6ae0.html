 NO.2 

[UK Chapter]Are you or someone you care about experiencing domestic violence?


1. [Legal Definition]

  • According to Section 1(2)–(3) of the UK Domestic Abuse Act 2021, domestic abuse refers to abusive behavior by one person toward another with whom they have a personal connection, whether a single or repeated act. This includes physical or sexual abuse, violence or threatening behavior, controlling or coercive behavior, economic control, and psychological, emotional, or other forms of abuse.
    (https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2021/17/contents)

  • Under Section 2(1), individuals who have a "personal connection" with the perpetrator include: partners or former partners aged 16 or over (including those in marriage or civil partnerships), individuals who have agreed to marry each other (regardless of whether the agreement has ended), individuals in intimate relationships, individuals who share parental responsibility for the same minor, and individuals related by blood.
    (https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2021/17/section/2)


2. [Current status of domestic violence in the UK]

  • According to the Crime Survey for England and Wales, it is estimated that in the year ending March 2023, 2.1 million people aged 16 and over (1.4 million women and 751,000 men) experienced domestic abuse. Compared to the previous year, there was no significant change in the rate of domestic abuse.
    (https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/crimeandjustice/bulletins/domesticabuseinenglandandwalesoverview/november2023  )

  • According to the annual report released in early 2023 by the UK women's support organization Women's Aid, among women who suffered domestic violence:

    • Most women (62.0%) had children, with an average of 1.3 children per service user.

    • Over a quarter of survivors (28.7%) reported having a disability, of whom 56.0% had a mental health disability and 22.3% had a physical disability.

    • The duration of abuse ranged from 0 to 66 years, with an average of 6 years and 1 month.

    • 4,611 women (12.1%) were not UK nationals, of whom 30% were subject to a No Recourse to Public Funds (NRPF) condition.
      (      https://www.wo mensaid.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Annual-Audit-2023-Briefing-FINAL-1.pdf)

  • Police in England and Wales receive over 100 calls related to domestic abuse every hour.
    (HMIC, 2015, https://www.justiceinspectorates.gov.uk/hmicfrs/wp-content/uploads/increasingly-everyones-business-domestic-abuse-progress-report.pdf )


3. Common types

  • In the year ending March 2022, among callers to the national domestic abuse helpline, 82.3% had experienced psychological or emotional abuse; 74.4% had experienced controlling behavior; and 56.4% had experienced threats or intimidation. (Office for National Statistics (ONS), released 7 September 2022, ONS website, article, Domestic abuse victim services, England and Wales: 2022)

[UK Chapter]How to seek legal protection?


1. [Emergency measures]

1.1 Emergency hotline:

  • In an emergency, dial 999; if you are in danger and cannot speak on the phone, dial 999 and follow the operator's instructions—respond by coughing or tapping the handset, or press 55 after the call connects.

  • Hotline for advice: Women's Aid:
    https://www.womensaid.org.uk/domestic-abuse-directory/ 



1.2 Preserving evidence:

  • According to Sections 9 and 10 of the UK Criminal Justice Act 1967 (CJA), evidence refers to information that can be provided through written statements or formal admissions. The Criminal Procedure Rules (http://www.justice.gov.uk/courts/procedure-rules/criminal/rulesmenu-2015) provide detailed provisions for Sections 9 and 10 of the CJA.

  • Types of evidence include:

    • Testimonial evidence (written witness statements; expert evidence)

    • Real evidence

    • Hearsay evidence

    • Confessions

    • Documentary evidence

    • Business records

  • For evidence related to domestic violence, you may request documentation from the following entities:

    • Courts

    • Police

    • Multi-agency risk assessment conference (MARAC)

    • Social services

    • Health professionals, such as doctors, nurses, midwives, psychologists, or health advisors

    • Shelter organizations

    • Domestic violence support services

    • Banks, such as credit card accounts, loan documents, and statements

    • Employers, or educational or training institutions

    • Any provider of benefits you receive


2. [Personal protection orders (Protective Order) ]

2.1 Introduction

  • A personal protection order is a legal document issued by a court or police to protect victims, designed to prevent the abuser from approaching or harassing the protected person and their residence.


2.2 Application types, procedures, and legal effects

(1) Types of personal protection orders:

  • Non-Molestation Orders: A civil court order that prohibits someone from contacting or approaching you. Breaching this order is a criminal offense.

    • Applicable situations: The person has been violent toward you or your child; threatens to use violence against you or your child; attempts to communicate with you or your child (except through a lawyer or other specific means); harasses you or your child by going to certain places (e.g., your home, workplace, or school).

    • Application body: When someone needs immediate protection from a partner, former partner, or family member, they can apply to the court.

    • Legal consequences: A non-molestation order is an injunction specifying what the other party must not do. If they ignore the order and do something prohibited, it is a criminal offense, and the police can arrest them; they may face imprisonment.

  • Prohibitive Steps Order: An order preventing a parent from removing a child.

    • Applicable situations: If you are concerned that a parent may try to remove the child from your care, you can apply to the court for an emergency Prohibitive Steps Order. The injunction will prevent one or both parents from removing the child from the other's care and/or from England and Wales.

    • Application body: Court

  • Occupation Order: An order preventing someone from living in or entering your property and surrounding areas.

    • Applicable situations: You can apply for this order if you own the property, or if you are a tenant or joint tenant. If you live in someone else's home, you can also apply, but only for a short-term order.

    • Application body: Court

    • Legal consequences: The order carries arrest powers, meaning police can arrest anyone who engages in prohibited behavior under the order. If the person breaches the injunction, they can be brought back to court, where a judge will decide the next steps.

  • Domestic Violence Protection Notice (DVPN)

    • Overview: An order applied for by the police to prevent the perpetrator from within 48 hours contacting or approaching you.

    • Applicable conditions: When the police have reasonable grounds to believe that the perpetrator has committed violence against a "related person," or threatened to commit violence; and it is necessary to issue a DVPN to protect the "related person." (Section 24 of the Crime and Security Act 2010)

    • Applying authority: Police

    • Legal consequences: If a police officer has reasonable grounds to believe that a person has breached a DVPO, the officer may arrest the person without a warrant. Under Section 29(1) of the Crime and Security Act 2010, there is a duty to remand the person who breached the order and bring them before a magistrates' court within 24 hours of the arrest. Under Section 63 of the Magistrates' Court Act 1980 (MCA), breaching a DVPO constitutes civil contempt of court and is treated as a breach of a civil order. Section 57A of the MCA allows civil proceedings to be transferred to another magistrates' court.

  • Domestic Violence Protection Order (DVPO):

    • Overview: An order applied for by the police from the court to prevent the perpetrator from 14 to 28 days after the order is issued, from contacting or approaching you. It may also restrict the perpetrator during this period from evicting the protected person from their home, prohibit the perpetrator from entering the protected person's home or its vicinity, or require the perpetrator to leave the protected person's home.

    • Applicable conditions: After a DVPN has been issued, a police officer must, within 48 hours, apply to the magistrates' court for a Domestic Violence Protection Order (DVPO). After weighing all possibilities, if the court is satisfied that the person has committed violence against a "related person" or threatened to commit violence, and considers it necessary to make a DVPO. (Section 27 of the Crime and Security Act 2010)

    • Applying authority: Magistrates' Court

    • Legal consequences: If a police officer has reasonable grounds to believe that a person has breached a DVPO, then the officer may arrest the person without a warrant . Under Section 29(1) of the Crime and Security Act 2010, there is a duty to remand the person who breached the order and bring them before a magistrates' court within 24 hours of the arrest. Under Section 63 of the Magistrates' Court Act 1980 (MCA), breaching a DVPO constitutes civil contempt of court and is treated as a breach of a civil order. Section 57A of the MCA allows civil proceedings to be transferred to another magistrates' court.

  • Emergency Protection Order (EPO): An order to protect children from immediate harm.

    • Applicable circumstances: An Emergency Protection Order (EPO) is an order designed to protect a child (or children) from physical, mental, or emotional harm, where urgent action is required. This application requires evidence of ongoing or imminent risk of harm.

    • Applying authority: Family Court

    • Legal consequences: Breaching the order will result in arrest.

3. [Shelter]

3.1 Introduction

  • If you have left your home due to domestic violence and do not wish to return, under Part 7 of the Housing Act 1996 and the Homelessness Act 2002, local authorities have a duty to assist you.


3.2 Eligibility criteria

  • Conditions for receiving housing assistance:

    • Involuntarily homeless: If someone has used or threatened to use violence against you, and you were forced to leave your home due to domestic violence, you will be considered involuntarily homeless.

    • Priority need: You are pregnant; you have dependent children living with you; you are vulnerable due to mental illness or disability; or you became homeless due to an emergency such as flood, fire, or disaster.

    • Meet other qualifying conditions


3.3 Application procedure

  • Applying authority: Contact your local homeless persons unit (HPU) or the housing department of the local authority, which will have a duty to investigate your case.

  • If the local authority has reasonable grounds to believe that you are homeless or threatened with homelessness, have a priority need (e.g., you are vulnerable due to domestic violence), and meet the eligibility criteria, they must immediately provide you with temporary accommodation until they complete their investigation and notify you of their decision in writing.


3.5 Legal basis

  • Homelessness Code of Guidance 2006
    (https://www.gov.uk/guidance/homelessness-code-of-guidance-for-local-authorities)

  • Homelessness Act 2002
    (https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2002/7/contents)

  • Housing Act 1996 (https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/52/contents)


4. [Free legal aid for shelter]

4.1 Introduction

  • Legal aid is a publicly funded scheme to help cover the costs of legal advice, representation in court, and family mediation. It is administered in England and Wales by the Legal Aid Agency. Civil legal aid is provided separately from criminal legal aid, and eligibility assessments differ. If there is evidence that you and your children have suffered domestic violence and cannot afford legal costs, you may apply for legal aid. It is not necessary to provide evidence before speaking with a legal aid lawyer or Civil Legal Advice (CLA), but CLA will need to see evidence to decide whether to grant free legal aid.


4.2 Eligibility criteria

  • Financial assessment must meet the requirements for legal aid. The financial resources of both the applicant and their partner will be assessed (PDF). However, if the partner has opposing interests in the dispute (e.g., in marital disputes), or if the applicant and partner are separated and the separation may be permanent, the partner's financial situation will not be included. If a person applies for legal representation relating to rights of occupation due to domestic violence, the eligibility limits on gross income, disposable income, and disposable capital may be waived. These rights include the right to occupy the marital or cohabiting home. In cases of female genital mutilation or forced marriage, eligibility limits may also not apply if seeking representation for an injunction or other protective order, or if the order has been breached.

  • The case must fall within the scope of legal aid. Domestic violence is within the scope of legal aid; however, apart from passing the income and capital test, legal aid is only available if the case meets the requirements of the "merits criteria." There are various general and specific tests to assess merits. Additional merits criteria that may need to be determined, depending on the type and circumstances of the case, include:

    • The likelihood of achieving a successful outcome at trial or other final hearing—the "prospects of success test."

    • The potential benefit of providing legal services, and whether this justifies the likely costs such that a reasonable private payer would be willing to initiate and continue the proceedings—the "reasonable private payer test."

    • The potential benefit of legal proceedings to the individual, and whether these benefits justify the likely costs—the "proportionality test."

 

4.3 Application procedure

  • Use the website (https://www.gov.uk/legal-aid) to assess eligibility. If eligible, apply directly to Civil Legal Advice (CLA) (https://www.gov.uk/civil-legal-advice).


4.4 Legal basis

  • The Civil Legal Aid (Financial Resources and Payment for Services) Regulations 2013 (https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/480/contents)

  • The Civil Legal Aid (Merits Criteria) Regulations 2013 (https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/104/contents)


5. [Pursuing criminal liability of the perpetrator]

5.1 Introduction

  • Although domestic violence and abuse are not specific criminal offences under the new legislation, perpetrators may have committed other criminal offences. Relevant offences may include, but are not limited to, controlling or coercive behaviour, harassment, stalking, criminal damage, assault, sexual assault, rape, and murder.


5.2 Application procedure—Offences the perpetrator may be charged with

  • Controlling or coercive behaviour in an intimate or family relationship

    • Under Section 76 of the Serious Crime Act 2015, when the perpetrator repeatedly or continuously engages in controlling or coercive behaviour towards the victim, causing serious adverse effects (severe alarm or distress).

      (https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/9/section/76)

  • Harassment

    • Under Sections 2, 4, and 7(2) of the Protection from Harassment Act 1997, when A's conduct causes B alarm or distress and places B in fear of violence. This constitutes harassment. (A and B may each refer to more than one person) (https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1997/40/contents)

  • Stalking

    • Under Sections 2A and 4A of the Protection from Harassment Act 1997, examples of stalking include A following, observing, or monitoring B, or forcing contact with the victim through any means (including social media). The offence is more serious if it causes the victim to believe their freedom is restricted, or causes severe alarm and distress. (https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1997/40/contents)

  • Forced Marriage

    • Under Sections 120–121 of the Anti-social Behaviour, Crime and Policing Act 2014, when a person uses violence, threats, or any other form of coercion without reasonable excuse to cause another person to enter into a marriage, it constitutes forced marriage. (https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2014/12/section/121/enacted)

  • Rape

    • Under Section 1 of the Sexual Offences Act 2003, when A intentionally penetrates B's vagina, anus, or mouth with their penis without B's consent, and A does not believe that B consents to the act. (https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/42/section/1)

  • Assault by Penetration

    • Under Sections 2–3 of the Sexual Offences Act 2003, when A intentionally touches, or inserts any part of their body or any other object into B's vagina, anus, or mouth without B's consent, and A does not believe that B consents to the act. (https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/42/part/1/crossheading/assault)

  • Sharing or threatening to share intimate photographs or films with intent to cause distress

    • Under Section 66B of the Sexual Offences Act 2003 and Section 188 of the Online Safety Act 2023, when A shares or threatens to share a photograph or film showing B in an intimate state without B's consent, and A has no reasonable belief that B consents to the act. (https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/section/188)

  • Strangulation or suffocation

    • Under Section 75A of the Serious Crime Act 2015, when A intentionally strangles B or engages in other conduct that impairs B's breathing.

      (https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2021/17/section/70)


5.3 Reporting the crime

(1) Call the police or go to a police station

  • In an emergency, dial 999. In non-emergency situations, victims may dial 101 or go to a police station to report. In emergencies and where reasonable, police may use their authority to intervene, issue warnings, or charge the perpetrator without the victim's consent.

(2) Initial Report

  • When domestic violence is reported to the police, they will determine whether the perpetrator's actions constitute a criminal offence, and if so, initiate an investigation. You will be asked to provide an initial statement and answer questions to assess whether you are in danger. If you report the incident in person at a police station, the police will issue a crime reference number for record-keeping, which helps with police coordination and may be used in subsequent legal proceedings. If you report by phone, the police will also provide you with a "CAD" or "ICAD" number.

  • At this stage, the restraining orders mentioned above may also be issued.

(3) Recording a witness statement

  • You will be required to give a recorded witness statement, primarily detailing your experience as a victim. You will be asked to read and confirm the accuracy of the statement before signing it. Alternatively, you may record your evidence via an "Achieving Best Evidence (ABE)" video, and provide a written signature to confirm its accuracy. The police will make every effort to provide you with the opportunity to communicate in your first language to ensure your statements and evidence are accurately received. When giving your statement, you may have a companion or support person present; although they may not be allowed in the room during recording, they can wait outside.

  • When giving your statement, it is advisable to provide any evidence of abuse you currently have (such as photos of injuries, harassing messages, medical reports, etc.). You should also keep receipts for medical visits or property repairs, as these may be used later in court when compensation is determined.

(4) Victim Personal Statement (VPS)

  • After recording your witness statement, the police will ask whether you wish to provide a Victim Personal Statement, describing the impact the abuse has had on you (including physical, emotional, and financial effects). This VPS may be used by the judge during sentencing.

(5) Investigation and prosecution decision

  • The police will then investigate the allegations; the duration varies and complex cases may take longer. During the investigation, the police are obligated to inform you of progress.

  • Once the investigation is complete, the police will submit the evidence to the Crown Prosecution Service (CPS) and discuss whether to prosecute the perpetrator. To meet prosecution standards, both "evidence" and "public interest" requirements must be satisfied. The CPS's decision to prosecute does not necessarily require the victim's support. If the CPS determines there is sufficient evidence to proceed, prosecution will continue even without the witness's cooperation.

(6) Court proceedings

  • Once the CPS decides to prosecute, the perpetrator will first appear in a magistrates' court, and the case will begin to be heard. At this stage, you may submit a Victim Personal Statement (VPS), in which you can detail the impact of the domestic abuse on your life and express your needs.

  • Notably, Sections 65–66 of the Domestic Abuse Act 2021 explicitly state that when the perpetrator has been charged, a protection order has been issued, and the victim has evidence of domestic abuse, the court must not require the victim and perpetrator to face each other during cross-examination, to prevent significant distress to the victim. Victims may instead give evidence via video link or from behind a screen.

6. [Divorce]

6.1 Types of divorce

  • There is only one type of divorce: no-fault divorce (No-fault divorce)

    • To apply for divorce, the following requirements must be met:

      (1) You have been married for at least one year;

      (2) The relationship has broken down irretrievably;

      (3) The marriage is legally recognised in the UK (including same-sex marriages)

    • In addition to divorce, you may apply for a legal separation, which allows you to live apart without divorcing or ending a civil partnership. To obtain a legal separation, you must complete a separation application form and send it to the court. https://www.gov.uk/legal-separation


6.2 Grounds/Conditions for divorce

  • Under the Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020: a divorce application must be accompanied by a statement from the applicant confirming that the marriage has broken down irretrievably. (Although the new law does not require any of the following circumstances to be proven, the court may still consider evidence of irretrievable breakdown, and typically looks for evidence such as:

    (1) Adultery;

    (2) Unreasonable behaviour;

    (3) Desertion;

    (4) Separation for two years with consent;

    (5) Separation for five years)


6.3 Divorce procedure

To apply for divorce, meet the above conditions and submit an application to the court. Application website: www.gov.uk/apply-for-divorce

  • Sole application: this requires you to provide the respondent's usual email address and postal address. The court will use these details to inform the respondent about the divorce application and serve the divorce papers to the other party.

    • In a sole application, since the relationship has irretrievably broken down, the divorce is uncontested. However, the respondent may defend the application on the following grounds:

      (1) The court lacks jurisdiction over the case;

      (2) The marriage is invalid;

      (3) The marriage has already legally ended.

  • Joint application: both parties jointly apply for divorce


6.4 Child custody

  • When both parties agree, custody arrangements follow their mutual decision.

  • If no agreement can be reached, apply to the court for an order.

    • Child Arrangements Order: determines where your child lives; when your child spends time with each parent; and the type and frequency of contact (e.g., phone calls).

    • Specific Issue Order: decides matters such as which school the child attends or whether they should receive religious education.

    • Prohibited Steps Order: prevents one parent from making certain decisions about the child's upbringing.


6.5 Child maintenance

  • Both parents are responsible for contributing to the costs of raising their children, even if they do not see them. This duty applies until the child is under 16 (or under 20 if they are still in full-time education).

  • Maintenance:

    • Agreed between both parties

    • If no agreement is reached, the Child Maintenance Service will determine the amount. The responsibilities of the Child Maintenance Service include: calculating the amount payable; arranging payments and taking action if one parent fails to pay; resolving disputes about parentage; and attempting to locate the other parent if their whereabouts are unknown.


6.6 Spousal maintenance

  • Shared assets: pensions; property; savings; investments.

  • Agreed division: sign a financial settlement agreement, then apply to the court for approval of the agreement.

    If no agreement can be reached, apply to the court for asset division. In deciding how to divide assets, the court considers the following factors: age; earning capacity; property and money; living expenses; standard of living; financial needs and responsibilities; role in caring for the family, for example, if you were the main earner or primary caregiver; and any disability or health conditions.

[UK Section]How do domestic violence and divorce affect your visa?


1. Visa types

  • EU Settlement Scheme family permit: You may be eligible for an EU Settlement Scheme family permit to come to the UK if you are a family member of someone from the EU, Switzerland, Norway, Iceland, or Liechtenstein who settled in the UK by 31 December 2020.

    • You may also apply if you are a family member of a qualifying person in Northern Ireland.

    • Some family members of British citizens may also be eligible for a permit. (1) Geographic requirement for the permit – you must be outside the UK to apply. (2) You are from the EU, Switzerland, Norway, Iceland, or Liechtenstein and hold a valid passport or a national ID card with a biometric chip.

  • Marriage Visitor visa: To apply for a Marriage Visitor visa, you must meet the following conditions: (1) You intend to marry or register a civil partnership in the UK; (2) You intend to give notice of marriage or civil partnership in the UK; (3) You do not intend to remain in or settle in the UK after the marriage or civil partnership; (4) Other conditions (e.g., being at least 18 years old and able to support yourself financially during your stay in the UK).

    • Visa duration: up to 6 months.

  • Retained right of residence refers to situations where your right to reside in the UK continues even after certain changes in your family circumstances:

    (1) Your qualifying family member has died, provided you lived continuously in the UK with them for at least one year;

    (2) You are their child, and they have died or left the UK, but you are receiving education in the UK;

    (3) You and your qualifying family member have a child, who is receiving education in the UK, and the family member has died or left the UK;

    (4) (4) Your relationship with your family member has permanently broken down due to domestic violence or abuse, and you are already residing in the UK,. and you originally obtained residence rights through marriage or civil partnership, and the marriage or civil partnership lasted at least three years, with both partners living in the UK for at least one year during that period, or you have custody of the former family member's child, or you have been granted the right to visit the child in the UK, and the child is under 18, or you and other family members have experienced particularly difficult circumstances (for example, being a victim of domestic violence or abuse within the marriage or civil partnership).


2. Acquiring British citizenship

  • You may be eligible for British citizenship if you meet the following conditions:

    (1) You are 18 years old or over;

    (2) You are married to, or in a civil partnership with, a British citizen;

    (3) You have lived in the UK for at least three years before the date of your application. Time calculation: you must not have spent more than 270 days outside the UK in the three years before applying, and no more than 90 days outside the UK in the last 12 months.
