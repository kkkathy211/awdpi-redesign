"Most people believe because they see, only a few see because they believe."

——Ginsburg






On September 18, 2020, the White House lowered its flag to half-mast in mourning for a legendary woman—Ginsburg—the second female justice in the history of the U.S. Supreme Court, a feminist, and affectionately known by young women as the "Notorious" RBG. Today, she has become a symbolic figure, guiding women worldwide to stand on the shoulders of giants and continue fighting for the attainment, protection, and safeguarding of women's rights.


The women's rights movement has a long history. In 1848, at the first women's rights convention, 68 women and 32 men signed the Declaration of Sentiments, borrowing the format of the Declaration of Independence to propose the idea of "all men and women are created equal." In 1920, the Nineteenth Amendment to the Constitution recognized women's right to vote; in 1972, Nixon signed Title IX of the Education Amendments, banning gender discrimination in education; in 1996, the ruling in United States v. Virginia required the Virginia Military Institute to admit women... As Ginsburg once said: "Social change requires gradual accumulation and incremental progress. Real, sustainable change happens step by step."

Ginsburg was born in 1933 in Brooklyn, New York, a poor and chaotic neighborhood. From childhood, her mother taught her to hold herself to the standard of a lady and to remain independent above all. Being a lady meant holding firm to one's beliefs, maintaining self-respect, patiently educating others, and not allowing negative emotions to take over the mind; remaining independent meant that while it would be wonderful to find true love and spend one's life with that person, one must first learn to stand on one's own. This shaped Ginsburg's entire life. Although she faced numerous injustices—graduating first in her class yet being rejected by law firms and courts due to her gender, and later being fired for pregnancy—she continued tirelessly advancing the women's rights movement. As Ginsburg said: "I don't ask for special favors based on my gender. My only request of my male counterparts is to give women basic respect."

While studying at Cornell University, Ginsburg met Martin, the soulmate with whom she would spend her life. While others praised only her beauty, Martin was the first man who truly appreciated her intellect. Later, they both entered Harvard Law School, supporting each other through life. At every major crossroads, Martin gave Ginsburg respect, support, and encouragement—never wavering.

In 1959, Ginsburg graduated first in her class and wanted to become a lawyer, but no law firm would hire her because she was a woman. Later, she became a law professor at a university, advancing many feminist causes. In 1972, she became the first woman to receive tenure at Columbia University.


Ginsburg rose to prominence, her presence beginning to appear in the highest judicial hall of the United States—the Supreme Court. As a lawyer, she argued six cases before the U.S. Supreme Court to secure women's rights, including Reed v. Reed, a landmark case in U.S. history. It was the first time the U.S. Supreme Court applied the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment of the U.S. Constitution to protect women's rights.


In her brief for Reed v. Reed, Ginsburg wrote: "Women may seem to be carefully protected on a pedestal, but upon closer inspection, they are locked in a cage disguised as a pedestal... Patriarchal society treats women as second-class citizens... No person should be disadvantaged under the law because of their biological characteristics."

In the case of Sprogis v. United Air Lines, female employees of General Electric sued the company because GE required all female employees to resign immediately upon marriage, and their health insurance excluded pregnancy coverage. In Ginsburg's brief, she wrote: "Personal development and equal opportunity should not be limited by stereotypes... Rules that force the dismissal of pregnant women are barriers to women's advancement... forcing them to accept a subordinate, dependent status in society." In 1978, the U.S. Congress passed the Pregnancy Discrimination Act, clearly stating that if an employer treats pregnant employees differently, it would be considered discrimination against women.

As more and more cases advancing women's rights changed the law, Ginsburg's reputation grew. In 1993, she became the second female Supreme Court Justice in U.S. history. In 2015, she was named one of the 100 Most Influential People in the World by TIME magazine.


As Ginsburg said: "Don't let them make you angry. Rise above. Reach for the stars." As a woman with unshakable inner strength, she taught us: "For both men and women, the first step to gaining power is to be seen and to demonstrate one's talents. When women gain power, the barriers between men and women will gradually disappear. When society sees women's achievements, more women will step forward, and our society will become more equal." She taught us: "Women should fight for the causes we care about—not alone, but by finding ways to bring like-minded people to join us in the struggle." She encouraged women to become decision-makers in their own lives: "Women should be part of every decision. Women should not be the exception."

Even when facing defeat, Ginsburg's dissenting opinions were always calm, reasoned, and well-argued. As she said: "No matter what, hope remains. If you lose today, there's still hope tomorrow."
