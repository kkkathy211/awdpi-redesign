International Menstrual Day

Today is International Menstrual Day.



Menstruation is a phenomenon that seems "everyday," yet has long been deliberately avoided. It occurs in the bodies of nearly half the world's population, yet has rarely been documented throughout history. We are accustomed to its presence, but may not truly understand it. 


Its name is whispered over within families, "politely" omitted in public spaces, and replaced in language with various euphemisms. It has been regarded in some cultures as a symbol of female impurity, and in contemporary society, it is expected to "disappear cleanly"—— unseen, unmentioned, and leaving no trace. 


Menstruation has never been merely a physiological phenomenon. Surrounding it is an entire complex network of how culture shapes perception, how technology intervenes in the body, and how specific systems allocate resources and power.


The history of menstruation is not merely the development history of hygiene products, but also a history of women striving to understand and define themselves, and of humanity continuously breaking disciplinary norms and reshaping narratives about the body.


 The Evolution of Menstrual Products 

From "managing physiology" to "commercial products"

For most of human history, menstruation has faced the "how to be managed" dilemma.



In ancient societies, menstruation was not technologized, but integrated into daily life as an invisible, mundane labor—including washing, drying, and storing—often borne solely by women.


Ancient Egyptian women rolled soft papyrus into tampon-like forms; ancient Greeks commonly used wool; Native Americans used moss or animal skins; in East Asia, cloth strips and grass paper were more common. These early "hygiene products" shared some similarities: they were easily accessible and reusable, but often insufficiently clean and hygienic.


From the late 19th to the early 20th century, with industrialization, menstruation gradually entered the commodity system.


Around 1897, commercial sanitary pads first appeared, but due to the strong "shame culture" of the time, such products failed to enter the market and instead remained in a state of secretive, cautious circulation. It was not until after World War I, when fibrous materials were repurposed for hygiene product manufacturing, that disposable sanitary products gradually became widespread and entered broader consumer markets in the 1920s.


In the following decades, related technologies continued to evolve: the introduction of tampons increased freedom of movement for menstruating women; adhesive sanitary pads brought convenience and stability to daily use; and the menstrual cup, reintroduced in the late 20th century, offered women a more sustainable option.


In the 21st century, as menstrual products have become increasingly diverse, scientific and technological integration has further enhanced women's control over their bodies. Digital period-tracking apps represent a timely response to women's more nuanced needs.


Yet, in the process of menstrual product iteration, a more complex issue has gradually emerged: continuous technological advancement does not necessarily mean synchronous updates in social attitudes.


The Construction of Menstrual Culture

From "taboo" to "invisibility"

In many ancient cultures, menstruation was often assigned "impurity" or "uncleanness" meanings.



In Nepal, a traditional taboo known as "Chhaupadi" has long existed: women during menstruation or after childbirth are required to leave their homes and live in temporary mud huts or sheds. They are forbidden from touching children, men, or water sources, and their diets are strictly restricted; otherwise, they are believed to bring misfortune or health risks to their families.


However, this "isolation" often means extreme insecurity. In reality, there have been multiple cases of women dying in these huts from suffocation, fire, pneumonia, or animal attacks (such as snake bites), and they may also face the risk of sexual violence.


Although Nepal legally banned Chhaupadi in 2005, the practice still continues covertly in some areas.


Similar cultural practices exist in India.


In many regions of India, menstruation is still regarded as a taboo that cannot be openly discussed. A large number of young girls lack any knowledge when they first menstruate, forced to face it alone in confusion and pain. According to statistics, only about 12% of women in India can access disposable menstrual products. In some areas, used sanitary pads must be collected and disposed of, and this cleaning work is often carried out by lower-caste groups.


Within certain Hindu cultural contexts, menstruating women may also be required to enter a state of isolation, prohibited from entering kitchens or participating in daily activities, and must undergo a ritual bath after menstruation to regain their "pure" status. This social mechanism that marks the female body as "impure" and "needing management" invisibly contributes to the maintenance of daily order and gender boundaries. 


In modern society, this taboo has not truly disappeared, but instead continues in more concealed ways, gradually forming a cultural habit associated with "shame" and "invisibility." Menstrual blood is almost never directly shown in menstrual product advertisements, but instead replaced with blue liquid; the term "menstruation" is often substituted with more euphemistic expressions like "women's hygiene."


A 2016 study by Clue and the International Women's Health Coalition found over 5,000 euphemisms or slang terms for menstruation worldwide. In different languages, people avoid directly naming this physiological phenomenon in extremely diverse and even humorous ways—from "red alert" and "auntie's visit" (dàiyímā) to "bloody Mary," among countless others.


Menstruation is allowed to exist, but only on the condition that it is managed "cleanly" enough—neither seen nor spoken of.


It was not until recent decades that "menstrual shame" began to loosen. 


With rising awareness of public health and gender equality,


"menstrual health" has gradually entered the public agenda. On social media, discussions around menstruation have become more direct, from personal sharing#PeriodPositive to various de-stigmatization campaigns, people are beginning to reframe menstruation—not as a physiological phenomenon to be concealed, but as a natural part of bodily function.


However, this shift in perception has not been equally accepted across all regions, and there are clear regional differences in the promotion of menstrual products and the rejection of "menstrual stigma."


Menstrual Poverty

The hidden economic burden and girls dropping out of school

In some poorer developing countries, due to weak infrastructure, clean water and safe toilets are often insufficient, and the supply of hygiene products faces challenges.



This reality causes many girls to struggle to attend school normally during menstruation, and some are even forced to drop out. A 2014 United Nations report indicated that in sub-Saharan Africa, approximately one in ten girls miss school due to menstruation-related issues, with some losing 20%educational opportunities as a result.


In Kenya, a 14-year-old girl, experiencing her first period in class without any hygiene products to manage the flow, was subjected to humiliating treatment by classmates and later died by suicide at home. Although Kenya passed a law in 2017 requiring free sanitary pads for all female students, implementation still faces financial and institutional barriers, resulting in a clear gap between policy and reality. 


In some developed countries, "menstrual poverty" also exists. For low-income individuals, the ongoing purchase of hygiene products is a long-term and heavy economic burden. Some are forced to extend the use of hygiene products or resort to alternative materials, leading to additional health risks.


Thus, menstrual issues have long transcended the scope of "personal hygiene" and have become a "public policy" issue.


In China, discussions around reducing taxes on sanitary pads, emergency supplies in public spaces, and menstrual protection for female healthcare workers continue to emerge. In 2021, National People's Congress representative Wang Zuoying proposed providing free menstrual products to specific female groups. Public debates on topics such as "loose-pack sanitary pads," "length standards," and "whether high-speed trains should provide sanitary pads" are continuously pushing this issue into the institutional sphere.


These measures are not merely adjustments to welfare policies, but also a reshaping of mainstream understanding. They signify that menstruation should no longer be seen as a negligible issue within the private sphere, but as a reality linked to resource distribution and social structural inequality.


Rethinking Menstruation

Challenging the definition of the "normal body"

As technology advances, culture gradually loosens, and policy begins to intervene, we might ask one step further: What is the core of the menstrual issue? 


If we treat it merely as a "hygiene issue," then seemingly, continuously improving products and supporting facilities would be sufficient. But reality shows this is far from enough.


The development of menstrual products is not a purely neutral technological progress; in actual use, it continuously shapes how bodies are seen and how people imagine the "normal body."


The widespread use of disposable hygiene products, while increasing convenience, also subtly reinforces an expectation: menstruation should be completely hidden, leaving no trace, and not affecting women's daily social interactions or outward appearance.


Yet, who gets to control the discourse about women's bodies? Who decides what is "clean"? What is "appropriate"? What bodily states are considered "normal"? Some judgments that seem natural are in fact deeply shaped by the cultural contexts of different countries and regions, and have become closely entangled with medical discourses and commercial market operations over time. In this process, certain menstrual experiences have been normalized, while others have been marginalized—or erased altogether.



In recent years, amid ongoing discussions about menstruation, a new term has begun to emerge in academic and public discourse: menstrual justice. 


This concept seeks to reframe menstruation within the framework of social equity. It emphasizes that menstruation is not only about physical health, but also directly linked to whether educational opportunities are equal, whether information is fully accessible, whether social resources are sufficient, and whether individuals can participate in public life with dignity and autonomy, free from the oppression of shame.


Therefore, whether a woman can experience menstruation with dignity and ease is not merely a matter of personal experience, but a reflection of how social structures allocate resources and recognize differences.


From this perspective, discussions about menstruation are no longer limited to "hygiene issues" or "women's issues," but point instead to a more fundamental question: how a society understands bodily differences among gender groups, how it organizes institutions and allocates resources based on these differences, and ultimately, how it defines what is "normal." 


When a society can openly confront menstruation, what changes is never just our attitude toward women's bodies, but our understanding of "human beings" themselves.
