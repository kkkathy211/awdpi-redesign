Some try to reframe the narrative of gender, carving out an untouched sanctuary beyond the walls guarded by traditionalists; some attempt to trace back the many strands of women's history, hoping to awaken the dormant cocoon so it may break through the soil once more; some remain anchored in the present, capturing the possibilities of womanhood in the minutiae of individual lives. *Your Summer Was Good, Wasn't It?* is Kim Ae-ran's attempt at this—eight stories, drawing infinitely close to the truth of lived experience through fiction, returning to the voices that belong to women amid the noise.


01

Who turns the "axis of a day"?

"The position of the rice cooker's light is like the distance between emptiness and appetite—seemingly near yet distant, faintly visible. It's also like the edge of a planet everyone treads upon but can never embrace in its entirety—that is the measure of hunger."

The protagonist of *The Axis of a Day* is Ms. Kkwiok, a cleaning worker at the airport. Her irresponsible husband died early, and her only son was imprisoned for theft and violence. Overwhelmed by stress, Ms. Kkwiok began losing her hair—though still middle-aged, her scalp is nearly bald. She is forced to endure extra scrutiny, forced to live in the gaps between sighs, pity, and shock. Ms. Kkwiok's quality of life is as thin as her hair: wearing a windbreaker bought from an outlet store, carrying a counterfeit Gucci handbag, she cleans the airport and also works part-time distributing flyers. Even so, she carries a fervor so heavy it feels weightless: cooking festive foods for different holidays, diligently cleaning every stain in the airport restrooms, cutting out healthy, uplifting news clippings from the newspaper to send to her son.



Yet even as Ms. Kkwiok works so hard, when she offers to cover for Madam Pungpyeong, the department head does not express gratitude—instead, he turns pale and trembles at the sight of her bald head. Even as Ms. Kkwiok has fulfilled her duty raising her son, he feels neither guilt nor gratitude; the first letter he sends his mother contains only one line—"Mom, buy me some snacks." The traces Ms. Kkwiok strives to leave are erased entirely; the helplessness of being ignored spills from the pages, spreading to every reader.


Kim Ae-ran concludes the story with a montage-like sequence:


It's past ten at night, and the world is darker than before. A young Bangladeshi man who has just arrived in Korea doesn't know what day it is; he stares wide-eyed at a scene he's seeing for the first time. A Vietnamese woman, convinced her groom is a good man, scans the crowd for the Korean man who will come to meet her. Flight after flight arrives, yet fewer and fewer people remain. A weary pilot tries not to think about what it might mean that a button fell off his uniform. A middle-aged man on a business trip, trying to ease his guilt over an affair, signs his credit card receipt at the duty-free shop after buying a scarf for his wife. A listless elementary school student does homework while looking up the meaning of the proverb, "Dress as if for a wedding, eat as if for Chuseok." In the center of the red-light district, a Pakistani man stands with arms raised like someone punished, holding a wooden sign that reads "Army Hotpot." Thousands of aviation lights illuminating takeoff and landing zones shimmer like a galaxy across the vast runway. A woman with hollow eyes, wearing a headscarf, wipes the floor of the airport restroom. On this night, an elementary school student, upset that his mother has to work at the supermarket on Chuseok, goes to the supermarket's corporate website and leaves a message: "18, A Supermarket, there will be a full-day power outage tomorrow. Drop dead." Near the security checkpoint, phrases like "Sayonara," "Taogeunssi," "Good-bye," "Take care," "See you," "I'll call you," "I'll write to you," "Go back," and "Don't cry" echo intermittently. Tomorrow is Chuseok.


The world keeps turning ceaselessly, yet Ms. Kkwiok remains trapped in a filthy, chaotic restroom, unable to escape the weight of her labor, facing alone the bewilderment, anxiety, and desolation of turning fifty. Her tireless efforts fail to become medals of honor, while her bald head becomes a mark of shame. Countless women like Ms. Kkwiok lay the foundation for everyone's lives within narrow spaces—corners everyone has passed through but never paused to notice. At the hour when others sleep, it is women like Ms. Kkwiok who tirelessly turn the world's clock, rotating the "axis of a day." What others take for granted is a peace and stability maintained by countless women like Ms. Kkwiok, who have long been excluded from historical narratives, rendered nameless within complex and vast modern systems, erased through silent yet brutally cruel gender-based violence, becoming rootless souls in desolate wastelands, roots swaying on empty islands.*The Axis of a Day* leads us through the busy, bright Incheon Airport, arriving at that unnoticed restroom, entering the core of one woman's life, sharing with her a profound collective memory.


02

Looking back at the threshold of "thirty"

"Everyone used to joke, 'Only by passing the exam can you escape this island.' Back then, I felt you were very much like my older sister. But now, I am thirty too. In the meantime, sister, you must have lived through years impossible to summarize in a few lines, right? Just as the wind carries away the seasons, time must have taken so much from you—things easily missed, impossible to simply call 'opportunity costs,' things that still pierce with pain today; secrets and sorrows you can only bear alone if spoken aloud."

*Thirty* is the final piece in the collection. Kim Ae-ran abandons her earlier third-person perspective, instead narrating the story through a letter. The "I" in the letter initially sees herself as exceptional—she passed the entrance exam for the French department, firmly believing that every hardship in life would later become experience and wisdom. But reality is far from that, even brutally so: finding a job proves unimaginably difficult, her father suffers a car accident, her family teeters on collapse, and finally, urged on by her ex-boyfriend, she falls into the bottomless trap of a pyramid scheme. "Besides 'if you work hard, anyone can achieve their dreams,' what other phrase in today's world could become a doctrine people are willing to believe?"—the "I" still clings to a sliver of hope, yet inevitably grows numb day after day in endless deception. Selling relationships in the scheme, she in fact sells her own soul; she loses the passion of youth, loses the courage to resist a sinking fate, and even extends her poisoned hand toward a girl she once tutored—only later learning that the girl, tormented by massive debt and shattered relationships, attempted suicide and, though her life was saved, has become a vegetative patient.

At first, the tone of the letter is calm, emotions subdued, but as the "I" reviews the process of losing faith, she can no longer resist the inner questioning and the helpless sense of being adrift:


Sister, I don't know what to do. I really want to ask someone, what should I do? Now, I have no one around me. Sister, what can I do? What should I do? I want to visit her in the hospital, but I don't have the courage. Lately, I've thought countless times: how much better it would have been if she hadn't reached out to me then. How much better if I hadn't worked at that academy. No—if I, the twenty-year-old me, hadn't been someone she liked, how much better that would have been. Sister, what should I do from now on? I don't know what kind of person I'll be at forty or sixty, don't know what words I'll rely on, what kind of trust I'll endure. It's not the circumstances that change, but the person, right? Then, what prevents a person from changing themselves? You wrote this at the end of your postcard, didn't you? Time flows by, leaving the past behind. Today, which will inevitably become the past, stands blankly before me at this very moment.

*Thirty* is an outpouring from afar, yet also an inward reflection—revealing Kim Ae-ran's deep sympathy and empathy for women's experiences of growing up. The self-narration is not an attempt to absolve guilt, but a courage to face life's vanity and humiliation, struggle and collapse. The neon lights of Seoul at dawn, the narrow, dark cubicles of the hagwon, the diligent and innocent days at university, the darkest moment of falling into the pyramid scheme, the conflict and remorse of betraying trust—these are indelible marks of life, leaving the most authentic scenes etched into aging skin and advancing years.

Kim Ae-ran is shaping an independent female narrative—not merely breaking free from the traditional path of self-objectification or flatly imitating male experiences—but carving out a linguistic space uniquely belonging to women, where trauma is confessed and self-deconstruction unfolds amid hesitation and uncertainty. We believe women's voices possess astonishing resilience, believe women's experiences can radiate infinite light through dark voids, believe the frustrations surrounding you and me are the foundation for rebuilding..


Finally, let us conclude with Kim Ae-ran's words from the book:

 

I don't know what has left me,

I want to name it.

String together a few long sentences,

that is a name no one can call out.

Even if you read it all the way through, you won't know,

you can only try reading it once more.
