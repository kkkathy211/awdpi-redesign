At six months old, her hometown was bombed by rocket fire.


The scars left by that war later became the reference points by which she calculated her age.


July 2021, Kabul, Afghanistan. Women took to the streets to protest a clause issued by the Taliban's interim cabinet banning "any sport that might expose women's skin." (Image source: Reuters)


Thirty-four-year-old Afghan woman Hadia Haydari holds a master's degree in economics from Kabul University. Before the Taliban regained power, she worked as a local governance expert in the presidential palace. On August 15, 2021, from that day onward, she—like many Afghan women—saw her right to work, to move freely, and to speak publicly stripped away one by one. Also lost was the nation's ability to imagine itself.





September 2024

Chinese journalist Hong Weilin contacted Hadia through Zan Times, an Afghan women's media organization, after reading her personal essays published there. After multiple exchanges, Hong compiled Hadia's fragmented writings into a near-complete manuscript and published it on a domestic WeChat public account, generating nearly 400,000 views. After the article was published, four readers donated $1,001 to Hadia through the platform, which became the seed money for her family's escape to Pakistan.


August 2025

Hadia's writings were compiled and published—Letters from an Afghan Woman, translated directly from Persian into Chinese, becoming the world's first edition.



This book can be read both as a record of Afghan women's conditions and as a literary work about how war seeps into everyday life. More importantly, in this book, Afghan women are no longer merely objects of description, but become writers themselves—this may well be the starting point for understanding the book.




Who is defining Afghanistan's "modernity"?


On the cover of Letters from an Afghan Woman, the words "civilizational regression" are printed—but whose context of "modernity" is being referenced here?


When people think of Afghan women, many recall a set of classic images circulating online: female students in miniskirts and sunglasses walking the streets of 1970s Kabul, contrasted with veiled figures after 2021. This "before and after" narrative has become popular, but it contains clear factual distortions.

Image source: Twitter


In 1979, Afghanistan's national adult literacy rate was only 18%. The widely circulated online images mostly depict a small urban elite, not the reality of the vast majority of rural Afghan women. In other words, the "modern Afghanistan" so frequently romanticized online never truly existed nationwide, nor was it ever completely taken away.


What people denounce is in fact a set of deliberately selected and manipulated materials, fabricated to support a certain narrative.


Zygmunt Bauman said that the core of modernity is the relentless pursuit of order—the impulse to name, classify, and organize chaos into governable structures.


But in a country like Afghanistan, long entangled in external forces, modernization has never been a linear, internally generated process. Since the 20th century, the country has attempted educational and judicial reforms multiple times, only to face repeated setbacks. Then, from the Soviet invasion in 1978 to the U.S. military withdrawal in 2021, Afghanistan repeatedly served as a backdrop for Soviet-American rivalry. Soviet-style secular socialist modernity, American-style liberal modernity, and the Taliban's own conservative faction reinventing an "anti-modern modernity" through extremism—all these ideologies have existed on this land.


All these projects, under the banner of "liberation" or "protection," used the condition of Afghan women as their most visible showcase.


Therefore, rather than simply understanding the present as "regression," it is better to recognize that the so-called "modern Afghanistan" was never stable to begin with, nor did it ever truly encompass the entire society.


In 2024, the Taliban issued decrees strictly restricting women's behavior in public spaces. A woman's voice in public became a "shameful part," and women fully wrapped in shawls, forbidden to make any sound in public, became living shadows.

Image source: BBC




This may not be the kind of women's narrative you expect

But at least she has reclaimed the pen


For decades, the identity of "Afghan woman" has almost always been written by outsiders. This book, marks an Afghan woman reclaiming her own pen.


Anthropologist Lila Abu-Lughod posed a deeply uncomfortable yet crucial question in her book Do Muslim Women Need Saving? When you say you want to "save" someone, you are actually implying: save her into what kind of life?



From Lord Cromer, the British consul-general in Egypt, to French aristocratic women in Algiers, to Mrs. Bush's televised speech paving the way for war in Afghanistan, armies stood behind all these speakers. Their talk of "liberating Muslim women" was less about care and more about a rhetorical veil for relations of domination.


Hadia's writing bypasses the "ideal perfect victim" required by such narratives. The Afghan women in her writing are not ideal figures in traditional feminist stories, which disappoints many readers who came with expectations.


Some readers complain that the characters are too "love-obsessed," with plots revolving almost entirely around romance.


Yet under the shadow of "honor killings" in Afghanistan, it is often taboo for women to speak publicly about their love lives. In the book, two sisters who taught together in Kabul—after the elder sister left her post, the younger one kept her sister's salary for herself. These women are not abstract symbols, not mere silhouettes of victims, nor passive "others" waiting to be liberated.


They feel jealousy, harm each other, compete, but also show sympathy and mutual support; within the narrow cracks torn open by patriarchy and war, they make messy, unappealing, yet vivid choices.


As anthropologist Saba Mahmood reminds us: We perhaps should not default to equating "agency" with "resistance." A woman putting on a veil to get something done, choosing to forgive someone who should not be forgiven, deciding to stay in a marriage with no way out, raising her children—these can all be forms of agency, even if they do not conform to the liberal feminist script of "awakening—resistance—liberation.".


The women in Hadia's writing rarely shout slogans; instead, they calculate, endure, strategize, and compromise, and in unnoticed moments, slowly claw back their lives through the cracks of their fingers.




Throwing the letter toward the distance


This book is not written in epistolary form, yet it carries the posture of a letter: intensely private, yet addressed to the public.



In a media interview, Hadia said: "I am inseparable from writing. As long as life allows, I will write about every aspect of the 34 years I spent living in Afghanistan."


Some of the characters in her writing are based on single real-life prototypes, others are composites of several real people; but what she insists on is her right to write, not the privilege of fiction. For her, "fiction" is a thin protective layer that keeps her from being directly targeted. These words are not written for any specific, visible reader, but thrown toward a distant place that may never receive her voice.


These words are both record and protection. "Fiction" provides a buffer for reality, allowing her to keep speaking. They do not point to a definite reader, but are cast toward a distance that may respond—or may remain silent. What she faces is an audience of Chinese readers who are highly unfamiliar with her language and culture.

She writes, Human connections matter more than relations between nations. This kind of female expression does not rely on grand mechanisms, but is a simple ethical stance, based solely on the most basic human care for one another.

In a follow-up interview, Hadia said that Chinese readers' responses to the situation of Afghan women filled her with "hope," and made her believe that the condition of Afghan women is unacceptable—and that it angers women around the world.




This is not a salvation story, but a form of collaboration


Some readers criticized the book for its naive writing style, arguing that its publication value outweighs the text itself.


This critique is not without basis. The work carries the stiffness of translation and retains the rawness of a first-time writer. But precisely because of this, it avoids being polished into the kind of standardized suffering narrative that is "beautifully heartbreaking."


The very process of publishing this book constitutes a correction to the "salvation narrative." The editor later said: "The idea of 'helping her' was extinguished the moment I sent my first email… This isn't help, it's collaboration, it's commissioning."


What the editor wanted to clarify was Hadia's position. She should not be treated as a "survivor" in need of placement, but as an Afghan writer who continues to write.


Even so, writing under Taliban rule is inherently risky: she can only publish under a pen name, continuing to document from exile, while also contributing to an underground education network for Afghan women from her garden.


As the online discussion around the book's publication story fades, perhaps we should reconsider it anew. Do we have the right to define this woman, far away in another land, as an "object in need of aid," and unilaterally demand donations from her as a one-time emotional consumption?


Using royalties instead of donations to build relationships can make such connections equal, professional, and sustainable.

As of early 2026, Hadia remains stranded in Pakistan with her family, surviving on royalties and sporadic support. She continues to write for media outlets and has begun publishing under her real name. She is writing more now than she did in Kabul. In one of her recent short essays, she mentioned listening to the audiobook of Margaret Atwood's The Handmaid's Tale during cold winter nights while she was pregnant. In that piece,


she wrote: "I choose writing as my weapon."
