Half the world's population, ignored and silenced


This is a groundbreaking work of women's studies from a fresh perspective, telling the story of how half the world's population has been ignored and silenced, exposing the discrimination behind "gender neutrality" and fundamentally reshaping our understanding of gender. Invisible Women uses vast data and examples to reveal the harsh reality of systemic neglect toward women. Behind countless "gender-neutral" rules, men are often treated as the default, while women remain unseen.


"Defaulting to the male is fundamental to human social structures." In archaeology, even human bones are not spared from the assumption "unspecified means male"—despite clear skeletal features, the remains of a female Viking warrior were classified as male for over a century. In linguistics, generic masculine terms are overwhelmingly interpreted as referring to men, even overriding strong existing stereotypes: in English, the word "man" can represent all of humanity, while women, the other half of the population, are merely the derivative "woman"; in gender-inflected languages such as French, German, and Spanish, feminine forms are often grammatical variations of masculine ones—for example, a group of 100 female teachers in Spanish is called "las profesoras," but the moment one male teacher is added, the entire group suddenly becomes "los profesores." In Chinese, this would be like adding one man to a group of 100 women, and they instantly become "they"—this is the power of the male default.


This linguistic "hegemony" extends into professions. When we see job titles like doctor, CEO, leader, driver, delivery worker—terms that have ostensibly become gender-neutral—the image that first comes to mind is still male. When the word "female" is added to these ordinary professions, her expertise is inevitably questioned to some degree. We've heard endless complaints about "female drivers," yet rarely do people single out male drivers for discussion, because driving is already assumed to be a male role—even though, under this assumption and in reality, men are statistically responsible for the majority of traffic accidents.


Gender bias is not limited to language or perception; the neglect of women permeates every aspect of daily life. Men still hold overwhelming dominance in decision-making roles across fields such as urban planning, car design, and pharmaceutical research. Their reference points—the so-called "ergonomics" and "study subjects"—are also predominantly male. Female users, who make up half the consumer base, are forced to spend extra time and effort to cope with constant discomfort: air conditioning set below women's thermal comfort forces them to carry extra clothing; city designs optimized for men's work commutes, while ignoring women's needs for household-related travel, waste women's time and money, blocking their paths to personal development. Some oversights even pose life-threatening risks—cars designed based on male bodies result in women facing significantly higher risks of injury or death in traffic accidents compared to men.



Make women visible


Whenever women complain about the many inconveniences caused by such neglect, they are often met with cold mockery like "troublesome," "overly demanding," or "exaggerating." When women shiver in freezing air-conditioned rooms and ask to raise the temperature, they are frequently dismissed with a flippant "you're just weak." Differences in physiological needs, such as longer bathroom breaks, are ignored—equal floor space for men's and women's restrooms means women spend exponentially more time finding safe toilets and waiting in line, while their male companions may complain about her "slowness."


The consequences of ignoring women are often worse. " Excluding women from post-disaster recovery efforts can turn the entire process into a farce. " Women perform 75% of the world's unpaid work, yet in reconstruction efforts, many planning processes fail to include women or even consult their opinions. This has led to rebuilt homes without kitchens, and humanitarian organizations distributing food that must be cooked without providing cooking fuel in refugee camps.


Silencing women's voices when problems arise is deeply ironic. Although men and women suffer equally from trauma, displacement, injury, and death during disasters, women also face unique injustices. During conflicts, incidents of domestic violence against women increase. Whether facing war, pandemics, or natural disasters, women confront the same fears as men—and on top of that, the risks of childbirth. Women cannot choose when they give birth, just as humans cannot predict when disasters will strike. The same urgency shown in rescuing the injured is not extended to women's biological needs, leading repeatedly to tragedies where women's sanitary needs are ignored and women die during childbirth in disasters. It is not the disaster that kills them, but gender—and a society that fails to account for how gender restricts women's lives.


The near-universal statistical neglect of women is often justified by one main excuse: women are too complex to measure. From transportation planners to medical researchers to tech developers, they regard women as abnormal, atypical. Simplicity is easier, simplicity is cheaper, but simplicity does not reflect reality. When your statistical models omit half the global population, what you create is not order—it is chaos.


In the face of this enormous gap, we must increase women's representation across all areas of life. Because as more women gain power and influence, another pattern becomes increasingly clear: women do not so easily forget the existence of other women, as men often do. We must close the gap in women's underrepresentation. Ensure women are not forgotten when participating in decision-making, research, and knowledge production. Ensure women's lives and perspectives emerge from the shadows. All it takes is—asking women.



About the author: Caroline Criado Perez



Caroline Criado Perez

(Caroline Criado Perez)


British writer and journalist.
She graduated from Oxford University and the London School of Economics. Her work frequently appears in major media outlets such as The Guardian, The Times, the Financial Times, and the New Statesman. Her 2015 book Do It Like a Woman, a collective biography of women, was named a book of the year by multiple publications. Her 2019 book Invisible Women won the British Book Awards' Book of the Year (Popular Non-Fiction), the Royal Society Science Book Prize, and the Financial Times and McKinsey Business Book of the Year Award, and has been translated into over thirty languages.


Beyond writing, Caroline Criado Perez has actively advanced gender equality and social justice. She successfully campaigned to prevent the Bank of England from removing the only female figure (besides the Queen) from British banknotes; she led the effort to erect a statue of suffragists in Parliament Square—London's first statue of women—commemorating British women gaining the right to vote in 1918; and she pressured Twitter to reform its procedures for handling online abuse and threats, significantly improving public discourse.


For her long-standing advocacy for women and marginalized groups, she was named one of the BBC's 100 Women in 2013, awarded the Order of the British Empire (OBE) in 2015, received the Finnish HÄN Prize in 2020, and was granted an honorary doctorate by the University of Lincoln in 2021.
