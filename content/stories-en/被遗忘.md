Nineteenth-century America's West was a land repeatedly torn apart by gold rushes, railroad projects, and immigration policies. In this land, Chinese men were recorded in official ledgers as "coolies," while <strong>Chinese women presented a more complex picture: trafficked "mui tsai" (young female servants), respectable wives found in clan associations and churches, laundry workers, and domestic maids.</strong>

  

Under the dual shackles of racial discrimination and gender oppression, these women who crossed the ocean did not become silent victims, but instead used their resilience to build families and resist injustice, becoming the hidden founders of Chinese American communities.

  

Their stories are a forgotten epic of survival, and also a hymn to women's resistance.

  

<strong>"Chains Across the Ocean: Trafficked Destinies and Desperate Survival"</strong>

  

In mid-19th century China, poor peasant families in the coastal regions of Fujian and Guangdong remained trapped in the custom of "son preference." It was common to see newborn girls abandoned in cradles or tied to city walls. Even those girls who survived infancy were often sold due to family debt or poverty.

  

At that time, California in the United States was in the midst of a gold rush. The legend of the "Gold Mountain" attracted countless adventurers seeking sudden wealth, and also gave rise to a criminal trans-Pacific trafficking network—&nbsp;<strong>Brokers lured or purchased Chinese girls, packed them into ship holds, and transported them to the United States like cargo. </strong>After 1860, this highly profitable trade was monopolized by criminal gangs. Some companies controlled 800 girls aged from two to sixteen, who were imported in batches in baskets and awaited sale at set prices.

  

In San Francisco's slave markets, Chinese girl children were priced openly. Before the passage of the 1882 Chinese Exclusion Act, a wealthy person could buy a Chinese maid for $80; in underground black markets, the price of a girl could reach $400 to $2,000.

  

![image](/media/stories/20260307-50e2/5003.webp)

Image: First page of the Chinese Exclusion Act (Wikipedia)

  

These girls, known as "mui tsai," were nominally servants in wealthy households, but in reality had no personal freedom and could not leave their employers before age 18. They were frequently subjected to physical abuse and sexual assault. Even more cruelly, after the 13th Amendment to the U.S. Constitution abolished slavery in 1865, criminal gangs forced girls to sign "voluntary" prostitution contracts to evade legal punishment: one day of illness extended the contract by two weeks, menstruation counted as illness, and any attempt to escape meant lifelong enslavement. This seemingly legal agreement in fact cast them into an indefinite abyss of servitude.

  

The "pigeon cages" on San Francisco streets were prisons for Chinese sex workers. These narrow wooden rooms were set along the streets, and girls who had been lured in were locked inside to attract passersby. The top price for services was only 25 cents, and there were even 10-cent "peep show" services.

  

Police "anti-prostitution" raids never truly rescued them, but instead drove them from busy streets to back alleys. After 1865, police even directly attempted to expel Chinese women from San Francisco.

  

Sexually transmitted diseases spread among the sex workers, and when they became critically ill, they were often thrown onto the streets or locked in small rooms to die alone. <strong>A coroner's report from 1860 recorded that a young Chinese sex worker was hastily buried after death. The autopsy revealed her neck had been broken and her body covered in bruises, yet her abuser was never held accountable.</strong>

  

Even if they managed to escape brothels, Chinese women still faced multiple hardships. In the 19th century, the gender ratio in California was as high as 92:8, and Chinese women made up less than 5% of the total Chinese population in the United States. They worked as laundry workers, maids, or seamstresses, suffering discrimination and wage theft from white employers; or they married Chinese laborers, managing households in crude wooden shacks while fending off hostility from white communities.

  

In 1876, a riot broke out in the town of Antioch, California. Over 40 white men, claiming that "Chinese prostitutes were spreading syphilis," stormed into Chinatown and issued an eviction order, throwing the belongings of Chinese women into baskets and forcing them to flee to the docks. That night, Chinatown was set on fire, the fire department stood by and did nothing, and local residents watched and cheered.

  

In the end, the only two remaining buildings in Chinatown were destroyed. The surviving Chinese each bought a 25-cent boat ticket and fled to San Francisco, while local newspapers celebrated&nbsp;<strong>"This indecent class has finally disappeared."</strong>

  

  

  

  

<strong>"Individual Resistance: Ah Toy, Suey Hin, and Mary Tape"</strong>

  

In the darkness, some Chinese women used wisdom and courage <strong>to break the chains of fate and become masters of their own lives.</strong>

  

The story of Ah Toy, a woman from Guangdong, is a legend among 19th-century Chinese American women.

  

![image](/media/stories/20260307-50e2/5004.webp)

Image: Ah Toy (Chinatown Archives)

  

In 1849, 20-year-old Ah Toy traveled to San Francisco with her husband to seek gold. Her husband died en route, and she became the captain's concubine, but through this she accumulated her first fortune. After arriving in San Francisco, using her striking appearance and unique Eastern charm, Ah Toy created a special business—charging one ounce of gold (worth about $560 today) for each "close viewing" by a man.

  

Ah Toy also proactively learned English and frequently filed court appeals to protect her interests. When clients paid with brass shavings instead of gold, she boldly took them to court; facing exploitation by Chinatown clan associations, she repeatedly filed complaints and resisted, refusing to be controlled.

  

A year or two later, Ah Toy transitioned into a madam, opening chain brothels and importing girls from China, some as young as 11, to work for her. In 1854, California passed a law prohibiting Chinese people from testifying in court, stripping Ah Toy of legal protection. That same year, anti-prostitution laws targeting Chinese people were introduced, and she was arrested several times for "dirty rooms," while her white counterparts were never prosecuted.

  

Realizing the injustice, Ah Toy sold her assets and left the business in 1857. She returned to California in 1868, married, and lived a quiet life with her husband selling clams in the town of Alviso in South Bay, until her death at the age of 100 in 1928.

  

Another woman, Suey Hin from Shandong, also had a tragic fate. At age five, she was sold by her father for a piece of gold; at twelve, she was resold to San Francisco. After ten years in prostitution, she was redeemed for $3,000 by a laundry worker. Yet happiness lasted only three years—after her husband's death, Suey Hin was left without support and had to return to prostitution.

  

She purchased eight girls from China and brought them to the United States under the names of her own children. In 1898, she sent one of the girls she had abducted back to China and, through a church, rescued the other seven, finding Christian husbands for them. Suey Hin's experience reflects the complex situation of Chinese women living in 19th-century America, <strong>where under the pressure of survival, they were both oppressors and victims.</strong>

  

Some Chinese women's resistance went beyond personal fate and touched on systemic injustice. Mary Tape was one such representative.

  

![image](/media/stories/20260307-50e2/5005.webp)

Image: The Mary Tape family, ca. 1884 (Smith Collection)

  

In 1868, the underage Mary Tape came to the United States and, with the help of the Christian Presbyterian Mission, learned English and became an amateur photographer and artist. In 1875, she married Chinese merchant Jeu Dip and had four children.

  

In 1884, when her 8-year-old daughter Mamie Tape applied for school, the San Francisco public school system&nbsp;<strong>refused her admission on the grounds that</strong>&nbsp;"Chinese children are not allowed to attend." Mary Tape and her husband immediately sued the school board, arguing in court: "All schools must admit children between the ages of six and twenty-one residing in the district, unless the child is dirty, has bad habits, or suffers from a contagious disease." In a letter to the school board, she wrote, <strong>"My daughter was born in the United States. She is an American citizen and is entitled to the same right to education as other children."</strong>

  

On January 9, 1885, the Superior Court ruled in favor of the Tapes. This lawsuit not only secured her daughter's right to attend school but also became a major milestone in the Chinese American struggle for educational equality. Mary Tape's resistance proved that Chinese women never gave up their pursuit of dignity and rights, and their courage paved the way for future generations.

  

  

  

  

<strong>"Systemic Strangulation:</strong>

<strong>Moral Coercion and Racial Segregation Under the Page Act"</strong>

  

The suffering of Chinese women in 19th-century America stemmed not only from human trafficking and gender discrimination but also from systemic institutional oppression.

  

On February 18, 1875, Horace F. Page, a Republican congressman from California, introduced a draft immigration bill ostensibly targeting "cheap Chinese laborers, immoral Chinese women, and domestic criminals," <strong>but in reality aimed directly at Asian women, especially Chinese women.</strong>

  

This law, known as the Page Act, was signed into effect by President Grant on March 3, 1875, stipulating that anyone attempting to bring "undesirable" Asians into the United States would face a $2,000 fine and one year in prison.

  

The implementation of the Page Act was full of discrimination and absurdity. The U.S. consul in Hong Kong required Chinese women to submit personal information, moral declarations, medical examinations, and personality assessments before immigration, and to undergo repeated interrogations at the consulate, on ships, and at customs. Officials even used "physical clues" such as leg bindings, demeanor, and walking posture to determine whether a woman was a prostitute.

  

This examination was essentially a racially biased presumption of guilt. In the eyes of white officials, all Chinese women were potential prostitutes, and the standard for distinguishing "wife from prostitute" was merely their subjective judgment of "honesty." Between 1875 and 1882, at least hundreds of Chinese women were arbitrarily deported, their fates rewritten by a single accusation of "immorality."

  

The true motive behind the law was far more complex than simply "upholding morality."

  

![image](/media/stories/20260307-50e2/5006.webp)

Chinese women and children at an immigration center in California (PBS)

  

In 19th-century America's West, Chinese laborers, known for their hard work and low wage demands, were seen by white people as a threat to the labor market. <strong>Lawmakers knew that the key to preventing Chinese population growth lay in cutting off the possibility of Chinese people forming families. </strong>Preventing Chinese women from immigrating further exacerbated the gender imbalance, making it difficult for Chinese men to establish families in the United States, and thus preventing them from settling there long-term, ultimately forcing them to return to China.

  

The Page Act, under the guise of "anti-prostitution," actually aimed to achieve racial segregation by restricting the entry of Chinese women. Some historians point out, <strong>"Emphasizing morality and focusing on prostitution was merely a strategy; the core of the Act was to protect the competitiveness of white Americans in the labor market and ensure that a permanent Chinese community would not take root in the American West."</strong>

  

Ironically, the Page Act actually worsened the prostitution problem it claimed to combat. Due to the strict restrictions on female immigration, the gender ratio among Chinese immigrants became severely imbalanced: around the time the Chinese Exclusion Act was enacted in 1882, 39,579 Chinese people entered the United States, but only 136 of them were women.

  

This imbalance, to some extent, made brothel businesses flourish even more, prompting criminal gangs to smuggle Chinese women more aggressively for profit. Meanwhile, white society never ceased its "slut-shaming" of Chinese women:

  

They claimed Chinese sex workers spread syphilis and endangered the health of white men, yet turned a blind eye to the humiliation suffered by Chinese women; they criticized Chinese marriage customs (such as concubinage) for threatening America's monogamous system, while ignoring the tragic reality of Chinese women being trafficked and enslaved. The essence of this double standard was the objectification of Chinese women, using moral accusations to mask racial discrimination and economic interests.

  

The impact of the Page Act was profound and devastating. It not only caused a sharp decline in the population of Chinese women in the United States, but also firmly pinned the image of Chinese women to the pillar of "immorality." This stereotype persisted for over a century. It was not until after World War II, with the increase in Chinese female immigration and the rise of the civil rights movement, that these prejudices gradually began to break down.

  

But the trauma left by the Act is difficult to heal. <strong>It legally confirmed the status of Chinese women as second-class citizens, institutionalizing racial discrimination and gender oppression, becoming an ignoble mark in American history.</strong>

  

Despite facing severe oppression, Chinese women in 19th-century America laid the foundation for the survival and continuity of Chinese communities through their resilient spirit.

  

Among them were former sex workers and mui tsai (young female servants), as well as native-born Chinese women. Regardless of their backgrounds, these women fought tirelessly for survival and family. In a typical Chinatown household, the husband was usually nine years older than the wife. The couple raised one or two children together, and the wife not only managed household duties but also supported the family through work such as laundry, sewing, farming, and serving. Under conditions of material scarcity and harsh environments, they held up their families with their own hands, ensuring the continuation of Chinese lineage on American soil.

  

![image](/media/stories/20260307-50e2/5007.webp)

  

Looking back at history, <strong>19th-century Chinese American women were a group of forgotten pioneers, yet also an unextinguished spark. </strong>They were forced to leave their homeland, enslaved in a foreign land rife with racial discrimination, and struggled to survive in the cracks of systemic oppression. But they never gave up <strong>their fight for dignity, for rights, for freedom. </strong>They used wisdom to resist injustice, courage to rewrite their destinies, their hands to build families, and mutual support to warm one another.

  

Their stories should not be buried in the dust of history; their resilience deserves to be remembered forever.
