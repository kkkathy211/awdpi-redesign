Guest Introduction



Wang Yingyi (Stephanie)

  • Ph.D. in Gender Studies, University of Washington

  • Assistant Professor in the Department of Gender Studies, St. Lawrence University, USA

  • Over ten years of experience working in the fields of sexuality and gender, both in China and abroad

Former participant in young feminist activist movements; served as a committee member of the Chinese Lala Alliance, Secretary-General of the Youth Committee at Marie Stopes International (sexual and reproductive health organization), and peer educator trainer in sexuality and gender.

Since 2020, Dr. Wang Yingyi (Stephanie) has been using podcasts and videos to increase the visibility of feminism and gender theory on Chinese social media, disseminating more accessible theoretical knowledge and practical experiences, and building a broader feminist community.

Dr. Wang Yingyi (Stephanie) is a scholar and activist in gender studies. She earned her Ph.D. from the Department of Gender Studies at the University of Washington and her master's degree from the Department of Social Work and Social Administration at the University of Hong Kong. Her research focuses on feminist political economy, NGOs and social movements, labor and emotional politics, queer studies, and transnational feminism. She previously worked at China Development Brief, deeply involved in research and editorial work on civil society. As a co-founder of the r&B bisexual group, since its establishment in 2013, she and her team have organized and coordinated volunteers to publish locally produced bisexual/pansexual handbooks in China, conduct qualitative and quantitative research on bisexual/pansexual communities, carry out oral history projects, host online salons and tea-chat mutual support groups, and translate international bisexual/pansexual community resources. These efforts aim to create safe spaces for China's local bisexual/pansexual communities, combat discrimination and violence against bisexual/pansexual individuals both within and outside LGBTQ+ communities, and develop indigenous narratives for China's diverse desire communities. She believes in the power of public education and is committed to spreading knowledge both inside and outside campuses, striving to be a bridge and agent between knowledge and action.

Without such systematic analysis, if we simply remain at the level of male resentment, true progress toward gender equality will be difficult."

Transformation: From Social Activist to Researcher


Dr. Wang Yingyi grew up in a Hakka family and, during her childhood, felt discomfort due to patriarchal clan traditions that treated women unequally, which sparked a raw sense of anger. In college, in search of answers, she began reading Simone de Beauvoir's The Second Sex and discovered that feminist theory could explain these inequalities. Filled with anger and excitement, she developed the desire to engage in both activism and academic research.



"My earliest research idea was formed at that time—after reading The Second Sex, I wanted to conduct a study on the living conditions of Chinese women. I felt extremely angry, especially about my own situation, and I had a sense of power that I couldn't release. So even though I was just an undergraduate student, I still wanted to do these things."

Dr. Wang Yingyi considers this period her early "feminist awakening," during which she read extensively and participated in many activities related to gender equality.

"In these activities, I used my own lived experiences of dissatisfaction and curiosity to understand feminist theory. I was very lucky to meet many people working in this field, which opened up a new world for me. From then on, I finally seemed to have found the direction of my life."



Speaking about her transition from social activist to researcher, Dr. Wang Yingyi points out that feminist research and activism are not binary opposites—activists can also be researchers. "Feminist theory is constantly in action, constantly challenging or updating itself. I think this really suits my personal 'vibe.'"


Challenge: The Isolation and Loneliness of Asian American Women Scholars in the U.S.


"Recently, I reflected carefully on how my six years of doctoral study in the U.S. impacted me personally. I realized I was deeply depressed, isolated, and lonely, unsure of where I belonged. This state accompanied me throughout my entire time as a graduate student in America." 



First, language barriers were one of the biggest obstacles. Although overseas Chinese people commonly face language challenges, these difficulties are often downplayed. In reality, even for someone at the doctoral level like her, it remained a significant hurdle.

Dr. Wang Yingyi's department was Gender Studies, where most of her classmates were non-white Americans. During the first three years of her Ph.D., she rarely spoke in class and struggled to express complex theories and ideas fluently, spending the early years of her doctoral studies in discomfort and self-doubt.



Another challenge stemmed from her identity as a Chinese woman and sexual minority, which made it difficult for her to integrate into American local culture and life.

Dr. Wang Yingyi tried joining two local communities—one being a grassroots group in Seattle and the other a group of Chinese Ph.D. students. However, she quickly realized she could hardly find a sense of belonging in either. The first group mainly focused on local U.S.-based movements, and she often felt like a "tool person," constantly asked to explain what problems existed in China and what LGBTQ+ movements had done there. The second group, composed of Ph.D. students, showed little interest in her feminist and LGBTQ+ activism.

This feeling of being caught between two worlds left Dr. Wang Yingyi deeply confused at the time. She found that American public perceptions of China were overly simplistic and polarized, and she felt neither truly accepted nor included, constantly sinking into intense loneliness.



"Our situation in a so-called democratic country like the U.S. is actually very passive. We can feel the long-standing 'othering' of Chinese people—either portrayed as 'democracy fighters' or 'little pinkos.' Many overseas Chinese have experienced this dilemma."

And to break through these specific difficulties and stereotypes faced by Chinese people, telling a complex and comprehensive story is extremely difficult. Due to the particularity of Sino-U.S. relations and the limitations of democratic politics, Dr. Wang Yingyi still found it hard to accurately express her personal background as a feminist, a bisexual international student, etc., which caused her deep feelings of "depression" and loneliness from the inability to communicate.

Although these barriers brought many difficulties to her doctoral life, Dr. Wang Yingyi still believes that her six years of research and study in the U.S. were immensely rewarding—being able to focus entirely on the fields and topics she was passionate about was truly "a luxury." During this time, she made significant academic and cognitive progress, becoming a committed Marxist feminist and continuing her research on gendered and racialized unpaid labor from this perspective.



"Things I couldn't figure out before, including certain debates within feminism—if you don't incorporate a political-economic lens, you can't analyze them more comprehensively. I think it's really important to connect individual fates with larger political and economic structures.", "The course that influenced me the most while studying abroad was Racial Capitalism."


Action: From Research to Media Practice


Dr. Wang Yingyi believes that the context for discussing feminism varies completely across countries, with different political systems following different trajectories. In the U.S., for example, rights for women and racial minorities are continuously consolidated through legal means. At the same time, however, there is also a force that resists institutionalization—while legalization and institutionalization advance, there are groups opposing the system, such as many anarchists in the U.S. who constantly seek to dismantle state control and build self-governing mutual aid groups.

When discussing local topics, Dr. Wang Yingyi mentioned the example of Quan Xixi's dialogue with Chizuko Ueno, which sparked public attention. She believes it was positive that this event generated such high discussion, as it encouraged the public to engage in conversations about identity politics and gender governance. However, she noted that some media platforms failed to delve deeper, limiting the discussion to superficial themes like "beauty" and "confidence." This surface-level empowerment, tied to consumerism, cannot further explore the political economy behind structural issues, thus restricting the empowering potential of these social media platforms.



Therefore, in the new media era, Dr. Wang Yingyi hopes to reach broader audiences through the internet, breaking down feminist theories and demystifying feminism using the simplest and most accessible language. She believes feminism still carries many stigmas and hopes to narrate theories in her own unique way, gradually changing the minds of some people.



"As for why I continue to persist, it's because I've made many videos on gender identity, and transgender viewers have told me they felt warmth and encouragement. As long as one person sends me such a message, I feel it's completely worth it, so I've kept going."


Vision: Hopes for the Development of Feminism


When discussing the future direction of feminism, Dr. Wang Yingyi believes that answering this question first requires addressing very concrete issues, such as "unpaid labor being recognized" and "housewives no longer seen as making a so-called individual choice," or "a world without gender-based violence." From a broader societal perspective, she believes every person should first gain the right to express themselves and speak freely.

 "Feminism should not become a dogmatic existence—for instance, dictating that you can only live one certain way, or that not marrying means you're not a feminist. Feminism means every person's choices are respected, and every person's reality is considered from multiple dimensions."

    She observes that the range of topics society can currently discuss in depth is too narrow, and feminism is sometimes reduced to a binary "gender opposition." In reality, "woman is a relative identity; in specific contexts, we must consider her class, education level, whether she is disabled, and more."

She points out that the challenges a group faces are multi-layered, not solely rooted in gender, though gender remains a crucial analytical lens and dimension of oppression. We should be able to identify the artificially constructed oppositions designed to distract and confuse—what we need is a deeper, multidimensional understanding and analysis of life, rather than remaining stuck on constructed binary oppositions.

"Although a small number of people have already begun engaging in multidimensional thinking, I see that most public discourse remains simplistic and binary, lacking systematic analysis of gender issues, and failing to recognize that women are also builders and practitioners who create and resolve gender issues. Without such systematic analysis, and if discussions remain merely at the level of male resentment, genuine progress toward gender equality will be difficult to achieve. "

How feminism can develop in broader spaces, and how each individual can practice true feminism in daily life, are the issues Dr. Wang Yingyi cares about most right now. In addition, she hopes online discussions can become more diverse and systematic, so that everyone's thoughts are respected and everyone is free to do what they truly want to do."
