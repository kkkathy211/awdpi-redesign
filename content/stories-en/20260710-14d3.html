Introduction

In recent years, an increasing number of Chinese organizations have launched public welfare projects in the United States. However, the U.S. legal system is complex and highly regulated; overlooking compliance issues may result in hefty fines or even legal litigation.


To help organizations effectively avoid risks, this article systematically outlines the core legal risks and compliance recommendations for U.S. non-profit organizations for reference.


I. Who Regulates You? — Understanding Your "Regulators"


In the United States, non-profit organizations are subject to overlapping oversight by multiple federal and state enforcement agencies. Clearly understanding the functions of each regulatory body is the prerequisite for effective compliance:


  • Internal Revenue Service (IRS): responsible for reviewing and regulating an organization's 501(c)(3) tax-exempt status. The IRS uses the annual information return (Form 990) to examine whether "excess benefit transactions" exist, and serious violations may lead to revocation of tax-exempt status.

  • Federal Trade Commission (FTC): responsible for combating fraudulent fundraising practices, especially false statements and misleading donors.

  • Department of Labor (DOL): enforces labor standards such as minimum wage, overtime pay, and employee classification under the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), preventing organizations from incorrectly labeling employees as volunteers.

  • Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC): enforces federal anti-discrimination laws (such as Title VII of the Civil Rights Act and the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA)), applicable to organizations that meet employee threshold requirements.

  • Department of Justice (DOJ): intervenes in investigations and prosecutions involving criminal offenses such as mail fraud, wire fraud, and money laundering.

  • State Attorney General: holds broad supervisory authority over charitable fundraising activities and may initiate civil or even criminal lawsuits.

  • State labor departments & Secretary of State offices: enforce state-level labor laws and manage registration and annual renewal of charitable fundraising.

To look up specific federal regulations, visit the Office of the Law Revision Counsel of the U.S. House of Representatives (http://uscode.house.gov) or the U.S. Government Publishing Office (http://www.gpoaccess.gov) to freely search the United States Code.


II. Risk Scan: Has Your Organization Stepped on a Landmine?


1

Employment: The Boundary Between Employees and Volunteers

This is one of the areas with the highest current litigation risk. In 2026, the California Court of Appeal established a stricter "volunteer" classification standard in the case Spilman v. The Salvation Army. To prove that an individual is a volunteer, an organization must satisfy two conditions simultaneously: first, the individual works for their own benefit rather than compensation; second, the use of unpaid labor is not a pretext to circumvent wage laws.

Providing housing or transportation allowances tied to hours worked or productivity may be deemed "de facto wages," thereby invalidating volunteer status. Once classified as an "employee," the organization must pay back minimum wages and overtime, and may face class-action lawsuits.

Other common risks include:


  • Wage and hour compliance: The federal minimum wage is $7.25 per hour, and many states have higher standards, with overtime pay at 1.5 times the regular rate. Non-profit organizations engaged in commercial activities with annual revenue of $500,000 or more are typically subject to FLSA.

  • Anti-discrimination obligations: Federal anti-discrimination laws have low thresholds, generally applying to employers with 15 or 20 employees. Many state anti-discrimination laws apply to smaller employers.

  • Background check compliance: If using third-party agencies for background checks, organizations must strictly follow the disclosure, authorization, and adverse action procedures under the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA).

  • Volunteer protection: The protection provided by the Federal Volunteer Protection Act (VPA) is limited, and organizations cannot rely on it entirely.


2

Fundraising: Not Simply "Asking for Donations"

  • Fundraising without registration: 44 U.S. states have registration requirements for charitable fundraising. It is important to note that federal 501(c)(3) tax-exempt status does not equate to state fundraising authorization. Fundraising without registration may result in fines of $1,000 to $10,000 per violation, and in some states, it is even classified as a criminal offense.

  • Fraudulent fundraising: Enforcement agencies strongly crack down on such behavior. For example, an organization once defrauded over $110 million under the guise of supporting veterans and was ultimately forced to dissolve, with its leaders permanently barred from fundraising.

  • Improper private benefit transactions (private inurement): The IRS imposes intermediate sanctions under Section 4958 of the Internal Revenue Code for "excess benefit transactions," and severe cases may result in revocation of tax-exempt status.

  • Failure to file annual reports: Failure to submit Form 990 for three consecutive years results in automatic loss of tax-exempt status, requiring reapplication.

  • Misuse of restricted donations: failure to use donated funds for their designated purpose as specified in donation agreements may lead to lawsuits from donors and reputational damage.

  • Loss of control over third-party fundraising: even if fundraising is outsourced to professional agencies, the organization remains ultimately liable.

To look up specific charitable registration requirements in each state, refer to the "Going Global Public Service Platform" (http://fec.mofcom.gov.cn). To search relevant case law, use FindLaw.com to freely access decisions from the U.S. Supreme Court and federal appellate courts.


III. Compliance Guide: How to Build Your "Firewall"?

1

Employment Compliance

1. Clearly define job classifications: develop detailed job descriptions for each position, clearly distinguishing between "employees" and "volunteers."
2. Carefully design benefits: ensure that provided benefits fall within the scope of "nominal expenses" and are never tied to hours worked or productivity.
3. Establish written agreements: sign written agreements with volunteers clarifying that services are voluntary and unpaid; sign formal employment contracts with employees.
4. Systematic training and recordkeeping: provide onboarding training and properly retain all training records.
5. Review insurance coverage: confirm whether existing insurance covers volunteer activities, and purchase supplemental insurance if necessary.
6. Background check compliance: if using third-party agencies, strictly follow FCRA procedures and pay attention to state "ban the box" laws restricting inquiries into criminal history.
7. Pay attention to state law differences: thoroughly research labor laws in states where the organization primarily operates, as state laws vary significantly.

2

Fundraising Compliance

1. Complete charitable registration: register and renew on time in every state where fundraising activities are planned.
2. File Form 990 accurately: ensure information is truthful and complete; organizations involved in political activities must ensure consistency with data reported to the Federal Election Commission (FEC).
3. Strengthen board governance: apply the "rebuttable presumption" procedure for executive compensation and related-party transactions, with approval and documentation by a conflict-free committee.
4. Track restricted funds: establish separate accounts for donations designated for specific purposes, use funds strictly as intended, and provide regular reports to donors.
5. Supervise third-party agencies: clearly specify compliance requirements in contracts and retain the right to review fundraising materials.
6. Establish anti-fraud internal controls: implement an approval process for promotional materials and a dual-review mechanism for fund flows, and establish an anonymous reporting channel.

3

Other Recommendations

  • Conduct regular compliance audits: hire professional firms to conduct comprehensive audits of employment and fundraising activities.

  • Monitor case law developments: continuously track the latest court rulings (such as the California Spilman case) and adjust compliance strategies promptly.


Conclusion

Operating a non-profit organization in the United States involves a complex and constantly evolving legal environment. From employee classification to fundraising registration, every step carries potential compliance risks. By establishing a systematic compliance framework and making good use of free online legal resources, organizations can effectively manage legal risks while achieving their public service goals, ensuring sustainable and stable growth.
