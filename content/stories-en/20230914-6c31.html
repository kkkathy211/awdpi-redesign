Taliban Shuts Down Women's Beauty Salons in Afghanistan


In July, the Taliban announced that businesses exclusively serving women violate Islamic law.


Local officials stated that all women's beauty salons in Afghanistan must close by July 25, one of several bans announced earlier this month by the Taliban government. In their view, such businesses could even create economic difficulties for men intending to marry.


Beauty salons are among the few public spaces where Afghan women can gather outside the home. The closure of these salons represents another erosion of women's rights in Afghanistan. Since seizing power in August 2021, the Taliban has steadily stripped women of their rights, banning them and girls from most public spaces, prohibiting long-distance travel without a male relative, and banning education beyond sixth grade.


The salons were given a one-month grace period to cease operations.


Sadik Akif Muhajir, spokesperson for the Taliban's Ministry for the Promotion of Virtue and Prevention of Vice, said that Taliban security forces in the capital Kabul conducted patrols on July 25 to ensure compliance with the salon ban.

Iranian Women Resist Return of "Morality Police"


According to a July 24 report by The Guardian, Iranian activists expressed disappointment over the resumption of patrols enforcing headscarf wearing, but insisted that protests would continue ahead of the anniversary of Amini's death.


A police spokesperson confirmed the news last week, stating that patrols had already begun on the streets to monitor civilians who "persist in non-compliance, ignoring the consequences of not wearing the proper headscarf."


Just two months before this announcement, 22-year-old Mahsa Amini died in custody last September after being detained for allegedly failing to wear the Islamic headscarf properly. Her death triggered the largest public unrest in Iran in years.


The Guardian interviewed women who participated in nationwide protests following Amini's death, who said they had already seen police harassing girls without headscarves on the streets.


"I don't feel anything about the news of the 'morality police' returning. Although Western media keep telling Iranians that Gasht-e-Irshad (Guidance Patrol) was abolished, not a single Iranian friend of mine believes this," said a 22-year-old from Rasht.

Hungarian President at Major Women's Rights Conference

Speech Sparks Anger


According to a July 19 Guardian report, key participants at a women's rights conference in Rwanda were shocked by the presence of Hungarian President Katalin Novák. The anti-abortion president has been repeatedly criticized for her anti-equality stances.


Before becoming president in 2022, Novák served as Minister for Family Affairs, overseeing work related to fertility policy. She once stated that Hungarian women "should not compete with men" and should not expect to earn as much.


Novák has also hosted representatives from Alliance Defending Freedom, a U.S. organization designated as a hate group by the Southern Poverty Law Center, and awarded several prominent anti-abortion activists.


"We were stunned," said Brazilian activist and European Young Feminists representative Bruna Martins. "We don't understand why this women's rights conference would invite Novák."

Asian Americans in Florida Protest SB264 Bill,

Warn It Will Legalize Hate Crimes Against Asians


On July 18, under the initiative of the nonprofit Florida Asian American Justice Alliance (FAAJA), Asian American community representatives and major civil rights organizations protested outside the Tallahassee courthouse against the state's discriminatory new housing law SB264.


The rally coincided with the hearing of *Shen v. Simpson*, a lawsuit jointly filed by the American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU) and other legal organizations challenging SB264.


Florida's SB264 bill was signed into law by the governor on May 8 this year, restricting Chinese citizens from purchasing real estate in Florida. It prohibits Chinese citizens holding non-immigrant status from buying land exceeding two acres or property within five miles of military bases.


Plaintiffs' attorneys argue that the bill expands housing discrimination against Asian individuals and violates the Equal Protection Clause of the U.S. Constitution.


Echo King, president of the Florida Asian American Justice Alliance, led the rally, stating that this law would legitimize anti-Asian hate and could trigger a chilling effect.

Nevada Man Suspected of Killing Chinese-American Girlfriend, Previously Convicted of Attempted Child Molestation


A 31-year-old man in Nevada, George Bone, was arrested on suspicion of murdering his Chinese-American girlfriend, Beverly Ma.


According to reports, Las Vegas police responded on July 26 to a residential call. Officers discovered a female victim who had been dead for a long time. Investigation revealed that the victim's boyfriend, Bone, had hidden her body in a closet in an upstairs bedroom and lived with the corpse for over two months. Bone claimed he feared his girlfriend might "come back to life," so he blocked the closet with objects.


Homicide detectives classified the case as murder and arrested suspect Bone on the same day on murder charges. Notably, victim Beverly Ma had called police on May 4, during which dispatchers heard a man and woman arguing. But when police arrived, no one answered the door, leading investigators to speculate the victim was already dead. It remains unclear why police did not enter the house to investigate at that time.


It is reported that Bone was previously convicted in 2014 of attempted child molestation and is listed on Nevada's sex offender registry.

Chinese-American Singer CoCo Lee Dies by Suicide Amid Depression


On the evening of July 5, CoCo Lee's elder sister, Nancy Lee, announced her younger sister's death, revealing that CoCo had suffered from depression in recent years and died by suicide at home on July 2, with resuscitation efforts failing on July 5.


CoCo Lee was a pop singer who achieved global success. For American audiences, her most famous work was the song "A Love Before Time," which she performed for the 2000 film *Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon*. The song was nominated for Best Original Song at the Academy Awards. In 2001, CoCo performed "A Love Before Time" at the Oscars, becoming the first Chinese-language singer to perform at the Academy Awards ceremony, hailed by *Time* magazine as "a pride of Chinese people."


Active in the music industry for 30 years, CoCo set multiple records for Chinese-language singers. She was the first Chinese-language singer to enter the U.S. music market, the first Chinese artist to release an English album globally, and the first Chinese-language singer invited to hold a solo concert at the Kennedy Center.

Research finds Asian Americans and women

are more at risk from the rise of artificial intelligence

A report released by the Pew Research Center on July 26 indicates that among all racial groups in the United States, Asian Americans make up the highest share of workers who frequently interact with artificial intelligence. 24% of Asian American workers are employed in fields categorized as having the "highest exposure to AI," compared to 20% of White workers, 15% of Black workers, and 13% of Hispanic workers.


The report states that "highest exposure to AI" means that the most important activities in these jobs could be altered or taken over by artificial intelligence; Asian workers have the highest levels of formal education and are more likely to work in analytical roles involving cutting-edge technologies—precisely the areas where AI is making an impact.


The study also finds significant differences between female and male workers in their exposure to artificial intelligence. 21% of women work in jobs with the highest AI exposure, compared to 17% of men. The report notes that women are often dominant in health and education fields, while men are more concentrated in industries such as construction or manufacturing.

New York City's first woman-owned

Asian American bookstore receives donations for rebuilding

Yu & Me Books, the first bookstore in New York's Chinatown owned by a Chinese American woman, suffered a fire on July 4, and owner Lucy Yu said the blaze caused severe damage to the store's inventory, furniture, and building structure.


Lucy Yu launched a fundraising campaign on the platform GoFundMe to help rebuild the bookstore, receiving widespread support. Actor Simu Liu, Chinese American author Celeste Ng, and Korean American author Min Jin Lee were among those who generously donated. The campaign has raised over $300,000, which will be used to upgrade equipment, pay employee salaries, and replace furniture.


Opened in December 2021, Yu & Me Books is celebrated as New York City's first woman-owned Asian American bookstore. Lucy Yu hopes the bookstore can tell stories of immigrants and people of color.


Cathy Linh Che from the Asian American literary organization Kundiman said that the pandemic has made spaces like Yu & Me Books increasingly rare.


Against the backdrop of rising anti-Asian hate crimes during the COVID-19 pandemic, Yu & Me Books—hosting regular events such as author readings and open mic nights—had served as a sanctuary for the community.

The "Leftovers" exhibition explores how Chinese Americans

build community through food in Dallas

On July 7, 2023, the Asian American History Society of Dallas hosted the opening of the exhibition "Leftovers: The Enduring Legacy of Chinese Cooking in Dallas" at Wilson Hall in Texas, and the exhibition will run through September 22.


A quick Google search of common Chinese American restaurant dishes—such as chop suey and General Tso's chicken—reveals a series of origin stories, some of which resemble culinary myths more than historical facts. Although Chinese chefs did adapt recipes to suit American tastes, Stephanie Drenka, co-founder of the Asian American History Society of Dallas, said this exhibition is not just about Chinese food, but about immigrant families and the identities tied to them. She hopes the exhibition will help people see Chinese restaurants differently—restaurants that, in many ways, paved the way for other Asian cuisines.


Moreover, the exhibition highlights how Chinese American history is deeply intertwined with the history of Dallas. On one wall hangs a tapestry showcasing parts of Chinese American history in Texas, dating back to the 1870s, when the first Chinese immigrants arrived as railroad workers.

Opinion

How to think about the impact of Barbie dolls on women's health


Amid the box office success and high public discussion of the film *Barbie*, many issues deserve interpretation. Here, we aim to explore the film's repeated explicit references to the harmful effects of Barbie dolls on women.


For decades, Barbie dolls have been blamed for various social ills, criticized as "instilling distorted ideas of perfect bodies in young women and representing a female reality warped by male fantasy" (Lucero, 2023).


A study published in the *International Journal of Eating Disorders* concluded that for an average woman to resemble a Barbie doll, she would need to grow 2 feet taller and reduce her waist by 6 inches. Barbie's male counterpart, Ken, is also unrealistic: for an average man to look like Ken, he would need to grow 20 inches taller and increase his chest size by 11 inches (Brownell and Napolitano, 1995).


For decades, journalists, feminists, and social critics have claimed that young girls idolize Barbie dolls (Dickinson, 2000; Edut, 1990; Hesse-Biber, 1995). Those who hold the "Barbie is guilty" view argue that Barbie motivates fourth-grade girls to diet, promotes an unattainable standard of beauty, and enforces homogenization in a diverse new world (Edut, 1990).


However, as philosopher Russell reminds us: "For anything at all, we should frequently ask ourselves whether what we have long taken for granted is truly justified."


We need to consider: while the idea that "Barbie is harmful to health" may seem reasonable, is it actually supported by theory or evidence?



1

Psychological theory:

Do girls (or women) imitate Barbie dolls?

First, many discussions in media influence and eating disorder research conflate Barbie dolls with supermodels, but although both represent near-idealized beauty, they are not equivalent. To understand the premise behind criticism of Barbie, we need to step back and examine some assumptions.


Many assumptions about imitation stem from a branch of psychology—social learning theory, based on the research of Albert Bandura, which emphasizes the importance of observing and imitating others' behaviors, attitudes, and emotional responses.


Bandura (1997) stated: "Most human behavior is learned through observation of modeling: by observing others, people form an idea of how new behaviors are performed, and this encoded information later serves as a guide for action in future situations."


Using social learning theory to explain the assumed criticism of Barbie and supermodels suggests that impressionable young girls see thin women and imitate them. However, some principles of social learning theory actually contradict the idea that young women imitate fashion models.


First, Bandura's discussion of imitation involves others' behaviors, attitudes, and emotional responses—not their physical characteristics. On the other hand, if media portray supermodels refusing to eat or binge eating, such behaviors could become models for young women. But thinness is not a behavior that can be imitated.


Second, social learning theory states that observers are more likely to imitate models who are similar to themselves. That is, people are far more likely to imitate those who resemble them.


If the person they decide to become is unlike themselves, imitation will not occur, because they recognize they have chosen an unattainable model.


Ordinary girls (or women) have little real similarity to fashion models or Barbie dolls; thus, the claim that they imitate Barbie lacks a plausible underlying mechanism.


2

Epidemiological data:

Is there a positive correlation between playing with Barbie dolls and anorexia?

Taking U.S. data as an example, according to *The New York Times*, in 1991, 95% of American girls aged 3 to 11 owned at least one Barbie doll. In reality, the number of girls who played with Barbie dolls may be even higher, as toys are often shared.


Moreover, the concern is generally about any doll perceived to have an "unrealistic" or unattainable body type; while Barbie is by far the most famous example, there are countless replicas that fit this description.


Therefore, the actual proportion of girls who played with potentially problematic dolls (whether or not they were branded Barbie) during childhood may approach 100%.


Using the criteria from the *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders* published by the American Psychiatric Association, the diagnostic criteria for anorexia include:


  • refusal to maintain body weight at or above a minimally normal weight for age and height

  • intense fear of gaining weight

  • disturbance in the way one's body weight or shape is experienced (especially excessive influence of weight on self-evaluation)

  • and amenorrhea (absence of normal menstrual cycles)


Anorexia has a very low prevalence in the United States and can be considered a rare disease (Treasure and Schmidt, 2004), and its rate has remained stable for decades (Lucas et al., 1999), without increasing alongside the popularity of Barbie dolls.


3

Empirical research on Barbie dolls and body ideals

Researchers Kuther and McDonald (2004) noted that existing literature on Barbie dolls is often opinion-based, consisting of essays and popular media articles rather than scientific or evidence-based research.


The best support for the basic assumption linking body image and dolls is not based on solid evidence published in medical, psychological, or social journals, but rather found in books about toys and clothing (Davis, 1987; Sutton-Smith, 1986).


Kuther and McDonald (2004) studied sixth-grade girls, and seventh- and eighth-grade boys and girls, to understand their perceptions of Barbie dolls and the images they represent. Their hypothesis was that girls might view Barbie as "an adult female beauty ideal that young girls learn to aspire to."


The study's conclusions were somewhat contrary to the hypothesis: girls did not generally admire Barbie dolls or view them as role models. In fact, the most commonly reported form of play among participants was torturing the dolls by damaging them. When researchers asked participants about their views on Barbie dolls, the general consensus was that Barbie's body was unrealistic (Kuther and McDonald, 2004).


Although research supports the hypothesis that exposure to Barbie dolls increases girls' body dissatisfaction (Dittmar et al., 2006), this study merely artificially measured exposure to images of Barbie and other types of dolls, rather than setting up experimental scenarios reflecting how children usually play with and interact with them in real life.


Another study that incorporated physical interaction showed that exposure to Barbie dolls (through physical handling, physical observation, or image observation) led to higher internalization of thin ideals, but had no effect on body self-esteem or body dissatisfaction. The long-term impact of Barbie doll exposure on body image remains unknown (Rice et al., 2016).


4

Conclusion: Dolls are just dolls

The idea that girls idolize Barbie dolls may seem intuitively correct, but it may not be true. A girl playing with a Barbie doll does not necessarily mean she idealizes it or sees it as a body role model. Most importantly, a girl is unlikely to develop anorexia simply because she wants to be like Barbie.
