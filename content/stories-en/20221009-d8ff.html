Pleasanton, California — Mamta Shah, 45, met her second husband at a student gathering in 2012. Soon after, they moved into a small apartment in Houston, starting a new life together.


Shah had recently arrived in the United States as a refugee from Nepal, fleeing a marriage filled with abuse. Previously, she had endured over a decade of living with a violent husband.



Her first husband was a local politician, who continued to exert pathological control over her even after their divorce. She said she trusted this new partner and believed she knew enough about domestic violence to recognize it.


But she was wrong.


"At first, he was very easygoing and pleasant," Shah said in a phone interview. "But as I got closer to him, his true nature emerged—yelling at me, pushing and shoving... constant verbal abuse."


The violence only escalated.


Shah said her husband forced her to cut ties with family and friends, threatening to ruin her reputation if she fought back. After their divorce in 2017, he would still show up at her doorstep shouting. He also harassed her online, "from phone calls and text messages to social media apps like Viber." Their marriage ultimately ended with a restraining order against him and a prison sentence.



"I was terrified," Shah said. "I kept blaming myself, constantly thinking I was a terrible wife and daughter."


Shah's experience mirrors that of many South Asian women suffering from domestic violence. Violence, humiliation, and threats are just the tip of the iceberg of the abuse immigrant women endure—and a reality they are gradually forced to accept.


The combination of the pandemic and immigration instability has made Indian-American women more vulnerable to abuse than ever before.


1

Alarming statistics


A 2003 study published by the U.S. National Library of Medicine found that among 160 South Asian women surveyed in greater Boston, Massachusetts, approximately 40% had "experienced intimate partner violence," including physical, psychological, and sexual abuse.


Most of these women had immigrated from South Asia within the past two years and lacked local family or social support. The study also showed that most of the women born outside the U.S. were unaware of support services available to domestic violence victims or lacked social networks to seek help.



Alarmingly, the number of victims continues to rise. A 2010 study by the National Institute of Justice found that younger generations of immigrants from India and Pakistan are more likely to experience partner violence than earlier waves of female immigrants to the U.S.


Meanwhile, the pandemic has intensified this upward trend. Lockdowns forced victims to remain trapped with their abusive partners in increasingly helpless situations. Usual sources of support, such as neighbors and relatives, became powerless to help. A Boston-based organization, Saheli, reported that its volunteers assisted many English-limited South Asian American women in filing police reports.


2

A new country


While domestic violence exists across all populations, immigrant communities are particularly vulnerable, said gender equality activist Bindu Oomen Fernandes, executive director of Narika, a nonprofit dedicated to helping South Asian domestic violence survivors.


"Abuse becomes more severe when you're far from home," Fernandes said in a Zoom interview. "Imagine a woman who doesn't know anyone besides her husband, doesn't know the emergency number, can't access local resources, and fears being alone."


Abusive partners often exert control over victims economically and legally, such as by withholding immigration documents. Fernandes described numerous cases where husbands on H1-B visas withheld their wives' documents—a practice she calls "immigration abuse.".



Fernandes said: "We've spoken to victims who didn't even know their own visa status. When the abuser files for divorce, she realizes she has no documents and doesn't know where her children's passports are. She can't even plan an escape because her immigration status violates deportation rules."


Even if survivors manage to leave, where can they go? Visa restrictions and income inequality leave few options for South Asian American women.


Maria Arshaad, one of Narika's project managers, said: "Economic dependency is huge. When these women arrive in this country, even if they have degrees from back home, their qualifications often don't transfer, and visa issues may prevent them from finding jobs."


Domestic violence survivors cannot care for themselves or their children without economic independence. They also cannot afford essential legal and counseling services. For example, Shah continued living with her second husband for several months after their divorce.


"I worked at a beauty salon for $3 an hour," Shah said. "He worked at a local gas station. I didn't want to live with him, but he convinced me it was for saving money."


3

Cultural norms—the curse of generations


Beyond financial hardship, domestic violence reveals deeper, uglier inequalities within South Asian culture. Immigrant women often do not leave abusive marriages because they fail to recognize the abuse. Instead, harmful and aggressive behaviors are wrongly interpreted as expressions of love. Shah recalled that despite her second husband's threatening and degrading language, she "forgave him multiple times."


"I convinced myself he wasn't that bad," Shah said. "My first husband used to beat me, but this one only yelled."



According to Neelofer Chaudry, executive director of the Boston-based nonprofit Family Harmony Foundation, South Asian American victims are taught from childhood to keep abuse hidden and silent. Chaudry believes the idea that all South Asian women should be "strong" and endure suffering alone is deeply flawed.


"These women grew up in South Asian households trained not to voice personal opinions about family matters. They can't talk about it with anyone, not even relatives," Chaudry said, echoing the suffocating atmosphere in such families. "Because domestic violence is so taboo and shameful, victims internalize it and interpret it as—'What's wrong with me? Is it my fault I'm being abused?'"


The myth of Asian Americans as a model minority—portraying them as universally successful economically and familially—further silences victims. In a 2017 opinion column in New York magazine, political commentator Andrew Sullivan attributed Asian American "success" to maintaining "stable two-parent family structures." Chaudry argues that the assumption that South Asian American families are all "stable" and unified is problematic.



"We're struggling," Chaudry said. "Our community faces immense pressure to appear perfect. When we first started speaking out, we faced harsh criticism from other South Asians—we were called family destroyers, asked, 'Why are you exposing our family shame?' We're afraid to discuss what's considered a 'private matter' between couples." But abuse is never private, and speaking out is the community's responsibility. "


Organizations like the Family Harmony Foundation provide emotional support services for clients, with trained professionals helping survivors navigate conflicting emotions about their relationships. They also run annual youth leadership programs to empower new generations and challenge harmful social norms.


" Abuse tends to repeat itself, Chaudry added. "If a son sees his mother being abused, he's more likely to repeat the cycle. This is deeply rooted and widespread in society, which is why we want an opportunity to break this cycle. When survivors come together and share their experiences, they realize they are not alone."


4

Seeking help and moving forward


In 2017, after divorcing her second husband, Shah "nervously" reached out to Daya, a Houston-based nonprofit.. She had no income, her phone flooded with hysterical messages from her ex-husband, including many threatening and sexually explicit texts. After removing his name from the apartment lease and changing the locks, she faced further harassment.


" "Daya really helped me a lot," Shah said. "They first helped me obtain a restraining order against my husband, and later, after I reported him to the police, he went to prison. Daya made consistent efforts to communicate with me, helping me understand that I wasn't to blame—that this wasn't my fault."


Shah is a lucky exception. According to data from the U.S. National Library of Medicine, among South Asian women who reported domestic violence, only 11% actually received counseling services, and just 3% successfully obtained restraining orders against their partners. Daya's CEO, Rachna Khare, said these numbers are extremely low due to high levels of distrust and disillusionment within the South Asian American community.



Khare said in a Zoom interview: "It's unreasonable—even though there are immigration protections for women, in reality, they're very hard to implement. If you're married to an H1-B visa holder and dependent on him, you may need 6 to 7 years to obtain independent non-immigrant status. Can you really wait that long?"


Khare refers to nonimmigrant visas, which allow victims of crimes such as sexual assault, domestic violence, and human trafficking to remain in the United States. Although nonimmigrant visas are designed to protect the immigration status of all abuse survivors, only 10,000 such applications are approved each year. Those who are denied are reportedly to be given "priority consideration" next year, which is why many South Asian women who apply are expected to remain undocumented for years to come.


A report by the Center for Investigative Reporting found a history of law enforcement agencies across the country undermining nonimmigrant visa applications. They found that applications have declined since 2018 because "nearly a quarter of agencies created barriers never intended by the program," said Khare. The impact has been devastating—not just for survivors.


"Interestingly, people see this as charity, but in reality, it's about protecting the safety of your community," Khare said. "Abusers may commit other crimes. Their children are more likely to require interventions at school, diverting attention from other students. This is an investment in public safety—holding abusers accountable and allowing survivors to stay in our country and build better lives. "


5

After the violence,


three years ago, Shah was unemployed and living alone in a country she did not trust. Today, Shah is a certified beautician and a proud business owner. With Daya's help, she established her own beauty salon in Houston, pursuing her passion for the beauty industry.


"Daya truly helped me immensely, teaching me how to run a business. For example, helping me secure a business loan, teaching me how to manage my shop, find clients, and interact with people... The way they taught me was just like teaching a young elementary school student."



Although she has achieved economic independence, Shah's struggle continues. Her two children still live in Nepal, and she is working hard to obtain U.S. green cards for them. Having lived through 13 years of domestic violence, this experience has deeply shaped her views on South Asian marriage and culture.


" "Asian men need to learn to yield and compromise," Shah said. "Even my father and brothers never respected my mother. At the same time, (Asian) women need to speak up and build connections around them. I hope they realize how much power they truly hold. "


She ended the conversation on a hopeful note.


'I'm not afraid of anyone anymore,' Shah said with a smile. 'I feel like I'm soaring in the sky.'
