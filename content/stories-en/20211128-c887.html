Part 01. Introduction



"Why, after suffering excessive and cruel acts of violence, do victims still choose silence—or even blame themselves?"



On November 25, 2019, Yu Yan, a popular makeup blogger known for her realistic special-effects makeup, chose to speak out on Weibo on the International Day for the Elimination of Violence against Women, after enduring a year of domestic violence.

From Yu Yan's account and the videos she released, we learned that a blogger who normally posted heartwarming, cute animal videos transformed into a violent, dangerous scumbag who brutally beat her.

Most chilling of all, the elevator where the abuse occurred was a semi-public space, and the perpetrator—known publicly as a gentle, artistic, and good-hearted male photographer—was someone whose image stood in stark contrast to his actions. Even after enduring a year of abuse, Yu Yan continued to present only stunning makeup transformations and serene, beautiful daily life on her Weibo account with over a million followers, effectively "covering up the truth" together with her abuser.

If Yu Yan had not spoken up, this cruel farce—already repeated five times in plain sight—might have continued unnoticed in the shadows.

 

According to statistics from the All-China Women's Federation, by the end of 2019, 30% of the 270 million households in China had experienced domestic violence against married women; among victims of domestic violence, 9 out of 10 remained silent, and on average, women endured 35 incidents of abuse before choosing to report it.

If Yu Yan, who spoke up after five incidents of abuse, can already be considered one of the braver individuals among countless victims, then how many other women, suffering equally or even more severe domestic violence in silence, continue to endure innumerable atrocities without a word?

We cannot break through the barriers of every private space; exposure of domestic violence usually depends solely on the victims themselves. The persistence of abuse and the concealment of evil often stem not from a dark world or public indifference, but from the light within the victim's heart being extinguished by the abuser. Before sunlight from the outside can shine into these dark corners, only the victim can reignite the light within.

Breaking the silence, tearing open the darkness, and exposing evil to the light of day are crucial steps in confronting and escaping domestic violence.

Even as public awareness grows and legal systems improve, the shadow of domestic violence still lurks in unseen corners of intimate relationships, occurring within families that appear exemplary and harmonious from the outside, perpetrated by seemingly respectable monsters in human form, and enveloping countless wounded victims who continue to piece together a facade of strength. For both those trapped helplessly in the quagmire and the public who lack understanding and empathy, the question "Why can't she leave?" remains a bitter and complex one, its answer hidden alongside the abuse in obscure, shadowy corners.



Part 02. Main Body


(1)

Gaslighting


1. What is "gaslighting"?—Brainwashing under the guise of love


The 1944 thriller film *Gaslight* tells the story of Paula, a confident, sunny, and lively young woman who inherits a large fortune and is then deceived and mentally manipulated by her husband, Anton, a scumbag obsessed with her wealth, ultimately driving her to madness and stripping her of her identity.

In a classic scene from the film, the male lead Anton deliberately dims the gaslights in their home, making them flicker, and when the female lead expresses confusion, he insists the lights are fine and blames her for hallucinating, further eroding her ability to distinguish reality from illusion—thus giving the term "gaslighting" its name.

"Gaslighting," also known as "gaslight manipulation," refers to a perpetrator systematically distorting facts or repeatedly feeding false information to make the victim doubt themselves, gradually undermining their memory, judgment, and mental state, ultimately stripping them of self-esteem, confidence, and independent thinking, leaving them completely controlled by the abuser.

The people who most directly influence our emotions are often those closest to us, which is why this form of psychological manipulation frequently accompanies domestic violence, using love as a cover to destroy the victim's mental well-being, making them increasingly self-loathing and self-doubting, even prompting them to make excuses for their abusive partner.




2. Three Common Types of Perpetrators


(1) The Charmer—Hot-and-cold romance, a cycle of push and pull

Common among outwardly romantic, charismatic men, this type first subjects the victim to prolonged emotional neglect until she is on the verge of collapse, then suddenly showers her with carefully orchestrated romance, causing her to give in and surrender. He refuses to acknowledge his prior coldness or explain his unexplained disappearances, instead attributing the victim's pain and anxiety to her own sensitivity and fragility.

(2) The "Good Person"—Controlling your life under the guise of caring

Obsessed with interfering in and directing your life in ways they believe are "good for you," they may outwardly agree with your views while inwardly dismissing them, making you gradually take on the "responsibility" of meeting their expectations through repeated compromises and concessions, even becoming depressed from constant self-reflection.

(3) The Threatener—Arrogant belittlement and put-downs

Compared to the first two types, which are more deceptive and subtle, this type's behavior is glaringly obvious—through loud verbal abuse, relentless hostile questioning, periodic outbursts of rage, or repeated mockery under the guise of "just teasing," they exhaust the victim emotionally, making her feel worthless.

 


3. Three Stages of "Gaslight Manipulation"

Gaslight manipulation often progresses in a step-by-step manner, gradually eroding the victim's originally positive and healthy mental state, sometimes so subtly in the beginning that it goes unnoticed until it fully occupies their thoughts and controls their emotions. Victims typically go through the following three stages as they sink deeper into the trap:

(1 (1) Disbelief

When faced with the abuser's absurd, illogical, or bizarre accusations (e.g., "That man who asked for directions clearly had bad intentions toward you!"), the victim attempts to correct the falsehood or engage in serious debate. At this stage, the victim still believes in their own perspective, and although they instinctively seek the abuser's approval, they do not yet desperately crave it. However, after prolonged attempts at sincere explanation fail to convince the abuser and only result in more blame and criticism, the victim falls into persistent confusion, frustration, and anxiety, gradually beginning to doubt themselves—"Is this really his problem? Or is it mine?"

(2) Justification

The hallmark of this stage is that the victim begins to actively justify their words and actions, desperately seeking evidence to prove the abuser is wrong and fiercely arguing to gain their approval. At this point, conflicts and arguments with the abuser may increasingly dominate the victim's thoughts—they involuntarily replay these interactions throughout the day, and proving themselves and regaining approval become the central obsessions, the only path they see to restoring normalcy. In this stage, the victim may feel troubled by their own obsession, sometimes overwhelmed by despair, no longer certain whether approval can ever be won, but they have not yet completely given up hope.

(3) Suppression

In this stage, the victim fully internalizes the abuser's negative narratives, idealizes the abuser to an extreme degree, and desperately craves their approval, abandoning their authentic self-perception. Exhausted, the victim begins to "give up on themselves," actively trying to prove and believe the abuser is right, attempting to comply with the abuser's demands to gain approval and thus "escape" the endless, meaningless accusations and arguments—but this "escape" marks the final fall.

 "At this point, you can no longer see hope. You feel lost in a vast, unknown desert, with no map and no landmarks. You can barely remember the person you used to be. You only know something has gone terribly wrong—maybe you are the problem. After all, if you were a good person, a capable person, surely you would earn your partner's approval. Wouldn't you?"

"The most painful moment in life might be when you realize the former best version of yourself—the real you—is now out of reach. You've lost confidence, self-respect, independence, and courage. Worst of all, you've lost happiness. All you want is the gaslighter's approval."

"At this stage, you begin to understand: that is a goal you can never reach."

—Robin Skynner


(2)

Why Can't She Leave?


In real cases of domestic violence, in addition to "gaslight manipulation" that casts a shadow over the victim's once-bright life and plunges her into despair, there is often also brutal physical violence—psychological and physical abuse working in tandem, cycling repeatedly, reducing the victim to dust.

Through mere psychological suggestion and mental control, victims are ultimately driven to madness and lose their sense of self. To onlookers, this often seems incomprehensible; and when victims simultaneously endure both psychological abuse and physical violence, their long-term silence and continued endurance strike observers as instinctively unfathomable—evoking condescending pity for their misfortune and anger at their perceived lack of resistance.

Intimate relationships should never be a cover for violent behavior, and leaving after experiencing violence should be a shared public consensus. Before being forced into domestic violence, victims were once ordinary members of society—educated, capable of independent thought, and even economically independent. In other words, they actually know they are enduring unjust violence and suffering.

No one wants to escape more than the victim themselves. Then, within the endless cycle of domestic violence, how exactly do psychological control and physical abuse repeat themselves, trapping victims in a vortex from which they cannot break free?

1. Push-Pull Cycle

(1) Tension-Building Phase

During this stage, conflicts between the abuser and the victim accumulate. The atmosphere in the intimate relationship gradually becomes tense, substantive communication decreases, and the victim begins to feel anxious and fearful, attempting to appease the abuser.

(2) Explosion Phase

A particular disagreement or friction becomes the trigger, and the abuser begins to inflict verbal and physical violence on the victim, causing psychological and physical harm.

(3) Calm Phase

The abuser begins to express remorse, weeping bitterly, making excuses for their actions—blaming the victim or attributing the violence to "love"—begging the victim for forgiveness, promising it will never happen again, and even threatening suicide to force compliance.

"Tell me, kind person who longs for love and emotional support, would you really reject and push away someone who is willing to get angry for you, make decisions for you, become emotional for you, and even change for you?"

——[Yuanyuan | Anti-Domestic Violence Public Education Class]Lesson 7: Why Don't You Leave?

(4) Honeymoon Phase

When the victim expresses forgiveness, gradually lets go, and stops holding the abuser accountable, the intimate relationship appears to return to normal. The abuser seems to revert to their original gentle and considerate self, even actively trying to please the victim. Yet, the end is also the beginning: this peace and sweetness are destined to be fleeting and illusory. If the established control dynamic remains unbroken, as tensions continue to accumulate, the brief happiness becomes merely the prelude to the next act of violence.

 




2. Common Concerns

What prevents them from crying out for help?

(1) Dependency

Many women in intimate relationships are completely dependent on their partners for material life and social activities—lacking independent income, having no friends with whom they can share experiences or seek assistance, and even losing their means of survival if they leave the abuser.

(2) Self-Sacrifice


• Family Integrity

In Chinese marital culture, marriage often signifies the merging of two families. When children are present in the relationship, the victim's escape also means the breakup of the family. Many victims choose to break themselves in order to preserve the family's outward integrity.

• Partner's Reputation

Seeking help means exposure, and exposure may lead to public disgrace. Some victims, faced with the abuser's apologies and justifications, ultimately choose to suffer in silence out of a desire to "protect" the other person's social reputation and personal image.


(3) Intimidation and Threats

To prevent the victim from seeking help or escaping, abusers often threaten the safety of themselves, the victim, or other family members, warning that any attempt to resist or leave will result in unbearable consequences. Alternatively, they directly deny the meaning and value of seeking external help, indoctrinating the victim with the belief that "help-seeking is useless."

(4) Sunk Cost

The happy memories before the domestic violence began, and the brief moments of sweetness during the "honeymoon phase" of the abuse cycle, may become emotional ties that bind the victim. Recalling the abuser's past tenderness and reflecting on their own past sacrifices and endurance, victims may feel reluctant and unwilling to leave.

(5) Social Pressure

Facing external public opinion, victims fear being gossiped about, believing that "family shame should not be aired in public," and wrongly blaming themselves for the violence and relationship breakdown as a sign of personal failure.



(iii)

How to Save Yourself? 


Then, in the face of this recurring violence, how can victims break free from manipulation, let go of their concerns, and step out of the darkness?


1. Recognize Manipulation, Understand the Problem

After enduring prolonged abuse and manipulation, many victims fall into learned helplessness—adopting a passive mindset in response to continuous setbacks, no longer believing they have the ability or possibility to change their situation, and internalizing or even complying with the abuser's malice and violence. The victim's tolerance and obedience are essential factors that enable the abuser to continue their violent behavior. Only by correctly recognizing that one is undergoing psychological control can one begin to attempt to break free.


If you suspect you are being manipulated, you can use the following points for a simple self-check:

(1) Do you often fall into self-doubt, feeling that nothing you do is right, even unable to make decisions for yourself?

(2) Do you often evaluate yourself based on others' expectations? Do you repeatedly question whether you are a good partner, wife, or girlfriend? Do you "reflect" before every encounter with your partner, wondering if you've done something wrong?

(3) Do you often actively make excuses or justifications for your partner in front of family and friends?

(4) Do you feel persistently sad and hopeless, unable to feel joy, no matter how vibrant life may seem?

(5) Do you feel that you are gradually becoming a completely different person from who you once were—slowly losing all your former strengths and qualities?


How could someone who truly loves you bear to make you feel self-loathing? A healthy intimate relationship should never bring only negative emotions. If, in a relationship justified by love, you gradually feel unrecognizable, hopeless, and lose confidence, then the relationship is undoubtedly problematic—and you are certainly not the only one to blame. No one deserves unwarranted violence.

2. Empower Yourself, Rebuild Your Will

Throughout the gradual process of violence and control, abusers often use threats, verbal manipulation, psychological suggestion, and other methods to subtly strip victims of their right to make independent decisions, pushing them toward complete dependence and obedience. Once the problem is recognized, one should try to reclaim the right to choose and decide, rebuild self-awareness, stop using the abuser's emotions as the sole standard of judgment, and stop treating demands imposed by the other person as one's own subjective will or responsibility. Learn self-compassion, pay attention to your own feelings, love yourself before loving others, and stop exhausting yourself in pursuit of others' approval.


3. Reconnect with Society, Seek Help

Isolating victims from their normal social circles is a common reason why manipulation and violence can persist undetected. Due to the abuser's excessive control and baseless suspicion under the guise of love, victims are often forced to withdraw from their existing social networks, becoming estranged from former friends and reducing or even cutting off contact. Try to return to normalcy—reconnect with old friends, rebuild an independent and healthy social circle, share your experiences with friends, society, and the outside world, and seek help, thereby stepping out from the abuser's shadow.



Part 03. Conclusion

As public attention grows and legal systems gradually improve, the shadow of domestic violence still lurks in the unseen corners of intimate relationships—occurring within families that appear exemplary and perfect to outsiders, perpetrated by seemingly respectable individuals who are in fact beasts in human clothing, casting a dark cloud over countless victims who are battered yet still struggling to maintain strength. For both victims helplessly trapped in the quagmire and the public who lack understanding and thus fail to empathize, the question "Why can't they leave?" remains a bitter and complex one—its answer, like the violence itself, hidden in obscure and shadowy corners.

We cannot break through all the barriers of private spaces; in most cases, the exposure of domestic violence can only come from the victims themselves. The persistence and concealment of abuse often stem not from a dark world or public indifference, but from the light within the victim's heart being extinguished by the abuser. And before sunlight from the outside actively reaches these dark corners, the victim must first reignite the light within themselves.

No one willingly enters darkness; it is the abuser who casts the shadow over them, not the victims who choose the violence. For Paula, the protagonist of *Gaslight*, the flickering gaslight is merely a trick played by her husband Anton, not a sign of her mental instability. Once freed from Anton's control, she can return to her former cheerful and confident self. Likewise, for all women still struggling under the shadow of domestic violence, the darkness over their lives, the despair and helplessness they face, stem only from others' malice. Once they break free from the cycle of abuse, their future remains bright and full of light.

Without personal experience, how can we truly empathize? We who have never suffered domestic violence and live in the light may never fully understand the despair and struggle of survivors. Yet we still choose to stay attentive, confront the darkness, shine a light into shadowed corners, expose hidden acts of domestic abuse to the clear light of day, and help those shrouded in the gloom of violence return to the light.
