In Chinese culture, "mother" is always associated with words like diligence, resilience, endurance, and selfless giving. Mothers are the subjects of their children's memories, celebrated as embodiments of unconditional sacrifice.


As mothers, they seem never allowed to complain about the hardships of parenting; as mothers, they appear unable to establish their existence solely through this identity. The role of "mother," regarded as a woman's natural duty, is constantly placed on a pedestal while simultaneously subjected to relentless judgment.


This Mother's Day, we are not trying to define what a mother should be, but instead letting "mothers" tell their own stories.


Motherhood is a process of re-balancing one's identity. 


"There isn't really true balance," she said, "it's more about pushing yourself to the limit to get everything done."


It is a process of constant pulling, splitting, and re-integration. After becoming a mother, Jessica has continuously learned how to balance her identities.


In 2019, Jessica came to the United States, completed her studies, and began working in the field of mechanical packaging engineering. She had already accumulated years of relevant experience in China before that. Today, she is an engineer with over a decade of experience and the mother of two boys, aged 11 and 6. Life has not reserved clear boundaries between these two identities; instead, they constantly overlap and even conflict with each other.


Her day begins early with preparing breakfast. She cares about her children's dietary health and spends time preparing relatively rich meals; then she sends the kids to school and rushes to work; at noon, she finds time to pick them up and arrange childcare; after returning home from work in the evening, she continues handling household chores, and often works late into the night.


Time is sliced into extremely small fragments.


Surprisingly, she does not view work as a burden, but rather describes it as a comparatively light experience.


At work, she feels in control, experiences a sense of achievement, and receives recognition—this is a domain she is familiar with, skilled in, and through which she can define herself.


She specializes in packaging design for large mechanical equipment. For machines weighing dozens of tons, every aspect—from pallet structure and transport safety to loading efficiency, strength, and cost control—must be precisely calculated in the design. In addition to whole-machine packaging, she also handles complex and diverse components, designing specific structural solutions for different parts.


This work is concrete and direct. She has participated in over a hundred projects, transitioning from traditional packaging relying on foam and wood to increasingly eco-friendly solutions represented by corrugated structures, significantly reducing material consumption while improving efficiency and safety.


More importantly, the results are "visible." She can see her designs being used in workshops, see problems being solved, and see previously doubted proposals gradually gaining recognition. Suppliers who once questioned the feasibility of her designs began re-evaluating her capabilities after the projects were successfully implemented.


In contrast, the family sphere she experiences is far more complex and challenging.


"Children are much more exhausting than work," she said, "it's not just about tasks—it's mental drain."


She admits that in the process of raising her children, she has not always been the ideal mother.


She loses patience when tired, gets angry at her children, and afterward feels intense guilt. She recalls her childhood judgments of her own mother—those moments of harshness and emotional outbursts she once couldn't understand—now, as a mother herself, they have become understandable.


"Now I realize that many parents are actually living in a state of chronic overload."


This understanding is not about forgiving the past, but about empathy weighted with real-life experience. But balance does not mean eliminating conflict—it means learning to adjust your position within it.


She began trying to change the way she interacts with her children—not just demanding they express their emotions, but using stories and metaphors to help them gradually connect with their feelings; she also started allowing herself to show vulnerability in front of her children, admitting "I'm really tired right now. I didn't do well just now. You need to help Mom."


In this process, her children actually grew closer to her. They comfort her, hug her, and respond to her emotions when she breaks down.


In those moments, she realized that a mother doesn't need to be constantly stable—relationships themselves matter more than perfection. She is striving to become a mother who is still growing and who allows herself to be incomplete.


Perhaps so-called balance is never something completed, but rather a continuous process of losing and reassembling oneself, bit by bit.

"I came into this world to experience happiness."


For Li Caixi, becoming a mother was not part of her original plan—at least not so "suddenly."


During school, she performed as an underground idol, standing on stage and being seen by many people. Challenging herself completely was, in her view, her first breakthrough. After graduation, she returned to China and joined a foreign enterprise. Everything became smooth and clear. Regarding these experiences, Li Caixi feels she has been relatively fortunate.


The arrival of her child was unexpected. She was 25 at the time. The once-clear vision of her future became shaky and blurred because of this unexpected life.


"If I had an abortion, I might never be able to take a peaceful shower for the rest of my life."


At that moment, she realized she had already made her decision, "Give birth to this child!" Li Caixi's first daughter was born in 2019. When the warm, soft life landed in her arms, her maternal instincts surged—she wanted to do everything possible to care for her. That year, she quit her job and chose to become a full-time mother.


"I feel that for this little life, I am willing to exchange my labor and effort, so that she can have this experience: to walk through the world. I think it's absolutely worth it."


When a woman becomes a mother for the first time, she often looks back at the image of "mother" from her own childhood.


Maintaining a sense of self is where she resembles her own mother. But in certain aspects, she has decided to be a mother opposite to her own.


When her child cries and fusses after wetting their pants, instead of scolding the still-unsophisticated child to stop crying immediately, she chooses a gentler yet stronger approach: "You're just a little baby. This is completely normal." For her, childbirth is not about burning herself out, but a form of exchange. Yet as a mother, there are always costs that must be paid. Over time, she fell into a panic of blurred self-boundaries. Time was infinitely divided, yet not a single second belonged to her: when the child cried, she changed diapers; when hungry, she cooked; when clothes got dirty, she washed them. Caught between the roles of wife and mother, she gradually lost her sense of self.


She recalls one time suddenly realizing she hadn't looked in the mirror for a long while. Looking at her reflection, she knew it was a sign of self-disappearance.


"The deeper pain is actually being trapped in the present. What stops you from jumping out is your own imagination."


She doesn't want to describe her time as a full-time mother as mere suffering—there were certainly many moments that brightened her days. But the underlying pain is like endless candle wax, the shadow behind every mother, something that needs to be spoken of. Many people are trapped in this daily routine, confused by their own unease. "You're supposed to feel that way." To mothers still stuck in daily life, Li Caixi says, find a way to step out.


The scenery during the commute


At age 29, she returned to school to complete her master's degree and rekindle her professional aspirations. Today, she has a job she feels satisfied with, a family she cherishes, and two lives she is fully supporting as they continue to explore the world.


Motherhood is a unique and extraordinary experience belonging only to women.


The day we scheduled the interview with Lily, she had just received a master's offer from her dream school.


She describes this current period as her "gap" time: the children are on vacation, she can travel with the whole family, and help her soon-to-be senior son prepare university application materials. Though she calls it a gap, to Lily, it feels like it will pass in the blink of an eye.


In 2021, Lily moved to the UK with her children, becoming what people online call a "study companion mother." Providing international education for their children had long been Lily and her husband's shared goal. That year, her eldest son was in 7th grade and her daughter in 6th. Her husband's business was primarily in South Korea and Japan, leaving him unable to leave, so the responsibility of accompanying the children to study in the UK naturally fell on mother Lily.


Being a study companion mother is not as light or easy as the term sounds.


Upon arriving in the UK, Lily lived in a hotel with her two children for a month. At the time, she had no friends in London and poor English skills. From renting an apartment and choosing schools to setting up gas, electricity, and water, she figured everything out entirely on her own.


"If I'm too tired today, I'll leave it and continue figuring it out tomorrow. That's how I got through it—one thing at a time."


Lily doesn't think this experience caused her pain; among the study companion mothers she knows, many have had similar experiences. Facing an unfamiliar environment and a daily pile of to-dos, while watching her two children, as a mother she occasionally had thoughts like "I want to kill myself," feeling ashamed of how "stupid" she was, unable to even set up a WiFi connection without great difficulty.


After finding stable housing in London, Lily enrolled both children in boarding schools. She got through all of this entirely on her own.


Having a happy family is certainly fulfilling for women, but Lily also has her own unfinished dreams. Going back to university has always been Lily's dream.


She met her husband while studying abroad in Japan, and in Lily's own words, she was a bit of a "love-struck" girl back then. Unable to bear being apart from her husband, she dropped out of university in her second year and returned to China to start a family with him.


Starting in 2017, due to her husband's career development, Lily's entire family moved to Japan. During that time, in order for her children to adapt to the foreign environment, she gave up her own jewelry brand and devoted herself entirely to her family. She stood alone confronting the school principal on behalf of her bullied son, and stayed awake all night caring for her sick child.


It wasn't until she sent her children to their respective boarding schools in the UK—leaving her with five free days a week—that Lily finally regained rare moments of solitude. "I just didn't want to waste my time, so I decided to pursue an undergraduate degree, to go back to university."


Lily taking photos on campus during her studies


Even though many people view accompanying children abroad for education as a mother's sacrifice and hardship, Lily feels that her own opportunity has finally arrived. "It's time to fulfill this dream of mine." From foundation year to university admission, Lily never hid the fact that she was a mother of two. Returning to campus in her forties, she has become a "proud figure" the whole family loves to talk about.


Lily's graduation ceremony


Last year, Lily graduated from the University of Roehampton with a degree in Business Management. On graduation day, her daughter and son bought flowers and came together to celebrate with her. Lily's children always introduce the fact that "my mom is in university" to those around them with a sense of pride. In their eyes, Lily is a supermom who can do anything.
