Origin and Introduction of the Day 


On 25 November 1960, Rafael Trujillo, the dictator of the Dominican Republic, brutally murdered the Mirabal sisters—political activists—in an effort to eliminate opposition and consolidate power. After the end of Trujillo's 30-year dictatorship, to honor these three "eternal butterflies,", in July 1981, the first Latin American Feminist Congress declared 25 November—the day the Mirabal sisters were killed—as a day against violence, to commemorate their resistance against tyranny and violence, and to help countless women living under the shadow of domestic violence transform the shame of "family disgrace should not be made public" into the courage to firmly say "no" to violence. In 1993,


the United Nations issued the*Declaration on the Elimination of Violence against Women*, which defined the scope of "violence against women" as "any gender-based violence that results in, or is likely to result in, physical, sexual or psychological harm or suffering to women, including threats of such acts, coercion or arbitrary deprivation of liberty, whether occurring in public or in private life." The *Declaration* also stated that domestic violence against women constitutes a violation of women's human rights and fundamental freedoms, and is a result of gender inequality. In 1995,


the Fourth World Conference on Women was held in Beijing, where opposition to domestic violence became a major theme and a key component of the adopted Platform for Action. On 5 November 1999,


with a proposal from the Dominican Republic and support from over 60 countries, the 54th session of the United Nations General Assembly officially adopted Resolution 134, designating 25 November each year as the "International Day for the Elimination of Violence against Women," also known as the (Day For the elimination of Violence against Women) " "International Day for the Elimination of Domestic Violence." The day aims to eliminate violence against women, serving as a mechanism to remind governments of their responsibility to end such violence—a global action to prevent tragedies. This decision by the UN General Assembly represents both a milestone in the international community's long-standing efforts against gender-based violence and a starting point for deeper action. To date, this day and its related campaigns continue to be used by individuals and organizations worldwide as an organizing strategy to advocate for, prevent, and eliminate violence against women and girls.

International Day for the Elimination of Violence against Women

—2021 Overview

In 2020, the theme for the "International Day for the Elimination of Violence against Women" was:


"Orange the World: Fund, Respond, Prevent, Collect"


The UN Women's Generation Equality campaign

Orange the World:

Fund, Respond, Prevent, Collect!


called for global action to close funding gaps, ensuring essential services for survivors of violence during the pandemic. To improve rescue services for women and girls, greater emphasis was placed on prevention and data collection. Correspondingly, numerous iconic buildings and landmarks were illuminated in orange to raise awareness of the need for a violence-free future. As in previous years,


the 2021 International Day will mark the launch of the "16 Days of Activism against Gender-Based Violence," a campaign that runs annually from 25 November, the "International Day for the Elimination of Violence against Women," to 10 December, "Human Rights Day." Since 1991,


the campaign has been initiated by activists from the first Global Feminist Leadership Institute, with participation from approximately 187 countries, (Women's Global Leadership Institute) over 6,000 organizations, and involving 300 million people. This campaign is annually coordinated by the Center for Women's Global Leadership (CWGL) and used by individuals, institutions, and organizations worldwide as an organizing strategy to call for the prevention and elimination of violence against women and girls. Even before the pandemic, violence against women was already a global epidemic and a major concern. However, official reports showed a misleading "illusion" of declining domestic violence statistics. For example, the head of the Women's Secretariat at Guatemala's Ministry of Public Affairs stated that since the pandemic began, daily reports of domestic violence in Guatemala dropped from an average of


150 to 55 per day. This decline in reports has raised greater concern, as victims found it harder to seek help and support through regular channels. Reduced interaction with the outside world, economic pressure, and extreme social isolation have made women and girls more vulnerable to exploitation, abuse, forced marriage, and harassment when facing sudden drops in living standards. Therefore, recently, 135 countries have strengthened their actions and resource investments to combat violence against women, yet much more is still needed. In 2021, marking the 30th anniversary of the Day against Violence,.


the situation was different. Violence against women, primarily domestic violence, intensified as city-wide lockdowns and home isolation measures continued. Closed doors kept out the virus but also silenced victims' cries for help. The rampant spread of the coronavirus not only put normal human life on pause but also made violence occurring in private spaces invisible to the wider world. Statistics show that the global number of domestic violence cases increased by 20% year-on-year, and calls to domestic violence helplines


increased fivefold. In addition to the pandemic, ongoing crises caused by natural disasters such as the Haiti earthquake and human-made conflicts have significantly increased global violence against women. Protecting women's equal rights and eliminating violence against women is crucial! Therefore, under the leadership of the United Nations, the theme for the 2021 International Day for the Elimination of Violence against Women was set as: "Orange the World: End violence against women now!" with a focus on the connections between crisis-related, rapidly changing environments and all forms of violence against women.

Related Activities and Legal Protection


Under the attention and leadership of the United Nations, more individuals and organizations are increasingly recognizing violence against women and taking spontaneous action.


In 2015, a feminist-led, anti-violence march took place widely across the United States, initiated by Spanish artist Alexsandro Palombo, who applied makeup to eight Hollywood celebrities to depict them as blank-eyed, battered domestic violence survivors, aiming to encourage women to break the silence and oppose domestic violence through performance art as a form of protest.


Behind the domestic violence makeup lies a cruel reality; through grotesque and shocking visual techniques, it delivers a powerful warning about real-life violence..


In addition, more and more members of the public have also taken spontaneous action to express the helplessness and pain women face from unwarranted violence. In 2019, an online campaign named "I Don't Want to Die" was launched on foreign social media platforms, where netizens shared over 9,000 photos recounting their personal experiences and stories of severe trauma inflicted on themselves, their children, and families.


In China, on December 27, 2015, during the 18th Session of the Standing Committee of the 12th National People's Congress, the Anti-Domestic Violence Law was passed and promulgated by President Xi Jinping through Presidential Decree No. 37. This law is also one of the few complete laws in mainland China that underwent only two readings and was "rapidly passed." The establishment of relevant laws and regulations


signifies that state intervention has built a legal system for prevention and response, meaning domestic violence is no longer seen merely as a private family matter. The introduction of China's Anti-Domestic Violence Law was also hard-won. Due to China's unique historical and cultural background, the concept of male superiority and female inferiority is difficult to eliminate from people's minds overnight, which determines the particularity and complexity of Chinese family relationships. Summary and Preview


Violence, in whatever form, is not unfamiliar to any culture; it looms like a persistent dark cloud over intimate relationships. "Women exist, so their voices can be heard." This sentence comes from the opening of a documentary. In the film, "Woman" over 2,000 women from more than 50 countries appear on screen—women of different ages, skin colors, and ethnicities—quietly sharing, with varying degrees of openness, the hidden, interconnected fates among women. Before the camera, they stand as subjects—dignified and inviolable—yet each of their testimonies poses a question to the lens, to viewers, and to society at large: What is the true situation of women in this world? One of the "shes" in the documentary broke the Guinness World Record for the longest triathlon—swimming over 100 miles, cycling over 2,000 miles, and running over 700 miles, yet she still cannot confront the fear from that moment. Behind this seemingly resilient face lies a terrifying, unknown memory, as she is a survivor of sexual violence and human trafficking.



In both developing and developed countries, the suffering and brutality women face are widespread.


In India,


a woman was attacked at night by her husband, who threw sulfuric acid on her, burning half her face and causing her to lose one eye—simply because her dowry at marriage was insufficient; in the Middle East, women must not only evade war and conflict but also remain constantly vigilant against the risks of sexual assault and assassination; even in seemingly highly civilized regions like Europe and North America, underground human trafficking networks remain rampant. It is hard to imagine these are still events occurring in the 21st century, but this is the reality.


Silence makes everything possible; violence grows in silence. Laws teach women to speak up and resist, but not all cries for help are heard or seen. In some countries and regions around the world, even laws protecting women have yet to be fully established. Women are the primary victims of domestic violence, and violence against women is rooted in structures of gender inequality. Simone de Beauvoir (January 9, 1908 – April 14, 1986),



one of the founders of the feminist movement in France, using existentialist philosophy, proposed her famous, "Other" theory: "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman." It is under the dominance of male consciousness that the "immanence" of women is formed. However, the victims of patriarchy are not only women. There is no predetermined essence, no fixed destiny. If we are to discuss differences between men and women, only biological differences exist; spiritually and mentally, the two sexes are equal, and thus there is no justification for one gender to commit violence against the other. Sometimes, holidays are created to commemorate and perpetuate present-day beauty; but sometimes, holidays exist merely to remind people to do things they could equally do on ordinary days, without pretending these acts are uniquely meaningful.
