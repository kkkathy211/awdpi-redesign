The first week of August 2023 marked the 32nd World Breastfeeding Week, with this year's theme being "Supporting Working Families to Promote Breastfeeding," emphasizing the need for workplaces to strengthen support for breastfeeding. The social benefits of empowering working women cannot be overlooked. In the United States, the power of working mothers has become evident: this August, women with young children drove the rise in female employment rates and contributed to economic recovery. Further efforts to empower working mothers include advocating for the measurement of unpaid care work—including childcare—at the United Nations Global Forum on Gender Statistics to promote corresponding policy development.


Women long overlooked across various industries are also being called for greater attention. In science, "Lost Women of Science" highlights the "unsung heroines" of the Manhattan Project, shedding light on female contributions long obscured in scientific history—among them, Chinese-American physicist Chien-Shiung Wu, who first confirmed Fermi's theory of beta decay in 1933, has re-entered public discussion. In the arts, the recent publication of a new biography of Anna May Wong, a highly influential Chinese-American actress of the 20th century, reminds us of the racism and gender discrimination Asian women face overseas.



I. Gender Equality

1. "Lost Women of Science" Highlights the "Unsung Heroines" of the Manhattan Project


On August 10, The Washington Post published an article titled "The Women Scientists Who Worked on the Atomic Bomb Are Mostly Absent From the Film 'Oppenheimer,'" written by Katie Hafner and Amy Scharf, creators and co-executive producers of "Lost Women of Science." Launched in 2021, "Lost Women of Science" is a podcast series that has so far told the stories of hundreds of women scientists. Its new season, "Lost Women of the Manhattan Project," aims to spotlight the hidden contributions of women in the Manhattan Project.


Hafner and Scharf wrote that the film Oppenheimer does not reveal how women scientists played crucial roles in the Manhattan Project during World War II—the effort to build the first atomic bomb. Though these women never became household names, they were undoubtedly leaders in their fields: Hafner and Scharf introduced Maria Goeppert Mayer, a theoretical physicist who won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1963, and Chien-Shiung Wu, the Chinese-American physicist who first confirmed Fermi's theory of beta decay in 1933.

2. The 32nd World Breastfeeding Week: "Supporting Working Families to Promote Breastfeeding"


August 1–7, 2023 marked the 32nd World Breastfeeding Week, with this year's theme being "Supporting Working Families to Promote Breastfeeding." On August 3, to celebrate the occasion, over two thousand women in Colombia gathered in the capital city of Bogotá to participate in a public breastfeeding event.


Globally, the rate of exclusive breastfeeding has increased by 10 percentage points over the past decade, reaching 48%. UNICEF and the World Health Organization emphasize that to maintain and further increase this rate, and to achieve the global target of 70% exclusive breastfeeding by 2030, workplaces must significantly strengthen their support for breastfeeding.

3. Ecuador's First Round of Presidential Election: Female Candidate Luisa González Leads


On August 20, Ecuador held its presidential election. According to Xinhua News Agency on August 21, after counting over 90% of the votes, Luisa González, candidate of the left-wing party Citizen Revolution, led with 33.31% of the vote and is poised to become the country's first female president.


Luisa González stands out for her focus on education, healthcare, and economic development. She has proposed innovative policies and programs to strengthen the education system, ensure access to high-quality healthcare, and encourage entrepreneurship and job creation.


Ecuador's constitution stipulates that a presidential candidate wins outright in the first round if they receive over 50% of the vote, or at least 40% with a lead of more than 10 percentage points over the second-place candidate. Since no candidate met these conditions in the first round, Ecuador will hold a second-round presidential election on October 15.

4. Japanese Author with Severe Disability, Saeko Ichikawa, Wins Akutagawa Prize for "Hunchback"


On August 25, 2023, the 169th Akutagawa and Naoki Prize award ceremony was held in Tokyo. Saeko Ichikawa, author of Hunchback—a novel centered on a woman with severe disabilities—won the Akutagawa Prize. In her speech, she called for the creation of a "reading without barriers" environment so that people with or without disabilities can all access reading, and urged continued efforts to improve reading accessibility.


Regarding her award-winning work, she said she wrote the book out of anger toward "publishers who ignored my plea letters," and added that she would strive her best to "transform from an angry writer into a loving one."


Saeko Ichikawa was born in 1979. According to an interview in the September issue of Bungei Shunju and other sources, she was diagnosed at age 10 with congenital myopathy, a persistent muscle disorder characterized by muscle weakness from infancy that persists throughout life; she has been bedridden with a respirator since age 14.


She began primarily writing women's light novels at age 20 and has submitted works to the Bungakukai New Writer's Prize for over two decades. Her first literary work, Hunchback, won the 128th Bungakukai New Writer's Prize in 2023, followed by the Akutagawa Prize.

5. U.S. Employment Rate for Women Aged 25–54 Returns to Pre-Pandemic Levels


When the COVID-19 pandemic hit the labor market and millions of women lost their jobs, the United States experienced its first female recession: for the first time, women lost more jobs than men. Economists feared it might take decades for women's economy to recover, but just three years later, recovery has arrived. Women aged 25 to 54—the prime working-age group—are leading this recovery, advancing into new careers, striving for higher pay and better benefits, and benefiting from greater workplace acceptance of remote work, which often allows them flexibility in managing caregiving responsibilities.


In August, the labor force participation rate for U.S. women aged 25 to 54 reached 77.6%, compared to 77% in February 2020 before the economic collapse. This figure represents the proportion of women in this age group who are either employed or actively seeking employment, and is a key indicator of economic health. It also marks a milestone: since April this year, the group's employment rate has remained at or above 77.5%, setting a historical high. Evidence also suggests this rebound is largely driven by women with young children. According to a study by the Brookings Institution, the labor force participation rate for women with children under five is approximately 70.4%, a record high.


Jocelyn Frye, President of the National Partnership for Women & Family, said: "It's not the economy that's resilient—it's women. In this economy, women are the resilient ones. I never underestimate women's ability, especially women of color, who are fundamentally able to withstand short-term shocks and turn crises into opportunities. Our economy is built to a large extent on women's efforts."

6. United Nations Global Forum on Gender Statistics: Measuring Unpaid Care Work to Inform Policy


On August 29, at the ninth United Nations Global Forum on Gender Statistics held in Sandton, Johannesburg, participants emphasized the importance of measuring unpaid care work to develop policies addressing this unrecognized labor domain.


Participants noted that while collecting gender statistics incurs costs, the cost of not collecting data will have long-term consequences. Existing data show that women bear the greatest burden of unpaid care work, including childcare, eldercare, and care for the sick and disabled.


Forum speakers stressed the necessity of demographic and gender statistics in formulating strong policies to support care work. Francesca Grum, Assistant Director of the United Nations Statistics Division, said unpaid care work accounts for approximately 40% of global GDP. She further pointed out that unpaid care work remains a heavy burden on women, as 70% of women engage in such work, while men's participation remains relatively low.

7. Impact of War on Ukrainian Women's Precarious Employment


An interview article published on August 29 by Presse-toi À Gauche stated that Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine has had a significant negative impact on Ukrainian women's precarious and insecure employment. The war has led to widespread job loss and income decline among women. Due to the temporary and informal nature of their work, they are easily dismissed by employers. Finding new jobs is also difficult, especially for older women. Working conditions have deteriorated: some employers exploit the war to strip workers of rights, reduce wages and benefits. Many women are forced to continue working during air raids without access to shelters.


The article points out that precarious employment has deprived Ukrainian women of the state support and protection they are entitled to during wartime. The lack of formal work contracts makes it difficult for them to access unemployment benefits or relocation assistance. Nevertheless, some women have begun fighting for their labor rights and, in certain cases, achieved improvements. The article emphasizes the need for further measures to protect the rights of women in precarious work, requiring joint efforts from the government, research institutions, media, and workers.

8. Two years after Taliban takeover, suicide rates among Afghan women rise


The Guardian reported on August 28 that hospital and mental health clinic data from public hospitals in Afghanistan show a worrying surge in the number of women attempting or dying by suicide since the Taliban took power in the summer of 2021. The Taliban authorities have not released suicide data and have banned medical staff from sharing updated statistics. Some healthcare workers have taken risks to privately share data from August 2021 to August 2022, highlighting an urgent public health crisis.


These data indicate that Afghanistan has become one of the few countries in the world where more women die by suicide than men. This is clearly linked to the severe restrictions imposed on Afghan women's lives. Many women face forced marriages, domestic violence, and bleak prospects. UN officials and human rights activists have already warned of the sharp rise in women's suicides, explicitly connecting it to the Taliban's restrictions on every aspect of women's lives. This despair may be the last attempt for women, who have lost all power, to express protest and dissent.

2. Series of home invasions targeting elderly Asian families reported in Oakland, California


Law enforcement in Oakland, California reported that approximately 50 home invasions have targeted elderly Asian families in Oakland, potentially linked to a small group of criminals.


In one case, an 80-year-old man and his 69-year-old wife, who were caring for two grandchildren, were attacked when robbers broke into their second floor. One robber guarded the couple downstairs, pointing a gun at them for nearly 40 minutes—an ordeal of terrifying torture. Meanwhile, another robber searched upstairs, trying to open a safe and rummaging through closets and bedrooms for valuables. Ultimately, the criminals escaped with $10,000 in cash and jewelry. The family had previously experienced three other home invasions, and after each theft, they implemented new security measures—fences, cameras, and dogs—but these proved ineffective.

4. Sacramento, US: Man who admitted robbing four Asian women sentenced to eight years in prison


According to a press release from the Sacramento County District Attorney's Office on August 28, a young man who admitted to robbing four Asian women in Sacramento last year was sentenced to eight years in prison. On August 8, the 21-year-old suspect pleaded guilty in court to three counts of robbery, one count of grand theft, two firearm charges, and four hate crime charges. In addition to serving eight years in prison, the man must also pay restitution to the victims.


The 21-year-old man and a juvenile repeatedly traveled from Fairfield to Sacramento to rob Asian women leaving stores along Stockton Boulevard and 65th Street. In these incidents, the suspect served as the driver. The juvenile would sometimes get out with a gun, forcibly taking victims' wallets, phones, cash, and other valuables. The firearms were never recovered. On one occasion, the suspect followed a victim to her home and stole her phone inside the garage.


During the investigation, detectives from the Sacramento Police Department found communications between the suspect and the juvenile indicating they targeted Asian women based on racial bias. This key evidence enabled the District Attorney's Office to classify the case as a hate crime.

III. Asian Voices

1. Chinese American writer Yunte Huang publishes new book "Anna May Wong and Her Encounter with American History"


On August 22, renowned Chinese American writer and Professor of English at the University of California, Santa Barbara, Yunte Huang, released his new book Daughter of the Dragon: Anna May Wong's Rendezvous with American History.


Anna May Wong was the first Chinese American actress to achieve major influence in the 20th century. In addition to her outstanding contributions to the entertainment industry, she was also an active advocate for the China Relief Fund, a charitable initiative aimed at providing aid and support to China during World War II. Wong used her fame to support this critical humanitarian cause during a turbulent historical period.


Published by W. W. Norton, this new book describes Wong's life and career, detailing the racism, sexism, and ageism she faced throughout her short life. It traces her journey from Chinatown to Hollywood's silent film era, from Berlin during the Weimar Republic to Shanghai on the eve of World War II. As the book reveals, Wong crossed paths with many remarkable historical figures, including Walter Benjamin and Marlene Dietrich, both of whom were captivated by her.

2. California passes anti-caste discrimination SB403 bill


On August 28, the California State Legislature passed SB403, making California the first U.S. state to enact an anti-caste discrimination law. This legislation aims to amend anti-discrimination laws to eliminate bias against marginalized communities.


The bill's sponsor, California State Senator Aisha Wahab, emphasized the importance of preventing caste discrimination within organizations and companies. Earlier this year, she stated: "We want to ensure that organizations and companies do not reinforce caste discrimination in their practices or policies. To do so, we need to make it clear that discrimination based on caste is illegal."


"The legislative vote is a victory years in the making. After over 700 advocacy meetings across California, there has been a loud call for caste equity. As a Californian who has endured caste discrimination my entire life, I deeply understand the unjust struggles and adversity faced by those affected," said Thenmozhi Soundararajan, Executive Director of Equality Labs.


Tarina Mand of the South Asian Bar Association Civil Rights Taskforce said California has taken a bold step toward eliminating caste discrimination—a call for fairness and a recognition that deep-seated biases can be passed down through generations and across continents within communities.

3. In the U.S., Asians have the highest exposure to cancer-linked "forever chemicals"


A new study published in August found that Asian Americans are more exposed to cancer-linked "forever chemicals"—per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS)—than any other racial group.


According to the study, the median level of PFAS in Asian Americans is 88% higher than in white Americans. Although researchers found that individuals with higher incomes generally have a higher "exposure burden," or cumulative exposure, to these substances, Asian Americans still show higher exposure levels even when controlling for socioeconomic status.


Anna Reade, PFAS lead scientist at the Natural Resources Defense Council, said cultural and dietary factors may contribute to this disparity, noting that freshwater fish are often associated with PFAS contamination. According to a 2017 report published in Environmental Health Perspectives, Asians have the highest fish consumption among all major racial groups.

4. Chinese Malaysian writer Tan Twan Eng's novel The House of Doors longlisted for the Booker Prize


On the afternoon of 1 August, local UK time, the Booker Prize announced this year's longlist, featuring 13 novels. Among them is The House of Doors by Tan Twan Eng, a Chinese-Malaysian author born in Malaysia.


Set during Britain's colonial rule over Malaya, The House of Doors draws on the life and works of W. Somerset Maugham, telling a story of forbidden love and eroding authority against the backdrop of revolution and empire.


Tan Twan Eng is a familiar name to the Booker Prize and is the first author whose entire body of published novels have been shortlisted. His debut novel, The Gift of Rain, was longlisted in 2007, and in 2012, his novel The Garden of Evening Mists was shortlisted for the International Booker Prize.
