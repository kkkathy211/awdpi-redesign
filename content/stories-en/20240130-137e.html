Research Background and Objectives




The healthcare industry in the United States, as a key component of the broader healthcare services sector, currently has a large and continuously growing market. In particular, the home healthcare services market is projected to expand from USD 90.47 billion in 2022 to USD 156.28 billion by 2030. This growth is primarily driven by services such as nursing care, physician care, and language therapy, among which nursing care holds the largest market share (Fortune Business Insights, 2020). The growing elderly population in the U.S., especially the significant increase in individuals aged 65 and above, has intensified the demand for care services. This demographic faces challenges from chronic conditions such as osteoarthritis, diabetes, and dementia, thereby driving the need for long-term care services (Samuels, 2023). In this context, minority caregivers, particularly Asian, Black, and Hispanic caregivers, play a crucial role in providing long-term care services, accounting for 37.3% of all caregivers in the U.S. (Zippia, n.d.).


Despite the significant presence of minority caregivers in the U.S. care industry, their unique challenges and needs have not received adequate attention or support, and currently, domestic non-profit organizations show relatively little concern for this group.. Caregivers are primarily responsible for assisting patients with daily living activities such as cleaning, hygiene, eating, and dressing, and serve as primary caregivers in nursing and residential care facilities (Nurse.org Staff, 2023). Among caregivers in the U.S., minority caregivers often face challenges such as low wages, poor working conditions, and limited career advancement. During the COVID-19 pandemic, Asian American caregivers in the U.S. experienced racial discrimination, including threats and microaggressions, highlighting the urgent need for greater recognition and protection of this group (Shang et al., 2021). Our research aims to raise public awareness of this group, deepen understanding of the difficulties they face, and explore potential actions that individuals, businesses, social institutions, and governments can take in response. Additionally, this study seeks to enrich research by domestic non-profit organizations on this population, particularly regarding the specific needs and challenges faced by minority caregivers (Nurse.org Staff, 2023; Tyler et al., 2021; Zippia, n.d.).

1

Overview of the U.S. Healthcare Industry and Its Workforce


The U.S. healthcare industry belongs to the broader U.S. healthcare services sector and occupies the largest market share within this sector. Currently, the U.S. healthcare services industry has a substantial market size, and it continues to grow steadily. According to the U.S. Home Healthcare Services Market Size report, the market was valued at USD 90.47 billion in 2022 (Fortune Business Insights, 2020), and is expected to grow from USD 96.08 billion in 2023 to USD 156.28 billion by 2030 (Fortune Business Insights, 2020). This market includes physician care, nursing care, physical, occupational, and speech therapy, and medical social services, among which nursing care holds the largest market share. (Fortune Business Insights, 2020).



The primary recipients of nursing care in the U.S. are individuals aged 65 and older. The U.S. elderly population is large and significantly increasing, greatly stimulating demand for home caregivers.. According to 2022 data from the World Bank Group, people aged 65 and above account for approximately 17% of the total U.S. population (Fortune Business Insights, 2020), a proportion expected to rise as the elderly population grows. With the aging population, the prevalence of age-related diseases such as osteoarthritis, diabetes, and dementia is also increasing. Prior to this, these conditions were major causes of death among the elderly. According to data from the American Psychological Association (APA), chronic diseases such as heart disease, cancer, stroke, and diabetes cause nearly two-thirds of all deaths annually among Americans aged 65 and above (Fortune Business Insights, 2020). In addition to the rising elderly population and the need for chronic disease care, changing patient preferences and the integration of telehealth technologies have further driven market growth.


The industry has a high demand for long-term caregivers. Among adults aged 65 and older, 70% require long-term care, with an average care duration of 3.2 years; over 20% of elderly individuals require care for five years or more (Samuels, 2023). In terms of gender, long-term care recipients are predominantly female. This is first due to the longer life expectancy of women compared to men—79.1 years versus 73.2 years, respectively (Samuels, 2023). According to data from the U.S. Administration on Aging, as of 2020, the ratio of women to men in the population aged 85 and older was 178:100 (Samuels, 2023). Second, because elderly men are more likely to receive care from their spouses at home, women are more likely to enter long-term care facilities. The average length of stay for women in long-term care facilities is 3.7 years, compared to 2.2 years for men (Samuels, 2023).



The primary practitioners in nursing care are nursing assistants, whose roles differ fundamentally from those of nurses. Nursing assistants are mainly responsible for helping patients with daily living activities such as cleaning, hygiene, eating, and dressing; they also assist with patient transfers and monitor vital signs. Nursing assistants are often the primary caregivers in nursing and residential care facilities and tend to build long-term relationships with patients. Nurses, on the other hand, are responsible for coordinating patient care and treatment with physicians, with specific duties including assessing patient conditions, recording medical histories and symptoms, administering medications and treatments, and developing care plans. In terms of education, nursing assistants typically need to complete a state-approved training program and pass a competency exam to become certified or licensed. These programs include instruction in nursing principles and clinical practice, and certification requires passing a state examination and periodic license renewal (Nurse.org Staff, 2023). Nursing assistants primarily work in nursing homes, residential care facilities, and hospitals, requiring close contact with patients. In terms of compensation, the median annual wage for nursing assistants was $35,760 in 2022 (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2023). Employment of nursing assistants is projected to grow by 4% from 2022 to 2032, on par with the average growth rate for all occupations (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2023).


Among these workers, gender and racial distributions are notably imbalanced. According to research and analysis by Zippia's data science team, there are currently over 35,695 home caregivers in the U.S., of whom 70.7% are women and 29.3% are men. The average age of U.S. caregivers is 47, and 7% identify as LGBT. As of 2022, female caregivers earned 98% of what their male counterparts earned. Compared to government employers, caregivers are 75% more likely to work in private companies (Zippia, n.d.). In terms of racial composition, the most common race among caregivers is White (56.9%), followed by Hispanic or Latino (18.6%), Black or African American (10.1%), and Asian (8.6%). Given the limited attention from NGOs toward minority caregivers, this paper primarily focuses on minority caregivers (Zippia, n.d.).


The COVID-19 pandemic has had a significant impact on caregivers themselves and the caregiving industry as a whole, affecting primarily their mental health and work-life balance. A study on the symptoms and mortality of COVID-19 among healthcare workers in the U.S. found that healthcare workers who were 50 years or older, male, Black, or Asian had higher mortality rates and more severe clinical symptoms—including shortness of breath, fever, cough, and gastrointestinal issues (Lin et al., 2022). To some extent, this suggests that minority healthcare workers received less protection at work, carried heavier workloads, and had their personal needs less met. The pandemic has also brought a series of changes to the U.S. caregiving industry itself, including the following aspects (Tyler et al., 2021). First is the shortage of personnel and increased service demand: During the pandemic, demand for caregiver services increased, leading home care and home health agencies to face staffing shortages. This challenge may prompt changes in industry policies and service delivery models. Second is the transformation of care services: The COVID-19 pandemic may be reshaping the way healthcare services are delivered and health policies are formulated in the U.S., potentially including new regulations and practices regarding telehealth, patient isolation measures, and the use of personal protective equipment. The above outlines the general situation of minority caregivers in the U.S.; the following section will focus specifically on Asian American female caregivers and their current living and working conditions.














2

Challenges Faced by Asian American Female Caregivers in the U.S.


The motivations of Asian American female caregivers in the U.S. can be categorized into three types: the first is survival necessity. This primarily involves earning income to support their families financially. In the U.S., the entry barrier for the caregiving profession is relatively low, especially in workplaces built on personal networks. These jobs often do not require formal professional certification and are typically obtained through personal referrals or demonstrated job competence in one's resume. The second type is influenced by family environment. Individuals are significantly influenced by family members' careers, and early exposure to the field gives this group an unusually high level of professional proficiency. "Caring is in my blood. My mother is a caregiver, and my (other) family members are all involved in healthcare-related businesses." (Respondent 1) The third type is self-actualization. These individuals wish to work in social services or healthcare, help those in need to the best of their ability, and give back to society. The profile of women with such motivations mainly consists of middle-aged women over 45 who have lived in the country for a long time, whose children have grown up and either become U.S. citizens or obtained permanent residency. "Since my son is now in college, I have more free time, and I want to give back the love I feel I owe my own mother to elderly people in need, using all the love and strength I have to help them." (Respondent 2) From in-depth interviews with four women from different Asian countries, we learned that earning income to cover their own living expenses is the primary employment motivation.


In the United States, caregivers have three main employment channels: One is through job agencies; the second is employment via medical institutions, home care service agencies, and long-term care facilities. In this type of employment, female caregivers are required to possess relevant qualifications (such as certificates or certifications) and pass related exams, interviews, and medical checkups; the third is through personal referrals, meaning accepting caregiving work arranged by relatives or friends. For example, Chinese American female caregivers primarily care for elderly Chinese American seniors.


Different employment pathways lead to significant variations in caregivers' job responsibilities. In workplaces with legal and formal employment relationships, caregivers mainly perform nursing assistant duties. Their overall work is monotonous and physically demanding, primarily involving assisting patients with daily living activities, providing medical support, and handling household chores. In workplaces built on personal networks, caregivers' duties are more complex and extensive. Their role is traditionally viewed as a "live-in housekeeper," requiring them to manage the client's three meals a day, personal care, house cleaning, and even yard maintenance and appliance repairs.



Because caregivers mostly perform auxiliary and labor-intensive tasks, even certified nursing assistants (CNAs) often have their work overlooked, and the majority of caregivers are subjected to poor working conditions. Under this context, the discrimination caregivers face is societal in nature. (Travers et al., 2020). Among these, racial discrimination is a major factor. When asked about experiences of racial discrimination in nursing homes, 56% of CNA respondents reported experiencing racial discrimination from residents, 23% from residents' family members, and 40% from coworkers (Berdes & Eckert, 2001). As foreign immigrants, Asian female caregivers from non-English-speaking countries experience linguistic injustice in the workplace. These injustices manifest in two ways: first, prejudice and disrespect—employers or care recipients make assumptions about caregivers' abilities based solely on physical appearance or mock their racial identity, assuming they cannot speak English or come from impoverished countries. A Filipina caregiver stated in an interview: "Just by looking at our faces, patients will joke with us or ask if we can understand English. But my coworkers wouldn't do that—mainly the patients, but there's nothing we can do because they're sick." (Respondent 3) Second, limited career advancement opportunities. There is an objective difference in language proficiency between non-native and native English speakers, leading employers—especially individual, non-institutional employers—to prefer native speakers. A Chinese female caregiver shared her experience: "Because of language barriers, I feel our development is limited. It's mainly because of language—I speak Chinese, so I can only find Chinese employers. Sometimes when I want to work for employers from other countries, they don't want us, saying we can't speak English." (Respondent 2) In addition to language barriers, difficulties adapting to a new culture and job instability (Novek, 2013; Ryosho, 2011) are also challenges faced by Asian female caregivers. Adaptation to a new cultural environment manifests in two aspects: first, cultural competence among Asian female caregivers—the set of behaviors, attitudes, and policies that enable effective work in cross-cultural settings within a system, organization, or among professionals (Cross, 1989); second, the employer's organizational culture. A qualitative study by Parker and Geron described a research and intervention project aimed at improving cultural competence among nursing home staff, revealing five issues faced by minority caregivers: 1. Uneven staff awareness of residents' cultural differences; 2. Challenges in verbal and nonverbal communication; 3. Minimization or generalization of recognized differences; 4. Experiencing overt discriminatory behaviors or remarks; 5. Inadequate organizational response to discriminatory behaviors or remarks (Parker & Geron, 2007). Even sixteen years later, although some improvements have been made, these problems remain unresolved.



Although female caregivers are protected by various laws safeguarding workers' rights, in practice, there are gaps in protection. These gaps are especially present in the second type of workplace mentioned above—those operating outside institutional oversight. Caregivers' working hours, wage levels, occupational safety, pensions, injury compensation, and healthcare coverage significantly differ from those of nurses and doctors and still require further improvement. In the early 2000s, major workplace challenges for caregivers included heavy workloads, low pay, limited benefits, high insurance costs, and little respect from others (Iowa Better Jobs Better Care Coalition, 2004; Probst et al., 2009). According to the National Center for Health Statistics, the most common reason for nursing assistant turnover was low pay (37.2%); the third most common reason was institutional issues or poor working conditions (15.6%) (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2008). High staff turnover among direct care workers has reportedly reached 400%, leading to persistent staffing shortages and an inability to deliver person-centered care, a priority for many national organizations (Dodson & Zincavage, 2007; Rakovski & Price-Glynn, 2010). Meanwhile, Asian female caregivers often face unfair pay, especially those not formally employed by medical institutions, who frequently endure low wages, long hours, and lack of benefits. One respondent commented on the pay gap between caregivers and nurses: "When there's overtime at my hospital, my overtime pay doubles, but it's still much lower than that of nurses and doctors. Nurses earn $70–80 per hour, while I (as a caregiver) only get around $30." Moreover, this situation of excessive workload and low pay is even more pronounced among caregivers employed through personal networks. One respondent in this study noted: "I definitely work more than eight hours a day, but I don't get overtime pay. Since my client is a lonely elderly person, it's hard for them too, so I don't make a big issue of it."














3

Existing support policies and related recommendations


Asian female caregivers play a vital role in providing long-term care services (Zippia, n.d.), yet they themselves often face risks and challenges at work (Abrahim & Holman, 2022). This study, through literature and interview data, highlights the cultural integration difficulties and various forms of injustice experienced by Asian female caregivers in the industry. To improve the working conditions of Asian female caregivers in the United States, it is necessary to comprehensively consider the efforts that laws and policies, government agencies, social organizations, and the caregiver community itself can make to protect their rights,. especially in addressing gender and racial discrimination, by establishing clearer and more specific regulations to ensure they do not face discrimination or threats based on their gender or race at work.


Existing U.S. laws and regulations already include some that may support minority female healthcare workers. For example, in terms of gender and race, Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibits discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, or national origin in employment, promotion, and compensation; the Pregnancy Discrimination Act of 1978 supplements this by prohibiting discrimination against women in hiring, promotion, or other employment actions due to pregnancy, childbirth, or related medical conditions; the Equal Pay Act of 1963 also requires employers to pay men and women equally for the same job, prohibiting gender-based wage discrimination. Additionally, the U.S. Occupational Safety and Health Act and the Hospital and Healthcare Facilities Act emphasize legal protections for healthcare workers, such as regulating working hours, rest periods, and shift requirements to ensure medical staff's physical and mental health; protecting healthcare workers' employment rights and preventing discriminatory or unfair hiring practices; ensuring employees' safety and health at work and requiring employers to provide work environments meeting specific standards. These legal provisions aim to safeguard caregivers' basic rights and safety as workers and provide a foundation for protecting the fundamental rights of Asian female caregivers. Our report shows that Asian female caregivers face more specific real-world challenges at work, such as discriminatory remarks from clients and implicit bias during hiring. Future laws and policies can use these findings as reference to better support Asian female caregivers and protect their working conditions and related benefits.

There are also some social organizations in the United States that help protect and support Asian female caregivers. Asian Women for Health (AWFH) is a U.S.-based nonprofit organization dedicated to protecting women's health. It offers free Community Health Worker Training programs to Asian American women, including supplementary instruction on professional terminology related to health work, helping Asian American women become community health workers and enter the healthcare industry to access employment opportunities. At the same time, certain labor organizations can provide protections for Asian American female caregivers, such as the Health Care Union established under The Home Healthcare Workers of America and the United Food and Commercial Workers, as well as regional healthcare worker unions in the United States, such as 1999SEIU. These organizations aim to help workers secure better wages and benefits across regions in the U.S., emphasizing that caregiving workers should receive fairer work schedules and rest time (for school, family, and community). According to data from the official websites of The Home Healthcare Workers of America and the United Food and Commercial Workers, union members earn on average 13% to 22% more than non-union workers. Such organizations can better support Asian American female caregivers in meeting their basic needs and self-fulfillment, while also providing a platform to share and exchange personal experiences and build connections with local communities (The Home Healthcare Workers of America, 2014; The United Food and Commercial Workers, n.d.).


Relevant literature and our interview findings indicate that caregivers in the U.S. often face long and unstable working hours, perform significant emotional labor, and interact with complex client groups (Shang et al., 2021; Travers et al., 2020). We should also consider the intersectionality of Asian American female caregivers in the U.S. as a racial minority group and their gendered work experiences. (Collins, 2020; Oishi, 2022), striving to minimize their exposure to gender and racial discrimination in the workplace. Government agencies and social organizations can provide targeted vocational training for Asian American female caregivers, especially newly arrived Chinese immigrant women caregivers, including cultural orientation programs to help them integrate into local society—such as virtual reality (VR)-based experiential projects that simulate real-life conversational scenarios (see: Cultural workshops strengthen social inclusion through VR)—which may help them better adapt to the work environment and enhance their professional competitiveness. Additionally, local authorities can create more opportunities to prevent staff shortages and excessive overtime among caregivers, and work toward offering flexible work arrangements, career advancement opportunities, and family care support. Through these measures, Asian American female caregivers can better balance their work and family responsibilities and have sufficient time to spend with friends and family and to rest. Relevant policy-makers should also strive to establish a more comprehensive institutional protection system, ensuring that gender equality and cultural diversity are fully considered so that Asian American female caregivers can work in fair and equitable environments. Social organizations with the capacity can offer language and cultural courses to newly arrived Asian immigrant female caregivers to help them integrate into the workplace. At the same time, Asian American female caregivers can use the internet and other available resources to organize and unite, strengthen their rights, and build platforms within their community for mutual support, assistance, and information sharing.
