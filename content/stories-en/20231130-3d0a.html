Introduction:


From the international political arena to everyday life in society, the importance of gender equality has been highlighted in a series of recent high-profile news events. Perhaps the most striking was the 2023 Nobel Peace Prize awarded to Narges Mohammadi, a prominent Iranian human rights activist, writer, journalist, and lawyer, in recognition of her outstanding contributions to the struggle for women's liberation and human rights in Iran. At the same time, a nationwide women's strike wave swept across Iceland, with Prime Minister Katrin Jakobsdottir personally joining the protests and loudly calling for attention to gender equality. This trend is not limited to Northern Europe—French President Emmanuel Macron announced that abortion rights for women would be enshrined in the French Constitution before 2024, establishing a new legal foundation for gender equality in France.


The focus of these news events extends beyond politics, as Asian communities have also become a focal point globally. From racist attacks in New York State sparking strong condemnation from public figures, to Chinese American political leaders calling for the establishment of a National Asian Pacific American History and Culture Museum, these developments reflect the active participation of Asian communities in societal progress. Equally celebratory is the successful hosting of an exhibition on East and Southeast Asian migration and Chinese diaspora artists' works at the French National Museum of Immigration History, promoting multicultural development through artistic practice. In the United States, hate crimes against Asians have declined for the first time since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, offering a glimmer of hope for the future survival and development of Asian communities.

The 2023 Nobel Peace Prize was awarded to Narges Mohammadi in recognition of her contributions to the struggle for Iranian women's liberation and human rights.


On October 6, Narges Mohammadi, a prominent Iranian human rights activist, writer, journalist, and lawyer currently serving a prison sentence in Evin Prison in Tehran, was awarded the 2023 Nobel Peace Prize. Berit Reiss-Andersen, Chair of the Norwegian Nobel Committee, delivered the award citation, honoring her "fight against the oppression of women in Iran and her fight to promote human rights and freedoms for all," and called on Iranian authorities to release Mohammadi, expressing hope that she could attend the award ceremony on December 10.


The 51-year-old Mohammadi has dedicated herself to women's rights and the abolition of the death penalty, joining the "Center for Human Rights Defenders" founded by Shirin Ebadi, later becoming its deputy director. Since 1999, she has been arrested multiple times for criticizing the Iranian government, supporting women's movements, opposing the death penalty, and exposing torture and sexual violence against political prisoners and women in prisons. She has been arrested 13 times, convicted five times, sentenced to a total of 31 years in prison, and may have endured 154 lashes.


Mohammadi's selection from among 305 candidates was partly due to the widespread international attention generated by the "Iranian hijab protests," which increased global desire to honor Iranian women fighting for women's rights. In September 2022, 22-year-old Iranian woman Mahsa Amini died in police custody after being arrested for allegedly violating laws mandating headscarf wearing. Outraged Iranians launched nationwide protests and struggles under the slogan "Women, Life, Freedom." Mohammadi, along with two other imprisoned female journalists, reported on Amini's death, earning them the UNESCO Press Freedom Prize in May 2023. This year, Mohammadi was also included in the BBC 100 Women list, recognized as one of 100 inspiring and influential women worldwide by BBC.

A nationwide women's strike in Iceland, with the Prime Minister joining protests to call for attention to gender equality.


On October 24, tens of thousands of Icelandic women launched a nationwide strike under the slogan "Do you call this equality?" to protest the gender pay gap and gender-based violence in Iceland, while highlighting the unpaid labor frequently carried out by women. Prime Minister Katrin Jakobsdottir joined the protest, stating that progress toward gender equality at home and abroad has been too slow, and called for greater attention and action. The strike lasted 24 hours, marking the first full-day strike in Iceland since the 1975 women's strike. Schools, banks, shops, libraries, and other services shut down, public transportation was delayed, hospitals struggled to operate due to staff shortages, hotel rooms went uncleaned, and the national broadcaster RUV announced reduced television and radio programming for the day. The number of participants was estimated between 70,000 and 100,000, while Iceland's total population was approximately 380,000 in 2022, with nearly 200,000 women.


Despite being ranked the "most gender-equal country in the world" by the World Economic Forum for 14 consecutive years, Iceland still faces significant gender pay gaps and gender-based violence. According to Statistics Iceland, in certain industries and occupations, women earn at least 20% less than men. A study by the University of Iceland found that about 40% of Icelandic women have experienced gender-based and sexual violence. Freyja Steingrimsdottir, communications director of the Icelandic Public and Professional Employees Union and a strike organizer, said that while Iceland has gained attention for its reputation as a "gender equality paradise," gender inequality persists—wages in female-dominated professions such as healthcare and childcare are significantly lower than in other sectors, and urgent action is needed to address these issues.

French President Macron announced that women's abortion rights would be incorporated into the Constitution by year-end and permanently preserved.


French President Emmanuel Macron pledged to enshrine unconditional abortion rights for women in the French Constitution by 2024, and stated that the French government would submit a draft to the country's highest administrative court within the coming week. He posted on social media: "In 2024, women's right to choose abortion will be irreversible." Earlier, on March 8—International Women's Day—Macron had made a similar commitment, responding on Twitter to the U.S. federal abortion rights being overturned the previous year: "Solidarity to all women today who see this right under threat—France will engrave in its Constitution the freedom for women to access abortion."


This right enjoys broad support among the French public. According to a summer 2022 opinion poll, approximately 80% of the population supports abortion rights for women and favors constitutional protection of this right.


Stéphanie Hennette Vauchez, a law professor at Paris Nanterre University, expressed skepticism about the strength of constitutional protection. She pointed out that while other countries' constitutions may indicate women's reproductive autonomy or the right to decide on childbearing, no constitution explicitly protects abortion rights. She said: "This raises very interesting theoretical questions about why constitutions are so silent on human reproduction. After all, the survival of every political community depends on human reproduction." At the same time, she noted, "If France or any other country writes abortion rights into its constitution, it would truly be a groundbreaking move."


Mathilde Philip-Gay, a law professor at Lyon III University, speaking about the U.S. movement for abortion rights, said: "We don't have the same problems as the United States, at least not yet, but this is about the future. From Donald Trump's 2018 campaign, we can see there was a strategic effort to overturn federal abortion rights. In the U.S., there is a long-term strategy; in France, we also need a long-term strategy."

Crimes Against Asians


News


An Asian boy was subjected to a racist attack while canvassing in Nassau County, New York State, prompting public figures to condemn hate acts against Asians.


On October 2, during a campaign event in Nassau County, New York State, a 14-year-old Asian boy was assaulted by a woman while canvassing for candidate Weihua Yan, who also shouted racist slurs at him. Fortunately, the boy did not suffer serious injuries, but he declined medical treatment and filed charges against the woman.


Yan Weihua condemned the attack at a press conference on October 6, stating that although the boy did not suffer serious physical injuries, the incident may have left him with psychological trauma. The Asian American Scholar Forum (AASF) also issued a statement calling for condemnation of hate speech and actions targeting the Asian American community. AASF Executive Director Gisela Perez Kusakawa emphasized, "The violence, hate, and racist attacks by adults against Asian American youth are inexcusable," "Asian Americans must never again be seen as threats or treated as 'perpetual foreigners' in their own country." He called on everyone to set a better example for the Asian American community and youth, and to address the deep-seated biases against Asian Americans.

Chinese American political leader Elaine Chao attends Harvard forum, calls for national Asian Pacific American history and culture museum


Former U.S. Secretary of Transportation Elaine Lan Chao spoke at the Harvard Kennedy School Forum, urging the establishment of a National Asian Pacific American History and Culture Museum to counter anti-Asian hate and promote greater tolerance, understanding, and appreciation.


As a prominent Asian American political leader, Elaine Chao has served as Vice President at Bank of America, President of United Way, Director of the Peace Corps, Senior Distinguished Fellow at the conservative think tank The Heritage Foundation, and U.S. Secretary of Labor under the George W. Bush administration, becoming the first Asian American woman and the first Chinese American to serve in the U.S. Cabinet..


Reflecting on generations of Asian American progress in the United States, Chao expressed her satisfaction: "The Asian American community used to be relatively conservative and quiet, but now we understand the importance of speaking up and voicing our concerns. This should be celebrated." Regarding the persistent wave of violent attacks against Asian Americans, she noted their close connection to the "spillover effect" of U.S.-China tensions, and emphasized that this underscores the importance of celebrating Asian Americans' contributions to U.S. history.


Born in Taiwan, China, Chao immigrated to New York with her family at the age of eight, giving her deep personal insight into the Asian American experience. At the forum, she recalled her initial impressions of American culture upon arriving in New York and shared a memorable family experience during Halloween. She expressed hope that establishing a National Asian Pacific American History and Culture Museum would advance social progress and mutual understanding. Her remarks drew significant attention and resonance from the audience.

The National Museum of the History of Immigration in France hosts exhibitions on East and Southeast Asian migration and Chinese diaspora artists


From October 10, 2023, to February 18, 2024, the National Museum of the History of Immigration in France is hosting an exhibition on East and Southeast Asian migration, alongside a showcase of works by ten contemporary Chinese diaspora artists in France, under the theme of "Asian migration history." Throughout the exhibition, a series of panel discussions, performances, and film screenings will explore, from political, cultural, and social perspectives, the challenges and various forms of discrimination currently faced by Asian immigrants in France.


The history of Asian immigration to France dates back to the 1860s during France's colonial era in Southeast Asia, when indentured laborers arrived. Today, with economic globalization, increasing numbers of economic migrants have come to France. As a result, a large number of second- and third-generation Asian immigrants born in France now present a markedly different image of Asian identity, challenging the stereotype of being "hardworking and submissive" by actively participating in French social and political life, advocating for greater equality for immigrant and minority communities, and fighting against racial discrimination..


Ms. Mélanie Wang is one of the curators of the exhibition and a researcher on Asian migration at the French National Centre for Scientific Research (CNRS). Her father is from Guangdong, China, and her mother is of Vietnamese and Lao descent. As a second-generation Asian immigrant born in France, she has sought to actively challenge French society's biases against Asian communities. In 2020, Wang founded a blog called Asiatitude to combat stereotypes and misunderstandings about Asian immigrants in mainstream French society during the pandemic. It became the first platform in France dedicated to introducing Asian immigrant experiences and quickly gained popularity among the Asian diaspora.

Anti-Asian hate crime rates in the U.S. drop for the first time since the pandemic, experts describe it as a "cyclical" trend


According to the latest data released by the U.S. Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI), anti-Asian hate crimes in the United States declined by 33% from 2021 to 2022—the first drop since the beginning of the pandemic. The number of hate incidents fell from 746 to 499. However, not all Asian communities experienced a similar sharp decline: bias incidents against Sikh Americans decreased slightly, from 185 to 181. Yet the number of Sikh victims affected rose from 195 in 2021 to 198 in 2022. Therefore, some experts suggest this decline may be part of a "cyclical" pattern rather than a long-term trend.


Regarding factors influencing anti-Asian hate crime rates, Janelle Wong, senior researcher at AAPI Data—a nonprofit focused on demographic and policy research for Asian Americans and Pacific Islanders—believes such trends "often correlate with national security concerns or other forms of U.S. foreign policy that heighten attention toward Asian Americans." She explained that in the context of economic downturns, when mainstream American discourse overwhelmingly blames Asians for the pandemic, hate crimes are likely to surge, and she anticipates crime rates may rise again depending on domestic and international circumstances.
