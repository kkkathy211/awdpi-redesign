When people talk about domestic violence, we often hear this question: "Why doesn't she leave?"


But... what if she has no stable income, nowhere to stay temporarily, and must care for her children—does leaving mean the end of danger? Is leaving a new beginning or destruction?


Perhaps, instead of questioning why someone chooses to stay, we should pay more attention to: What makes it difficult for her to leave?



Let's begin with a film.



*There's Still Tomorrow* (*C'è ancora domani*) is a film set in post-World War II Italy.


The story centers on the family life of an ordinary woman. She takes care of her husband and children, handles household chores, and strives to maintain daily family life. Yet beneath this seemingly normal existence, domestic violence and deeply entrenched gender norms continually shape her circumstances.



The film shows us that violence is not only physical harm. Economic control, emotional oppression, and unequal power dynamics in daily life can also restrict a person's choices.


And these issues do not seem to have disappeared along with the era depicted in the film.


When someone wants to leave a violent relationship, economic stability, housing, family responsibilities, and social support all influence the choices available to her. We must therefore ask ourselves: Beyond the courage and determination of the person in crisis, what else can we do? What kind of support can society offer?



Watch. Discuss. Act.



In connection with the International Day for the Elimination of Violence against Women on November 25, AWDPI Chicago & DC will host an online screening and discussion of *There's Still Tomorrow* on December 4.



This event is more than just sharing viewing experiences. We hope to use the film's characters and plot as a starting point to discuss the power dynamics behind domestic violence, and the roles that laws, public policies, and social support systems can play. The discussion will be structured around three parts:


WATCH

See


What forms of violence and control appear in the film? Which scenes are treated as normal within the story, yet make us uneasy today?

DISCUSS

Discuss


Why is it so difficult to leave a violent relationship? How do economic dependence, housing, caregiving responsibilities, and social attitudes affect a person's decisions? Is domestic violence merely a private family matter?

ACT

Act


If you could change one policy or improve one social support mechanism, what would you choose?


During the event, we will use open discussion, live polling, and anonymous interaction to invite everyone to share their observations and thoughts. No professional background is required, and you don't need to prepare answers in advance. We simply hope to hear your voice—whether your experiences or expertise come from different walks of life.



Special Guest






We are delighted to invite Maria Angélica Bautista to participate in this event. Maria is a professor at the Harris School of Public Policy at the University of Chicago, and her research focuses on political and social institutions and gender inequality.


Maria will join the discussion on the day and engage in a Q&A session with participants. We also look forward to using this opportunity to further explore the issues presented in the film from the perspective of public policy.

A single film may not change reality, but it can help us start paying attention to issues we've never seriously considered before.


When we stop only asking "Why doesn't she leave?" and begin to care about what would enable her to leave safely, perhaps we can find some things worth changing.
