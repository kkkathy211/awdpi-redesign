On July 23, 2026, in Philadelphia, USA, the International Congress of Mathematicians.


When the International Mathematical Union announced the name "Wang Hong," thunderous applause erupted from the audience. The 35-year-old Chinese mathematician Wang Hong walked onto the stage and accepted the Fields Medal—a prize hailed as the "Nobel Prize of Mathematics" and the highest honor awarded every four years to no more than four scholars under the age of 40.


At that moment, history was rewritten.


This was the first time a Chinese national had received the Fields Medal. Wang Hong also became the third female recipient in the over ninety-year history of the Fields Medal, and the first Chinese woman to win it.


But perhaps the honor is only a starting point.

「"Behind the Honor"

Harassment, discrimination, and barriers to advancement


According to UNESCO statistics, in 2022, women accounted for 31.1%only one-third of researchers worldwide. The organization's 2025 report, *The Status and Trends of Women in Science*, noted that this proportion has grown slowly over the past decade and remains below that level in some countries. 10%.


A 2025 report by the International Science Council found that women face three times as many obstacles as men in career advancement, including missed opportunities due to caregiving responsibilities. Women are 4.5 times more likely than men to report experiencing harassment and micro-discrimination.


A 2020 documentary—*Picture a Scientist*—directly addresses this issue. Centered on the careers of three female scientists, the film documents their journeys of "overcoming brutal harassment, systemic discrimination, and years of subtle slights to revolutionize scientific culture." of the journey.


Documentary: *Picture a Scientist*


When a scientist mother hears her daughter exclaim, "I want to be a scientist just like you," her first thought is "horrible" and she begins to cry. She can only tell her daughter these are tears of joy, but in reality, these tears stem from the experiences of female scholars facing everything from overt sexual harassment to implicit bias, from institutional discrimination to the intersecting challenges of race and gender.


Hopkins, the eldest of the three protagonists in the film, tells a story spanning decades.


In the film, Hopkins recalls an incident early in her career while working at Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory. Nobel laureate and co-discoverer of the DNA double helix, Francis Crick, approached her from behind and placed his hands on her breasts, asking, "What are you working on?" Terrified, Hopkins could only pretend nothing had happened. She chose to "move past the violation and keep going."


Born in 1998, Zaiying is now conducting research in data engineering at a university in Seoul, South Korea. She completed her PhD at KAIST (Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology). Reflecting on an uncomfortable encounter during her undergraduate years, she described it as "like someone shoved a block of ice down your neck."


After that incident, she became even more determined to pursue feminist activism. During last year's Seoul Rainbow Parade, she raised her banner high, shouting for peace and freedom. She has also carefully studied relevant laws on gender discrimination and bravely defended a close friend who experienced workplace gender-based violence.


After completing her master's degree, she went to KAIST for her PhD in data engineering. She joked, "It's a field where you deal with code, algorithms, and models every day—those who like it might really love it. Maybe my personality is quite resolute; I'm someone who, once I make a choice, keeps moving forward. I enjoy the process, even when it's not particularly interesting."


KAIST (Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology)


In 2020, KAIST had nearly twelve thousand students, fewer than 20% of whom were women, and female faculty were even rarer—only about 11%. She was one of that less-than-20%, and often the only woman in her lab.


She said, "I've followed some of the news about Professor Wang Hong's award and felt inspired. When an Asian woman scholar steps into the spotlight, or onto an even brighter stage, you may not know what she has endured or how much effort she has put in."


Years later, Hopkins became a full professor at MIT. She discovered that female faculty were allocated far less laboratory space than their male colleagues. In 1994, she sneaked into various MIT labs at night with a tape measure, measuring and mapping the spatial distribution of all laboratories to gather data proving that women were given less lab space. Before sending a protest letter, she discussed the issue with a female colleague and realized she was not the only woman who felt a disparity in professional quality of life compared to male colleagues.


At that time, in the six departments of MIT's School of Science, there were only 15 tenured female faculty members, compared to 194 men. They formed a committee aimed at improving the status of women faculty in the School of Science, reviewing whether the allocation of space, resources, salaries, and teaching duties was fair, and establishing mechanisms to correct identified inequalities.


This effort ultimately led MIT to publicly acknowledge its discrimination against women in STEM faculty—a moment that triggered widespread scrutiny of gender bias across academia in the United States. Hopkins herself received the U.S. National Academy of Sciences' highest honor, the Public Welfare Medal, in 2024.

「"The Plight of PhD Students"

Hyper-competition, formalism, and lack of support

In terview with the International Mathematical Union – Wang Hong


What is the truth behind it all?


After learning of Wang Hong's award, Song Chunwei, her former instructor in Advanced Algebra and a professor and doctoral supervisor at Peking University's School of Mathematical Sciences, was interviewed by *The Beijing News*. Speaking about the current research environment in China, he said, "What's most anxiety-inducing in Chinese academia right now is this 'level-by-level monster grinding' system—where young researchers are burdened with a series of non-academic tasks. To pass these hurdles, people may choose to work on short-term, quick, and easily measurable projects, which isn't necessarily beneficial for long-term academic development." "


Facing the impact of the academic environment on individuals, Wangye, currently pursuing a PhD in psychology at a university in the American Midwest, frankly admitted, "Every PhD student probably thinks about 'whether to drop out' this matter. I feel that during the academic research process, some of the pressure actually comes from my disapproval of our school's management model and selection mechanisms. The psychological toll brought by this sense of disconnection is greater than I had imagined. Some things you don't notice when you first arrive in the U.S.; only after staying longer or hearing others explain them do you realize they're different from what you had imagined.


Most American universities strive to create and promote their campus image and establish relevant policies, but when you actually try to use those policies to protect your rights, you may still encounter many obstacles. The support pathway appears to exist, but when someone truly needs to walk it, they find it's not as expected. Yet you can't blame them, because technically they've done everything they were supposed to do. Whether you actually receive help is outside their evaluation scope."


Wangye's reflections dismantle the simplistic assumption that academic environments abroad are necessarily better. When academic support becomes a form of "performance," students not only fail to receive substantive help but also lose the legitimacy to complain, because the system has technically "fulfilled its duty."


Thus, it becomes clear: two different systems face the same dilemma: The seemingly rational academic systems are gradually devouring academia itself.


Whether it's being forced into non-academic battles at home or being suspended in a formalistic support system abroad with no accountability, genuine pursuit of knowledge is being pushed to the margins.


「Un der Parental Suppression"

Searching for a new belonging amid doubt and pain


In Tara Westover's memoir *Educated*, she tells a story—Tara, born into an extreme Mormon family in Idaho, USA. Her father obsessively believed that schools and hospitals were government conspiracies.


As a result, Tara never set foot in a classroom before age 17. Her childhood was forged from scrap metal in a junkyard and the roar of cranes. Yet driven by an inner longing for the outside world and education, she eventually escaped the mountains.


Tara Westover and her memoir *Educated*


The book's original English title, *Educated*, emphasizes the state of "having been educated, having undergone transformation through education." The Chinese title, *You Should Fly Like a Bird to Your Mountain*, is taken from the *Book of Psalms* in the Bible, carrying dual meanings of "escape" and "finding a new belonging." the dual meaning.


Zhang Yang, currently studying at a university in the southwestern United States, focuses her doctoral research on quantitative communication. But outside class, she is deeply engaged in gender-related topics. Her personal motto is exactly this phrase: "You Should Fly Like a Bird to Your Mountain."


No one in her family works in the humanities, so from the moment she announced her plan to pursue a PhD in the U.S., a heavy atmosphere hung over her household.


Recalling that period, she said: "My family initially opposed my decision to pursue a PhD. First, they didn't understand the career prospects of a humanities PhD. Second, they'd heard that job opportunities for humanities PhDs are limited. They fundamentally lacked confidence in my abilities and would suppress and belittle my choices." In my view, this kind of obstruction essentially stems from their inner insecurity. They see their daughter as an extension of themselves, and when I make choices that deviate from their expected control, it triggers panic—this panic manifests externally as controlling, suppressing, and blocking me. "


The impact of one's family of origin on women who choose academia does not end here; it can also significantly alter the research directions of some female scholars.


In 2011, Tara Westover was pursuing her PhD at the University of Cambridge. Her academic path shifted—she began studying history, particularly the relationship between "family, morality, and the social sciences." Her doctoral dissertation was titled "Family, Morality, and the Social Sciences in Anglo-American Cooperative Thought, 1813–1890."


A literary critic once pointed out on a public platform, "Tara's academic choices are no coincidence. Her entire life has been devoted to studying how families shape people, how morality is constructed, and how the social sciences understand the complexity of 'human beings.'" Her academic pursuit is, at its core, an ongoing, systematic process of self-understanding. "


The pain from her family of origin not only drove her to leave but also shaped what she wanted to study. Her academic interests are deeply intertwined with her life experiences. She studies family because family both shaped her suffering and became the puzzle she finds hardest to solve.


"Returning to the scholar themselves"

What kind of university education system do we need?

Regarding Wang Hong's recent Fields Medal win, Wang Ye believes this actually confirms the success of an internationalized training model for scholars—such heights are difficult to achieve within a single educational system, which lacks the vision and diverse environment.


Whether domestically or abroad, the root issue always comes down to one core question: Can academic systems truly "nurture" the scholar as a person?


Wang Hong, classroom photo


Speaking about specific support mechanisms, Wang Ye stated, "For a scholar to reach this level, you need to grasp the best elements from different educational systems, including encountering excellent mentors."


In her view, if mentors are considered part of community support, then the core support that truly shapes a female scholar— "forging" her—is actually 90% community support + 10% individual ability.


"Individual ability is actually dynamic; it can advance rapidly within a good community and system. A good system doesn't push you—it nurtures you. Just like in my mentor's lab: he provides you with abundant nourishment, and how tall you grow depends on your personal ability to absorb it—individual ability is the capacity to absorb environmental nourishment," said Wang Ye.


Although Wang Hong's award marks a historic breakthrough for Asian women in the international academic community,


the reality has not changed as a result: women researchers worldwide still make up only one-third of the field, sexual harassment and implicit bias are widespread, domestic "level-by-level combat" evaluation systems squeeze out space for originality, formalistic support mechanisms abroad leave students at a loss, and family obstruction of women's academic choices remains common.


These overlapping difficulties together form the complex obstacles that female scholars must overcome.


After Wang Hong, the "forging" of female scholars remains a protracted battle.
