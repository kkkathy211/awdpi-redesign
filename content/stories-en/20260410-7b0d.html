Introduction

UN Women points out that from preventing gender-based violence to achieving equal pay for equal work, women and girls worldwide still face legal inequality, and violations of their rights continue to go unpunished.


On this year's International Women's Day, UN Women issued a global warning: judicial systems, which are meant to uphold rights and the rule of law, are failing women and girls around the world. Women globally enjoy only 64% of the legal rights that men do, leaving them vulnerable to discrimination, violence, and exclusion at every stage of life.


These findings come from a report released by the UN Secretary-General titled "Ensuring and Strengthening Access to Justice for All Women and Girls." Additionally, the report states that 54% of countries globally lack a legal definition of rape based on the principle of "consent," meaning that even if a woman is raped, the law may not recognize it as a crime. In nearly three out of four countries, child marriage remains legally permitted under national law. In 44% of countries, equal pay for equal work is not protected by law, making it legally acceptable for women to receive lower wages than men for the same work.


"

Sima Bahous, Executive Director of UN Women, said: "When justice fails to protect women and girls, the damage extends far beyond individual cases. Public trust erodes, judicial institutions lose credibility, and the foundation of the rule of law is weakened. A justice system that cannot protect half the population has no right to claim it is a guardian of justice."

"


Despite long-standing advocacy for gender equality, backlash against gender equality is intensifying. A global culture of impunity is spreading from courtrooms to cyberspace and into conflict zones, fueling repeated violations of women's and girls' rights. Laws and regulations are being rewritten to restrict the freedoms of women and girls, silence them, and allow perpetrators to escape accountability. Today, technological advancement far outpaces regulation, and digital violence against women and girls is growing increasingly severe, with perpetrators rarely held accountable—impunity is everywhere. Moreover, in armed conflicts, rape continues to be used as a tactic to gain military advantage—according to reports, the number of conflict-related sexual violence cases has increased by 87% in just two years.


The report also shows that progress is not out of reach: 87% of countries globally have enacted laws against domestic violence, and over the past decade, more than 40 countries have strengthened constitutional protections for the rights of women and girls. But laws alone are not enough. Discriminatory social norms—stigma, victim-blaming, fear, and social pressure—still silence survivors and obstruct access to justice, allowing even extreme forms of violence such as femicide to go unpunished. In addition, women face numerous practical barriers in accessing justice, including the financial and time costs of legal proceedings, language barriers, and lack of trust in institutions meant to protect them.


This year, UN Women marks International Women's Day under the theme "Rights, Justice, Action—For All Women and Girls," calling on all stakeholders to take urgent and decisive action: end impunity, defend the rule of law, and achieve equality for all women and girls in law, practice, and all areas of life. The Commission on the Status of Women (CSW) is the UN's highest-level intergovernmental body responsible for setting global standards on women's rights and gender equality, and this year's seventieth session of the Commission (CSW70) presents a once-in-a-generation opportunity to reverse the rollback of women's rights and advance justice. Sima Bahous, Executive Director of UN Women, emphasized: "Now is the time for us to stand up, speak out boldly, defend rights, pursue justice, and take action—so that every woman and girl can live safely, express themselves freely, and enjoy equal rights."
