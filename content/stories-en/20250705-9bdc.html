The verdict is naturally satisfying, but one question has continued to trouble me and my friends:
In this case, almost all victims were Chinese women, and most initially chose silence. This is not an isolated incident, but a common phenomenon within the Chinese community: According to academic research, when experiencing sexual violence in the UK, fewer than 5% of Chinese women choose to report it to the police, far lower than other ethnic minority groups.
This involves factors such as cultural differences and lack of social support systems: The difficulties Chinese women face when experiencing sexual violence overseas may be far greater than we imagine.
01 | Existing systems in the UK

Taking universities as an example, most UK higher education institutions have established relatively comprehensive institutional support mechanisms to respond to incidents of sexual assault.

For instance, if you experience sexual assault, you can immediately contact your university's emergency support service to receive timely assistance and file a report. Additionally, if you experience ongoing psychological distress after the incident, universities typically offer mental wellbeing services that can arrange counseling or further referrals. At the same time, the university can also assist you in contacting the NHS for a forensic examination to protect your legal rights.

However, can these current systems fully address the difficulties faced by Chinese women?

02 | Common barriers for Chinese women reporting to authorities
First, there is the language barrier. Some might say: "Overseas Chinese students in the UK don't speak good English? Then they're just here to buy a degree!" That's not true. Not to mention the difference between academic English and everyday English; adapting to various accents upon first arriving in a foreign country, and dealing with others' attitudes toward your own accent, is a long-term challenge.
Even when reporting in one's home country, it's normal to become incoherent, stutter, or forget words. Let alone the fact that in Zou Mouhao's case, the victims had to use English, their second language, to accurately describe their traumatic experiences?
Second, there are cultural factors. What kind of interaction counts as dating? What counts as being in a romantic relationship? If someone invites you to their home, is that an implicit invitation for further intimacy, or just a friendly gathering? What do gestures like hugging, shoulder patting, or kissing really mean—am I overthinking it? When I felt uncomfortable, why didn't I refuse immediately, yet continued to stay in contact?
Facing someone unfamiliar with your cultural background, having your actions and experiences scrutinized piece by piece—even if the police are professional—the victim still faces immense pressure.

03 | Structural challenges faced by overseas Chinese students and women
Some scholars argue that Chinese international students in the UK are "highly visible yet deeply misunderstood." (highly exposed yet most misunderstood) .

In the UK, they are often seen merely as "economic instruments" or mistakenly perceived as a "wealthy, privileged group"—stereotypes that lack data support. Meanwhile, support networks within UK universities lack substantive assistance for international students, especially Chinese students, on structural issues such as legal rights, employment, mental health, and residency.

Even with long-term visas, seeking help remains difficult for them. Many victims rely on spousal visas and are ineligible for government assistance; they also bear greater family responsibilities, making them more economically dependent on their partners and losing opportunities for independent help-seeking. Due to visa status, they may choose to compromise even after enduring long-term threats from their partners.
Regarding financial pressure, this is also a challenge faced by international students. I personally know several student couples who continue living together even after breaking up, because subletting rental contracts in the UK is complicated, finding new housing takes time, and prices may be prohibitively high. If someone needs to leave their home immediately due to violence, without friends' support, the costs are extremely high.
04 | Existing resources and our actions
Therefore, to address these issues, the UK Office of AWDPI Asian Women Development Plan International has completed this "Guide to Recognizing and Responding to Gender-Based Violence: For You in the UK". This Chinese-language information guide can help everyone better understand response procedures and available resources; we hope it can offer practical support and guidance when you or a friend need it.
The handbook includes definitions, real-life case studies, practical response guidelines, and extensive resources for help.

Asian Women Development Plan International (AWDPI)

AWDPI is an international nonprofit organization dedicated to eliminating discrimination and gender-based violence against Asian women living overseas. Upholding the principles of empowerment and development, we provide systematic support to help Asian women achieve equal opportunities in social, economic, cultural, and educational fields, enabling them to become a vital force in advancing society. We pay special attention to overseas Chinese women facing gender-based violence and domestic violence, offering them comprehensive, ongoing protection and assistance—including language support, psychological counseling, legal aid, community mutual support, transnational rescue networks, and media advocacy—so they can rebuild their lives in safety and dignity.
