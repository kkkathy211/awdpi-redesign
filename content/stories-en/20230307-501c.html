Abstract


Since the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic, domestic violence in the United States has emerged as a "shadow issue" exacerbated by the pandemic, placing Asian American women—a group already relatively vulnerable—under even greater threat to their life safety. This article, set against the post-pandemic context, focuses on the Asian American women affected by domestic violence , and explores the current living conditions of these victims alongside an overview of U.S. government and societal support efforts, aiming to reveal the challenges Asian American women currently face in seeking help.


Keywords: United States, Asian American women, domestic violence, women's assistance

1. Research Background and Research Questions


(1) Domestic Violence as a Shadow Issue in the Post-Pandemic Era

The outbreak of the novel coronavirus pneumonia (Coronavirus Disease 2019, COVID-19), hereinafter referred to as the pandemic, has brought unprecedented impacts and challenges to humanity, threatening not only global economic development but also severely affecting people's mental health, even triggering a series of violent behaviors and causing a global public health crisis. Although countries worldwide have implemented various prevention and control measures—such as home isolation policies, closure of schools and other public places, and suspension of social services—to slow the spread of the virus, data including reports from the World Health Organization (WHO) indicate that these measures have had catastrophic consequences for individuals already suffering from domestic violence, fostering numerous acts of violence against women (Davis, Gilbar, & Padilla-Medina, 2020; Jarnecke & Flanagan, 2020; Wenham et al., 2020; WHO, 2020), with one of the most significant issues being a global rise in domestic violence cases (Boserup, McKenney, & Elkbuli, 2020; Campbell, 2020; Duncan et al., 2020; Jetelina, Knell, & Molsberry, 2020).


Among all possible contexts and forms of domestic violence, intimate partner violence (IPV) is a primary form. Intimate partners include individuals currently or previously in marital, dating, or cohabiting relationships (Zou et al., 2007), and intimate partner violence includes physical, emotional or psychological, sexual violence, financial control, as well as surveillance and isolation—where the abuser distances the victim from family, friends, and support networks. (Ribeiroetal., 2022; Symthetal., 2021; WHO, 2022).


On November 24, 2021, UN Women held an online event commemorating the International Day for the Elimination of Violence against Women and released a report titled "Measuring the Shadow Pandemic: Violence against Women during COVID-19," pointing out that the COVID-19 pandemic, which began in 2019, has intensified domestic violence, creating a vast "shadow" that has rendered many acts of intimate partner violence invisible "shadow" issues, posing a significant threat to women's safety worldwide. The report states that in just 12 months from July 2019 to July 2020, nearly 243 million women aged 15 to 49 experienced sexual or physical violence by an intimate partner (UN, 2020; UN Women, 2020b).


The report also notes that during the pandemic, approximately 65% of respondents reported experiencing violence, about 25% said family conflicts had become more frequent, around 70% reported that verbal or physical abuse from partners had become more common, about 40% felt less safe in public spaces, and about 20% even felt unsafe walking alone during the day. Women living in rural areas experienced violence significantly more severely than those in urban areas, and unemployed women experienced violence more severely than those with jobs. Globally, in 2021, 243 million women aged 15–49 experienced sexual or physical violence by an intimate partner. Since the lockdown on March 17, reports of domestic violence in France increased by 30% compared to the previous three months; in Cyprus and Singapore, hotline calls increased by 30% and 33% respectively; in Argentina, emergency calls related to domestic violence increased by 25% (UN Women, 2020). According to a study published in The Lancet, over half of Bangladeshi women who had previously experienced intimate partner violence reported an increase in such violence after the implementation of the COVID-19 home isolation policy (Hamadani et al., 2020).


High-risk groups for domestic violence in the United States also experienced remarkably similar domestic violence challenges during the pandemic: their risk of violent harm significantly increased, the consequences of such harm became more severe, and at the same time, their channels and resources for seeking external help were greatly reduced.


For example, research data from Davis et al. (2020) clearly show that Americans infected with COVID-19 face higher risks of domestic violence. Individuals who reported COVID-19 symptoms but refused testing were three times more likely to experience domestic violence than those without reported symptoms (OR=3.15, 95% CI 1.19–2.29, p<0.05). Participants who tested positive for COVID-19 were over three times more likely to use psychological violence (OR=3.24, 95% CI=1.18–8.89, p<0.05) and physical violence (OR=3.02, 95% CI=1.12–8.17, p<0.05) against their intimate partners. Another study analyzing publicly available police data from 25 cities and one county across 22 U.S. states found that, from March 13 to May 24, 2020, domestic violence incidents increased by an estimated average of over 5% due to people staying at home during the pandemic (Hsu & Henke, 2021). Among all published studies, data on Asian populations remain extremely limited, but it is not difficult to imagine that under the combined impact of the pandemic and racial discrimination, Asian American women face even higher risks and more difficult circumstances regarding domestic violence.


(2) Escalating U.S.-China Strategic Competition Worsens the Plight of Asians

Since the large-scale outbreak of the pandemic in the United States in March 2020, Asian Americans have faced "racism" and "anti-Asian sentiment" as secondary threats, becoming ongoing targets of harassment and attacks by entrenched anti-Asian actors. Discriminatory speech and even violent acts against Asian Americans have spread across American society like the coronavirus itself, becoming a nationwide phenomenon. The rising number of anti-Asian incidents in the U.S. has not only drawn national attention but also attracted global concern. Considering the unique situation of Chinese Americans within the anti-Asian movement and the high proportion of anti-Chinese rhetoric and actions within the broader anti-Asian incidents, the pandemic has further intensified the already tense relationship between China and the U.S., and this strategic competition, in turn, has to some extent exacerbated the survival challenges faced by Asians in America.


The relationship between the U.S. and Asian countries closely corresponds with the experiences of Asians in America. Whether it was the mass incarceration of Japanese Americans during World War II or the "Chinese Confession Program" during the early Cold War, these are concentrated manifestations of this linkage (Greg Robinson, 2001). Since the 1990s, the U.S. has continuously pressured China through legislation, diplomacy, and other means, citing issues such as human rights, intellectual property, Tibet, and Taiwan, and has repeatedly promoted the "China threat theory." The sharp deterioration of U.S.-China relations during the Trump administration affected Chinese Americans and, by extension, Asians broadly. The Biden administration has inherited Trump's strategy of "decoupling" and confronting China, and U.S.-China relations continue to face strong clashes of interest. As Henry Kissinger (2015) noted, due to vast differences in values, the U.S. and China have not formed a "shared world order view."


According to a Gallup poll in March 2021, 45% of respondents viewed China as America's greatest threat—more than double the figure from a year earlier (22%)—ranking first. Andrew Nathan, a Columbia University political science professor specializing in China studies, believes Americans are worried, even fearful, about China's rise. According to a national public opinion survey conducted by the Committee of 100, nearly 40% of the public believes "Asian Americans are 'too' influential in government and high-tech fields, and these people are more loyal to their ancestral countries." Some Chinese immigrants have clearly realized that "whenever there is a shift in U.S.-China relations, Americans still treat them as foreigners" (Krejsa & Cho, 2018).


Today, ideological and value-based clashes between the two countries have become increasingly severe, and their strategic competition shows signs of spreading into various domains, contributing to a certain extent to the intensification of anti-Asian violence and worsening the living environment for Asians in the United States. An analysis of the ethnic identities of reporters in the "Stop AAPI Hate" (Stop AAPI Hate) organization's "2020–2021 National Report" reveals that among those who reported discrimination, Chinese people accounted for 43.5%, the highest among all ethnic groups, while Korean, Filipino, Japanese, and Vietnamese people together accounted for 42.7%. Nearly 90% of reporters believed their Chinese or Asian identity was the primary reason for their attacks. Although many were born in the U.S. or have lived there for years, fully "Americanized" in language and lifestyle, their yellow skin and black eyes mark them as "perpetual foreigners," making them obvious targets for attacks. Among them, vulnerable groups are more likely to be attacked, with most anti-Asian assaults in American society targeting elderly Asians and women. In a statement, Asian Americans Advancing Justice Los Angeles noted that among the 6,603 anti-Asian incidents recorded in the report, 64.8% of reports came from women.


In addition to facing possible discrimination and verbal abuse, Asian Americans in the United States have also experienced a sharp rise in racially motivated crimes due to the pandemic. In February 2021, the Congressional Asian Pacific American Caucus (CAPAC) issued a warning stating that since the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic, violence against Asian communities in the U.S. had surged, creating a "crisis impossible to ignore" for Asian Americans. From Manhattan, Washington, Seattle to Los Angeles, attacks against Asians have spread across major cities nationwide. Since the onset of the pandemic, numerous reports have documented discrimination and violence against Asian Americans: 32% of Asian adults said they feared someone might threaten their personal safety, and 80% of Asian Americans believed that violence against Asians was increasing (Ruiz et al., 2021). According to hate crime research data released in June 2021 by the Center for the Study of Hate and Extremism at California State University, San Bernardino, anti-Asian hate crimes in the first quarter of 2021 rose by 189% compared to the same period in 2020. The three cities with the highest growth rates in hate crimes were New York, Los Angeles, and Chicago. In New York, the number of reported hate crimes increased from 13 in the first quarter of 2020 to 47 in 2021, a surge of 262% (China Society for Human Rights Studies, 2021).


These forms of discrimination and violence have consequently led to Asian Americans having the lowest sense of belonging and acceptance among all racial groups in U.S. society. According to the STAATUS (Survey of Asian American Attitudes and Status) Index, only 19% of Asian Americans aged 18–24 fully agree that they feel a sense of belonging and acceptance, compared to 51% among those aged 65 and older. The proportion of Asian American women who feel they fully belong in the U.S. (27%) is also lower than that of men (33%). Even more strikingly, U.S.-born Asian Americans report a weaker sense of belonging than those born outside the United States. Low levels of belonging easily lead to declining trust, making it difficult for individuals to believe that the government or society will offer help when they face discrimination or unfair treatment, thus discouraging them from speaking out or seeking assistance. The convergence of the COVID-19 pandemic, the China-U.S. trade war, and xenophobic nationalism has undoubtedly made the living environment for Asian Americans even more difficult and has left Asian American women suffering from domestic violence often caught in a dilemma, forced to silently endure abuse.


(3) Dual Vulnerability of Asian American Women from an Intersectional Perspective


Intersectionality is defined as a feminist perspective for understanding complex social power dynamics (Peng, Q., 2019). On one hand, gender, together with other social categories such as socioeconomic background, race, nationality, ethnicity, and sexual orientation, are key variables in determining social power. On the other hand, women are not a homogeneous group, and focusing solely on gender risks obscuring other forms of structural oppression. An intersectional approach helps deconstruct structural inequalities and reveals the complexity behind social issues.


Taking the lockdown policies implemented after the outbreak of the pandemic as an example, their potential and unintended consequences have exposed the failure of mainstream policy-making to adequately consider the vulnerabilities of marginalized and minority groups. First, the enforcement of lockdowns likely placed a heavier burden of unpaid caregiving on women, making it harder for them to maintain economic independence. Additionally, a potential and unintended consequence of lockdown orders may have been an increase in domestic violence, including abuse of partners, elders, or children. For perpetrators or potential perpetrators, the requirement for family members to remain in confined spaces for extended periods also meant they could use violence more openly and without concealment. On one hand, women—already the primary victims of domestic violence—became even more vulnerable after the pandemic outbreak. On the other hand, examining the impact of domestic violence during the pandemic across different racial groups, Asian American women clearly faced more severe challenges than white women.

Women perform more than three times the amount of unpaid caregiving work as men (Amarante & Rossel, 2018; Baxter & Tai, 2016), bearing a heavier double burden of both employment and caring for children, elderly parents, and disabled family members. Affected by prolonged social isolation, staying at home, and school closures, people—especially working-class individuals—faced increased psychological stress. As a result, women who carry greater household responsibilities may not only have to endure increasingly irritable partners but also absorb the escalating mental strain caused by unpaid caregiving. Since reopening schools was not a top priority during the pandemic, mothers were effectively excluded from the labor force. The longer these conditions persist, the harder it becomes for women with children to fully recover from long-term unemployment, leading to continuously reduced opportunities for career advancement. At the same time, being forced to remain in a confined space for extended periods increases women's risk of experiencing domestic violence. In an environment where it is already difficult to seek external help, ethnic minority women lacking structural social support undoubtedly face even more severe survival challenges.


According to an NBC News report on October 1, 2020, when pandemic-related lockdowns began in March, staff at several Asian American domestic violence organizations watched their work come to a standstill. Even after pandemic restrictions eased and reports of domestic violence surged, Asian American women—the group already least likely to report abuse—still tended not to seek help. Therefore, when considering the difficulties faced by Asian American women experiencing domestic violence, we find that racial identity and cultural background are significant influencing factors. In many Asian American communities, domestic violence is often seen as a shameful family matter that should not be shared with outsiders. Even while living in the United States, public discourse surrounding victimized Asian American women remains subtly influenced by traditional values. When facing domestic violence, influenced by the "family-centered" (jia bentiwei) ideology, many women choose compromise and endurance. When Asian American women finally decide to seek external help, economic dependence, cultural differences, and language barriers often lead them to turn first to informal, unofficial support. However, non-governmental organizations and individual aid services specifically dedicated to assisting Asian American women experiencing domestic violence are extremely scarce, and even existing organizations and individuals often lack consistent and stable funding. Moreover, many Asian American women also face heightened risks of racism and deportation. Fear of these consequences further discourages them from seeking help from the police or other government agencies.


After the outbreak of the pandemic, crimes targeting Asians in U.S. society became more severe, and Asian American women's sense of insecurity in public spaces intensified, which to some extent suppressed their initiative to attempt escaping from abusers. According to FBI records, hate crimes against Asians living in the United States increased by 77% from 2019 to 2020 (Department of Justice, 2020). Between March 2020 and June 2021, over 9,000 anti-Asian hate incidents were self-reported to the advocacy group "Stop AAPI Hate" (SAH, 2021). Numerous organizations tracking violence and harassment against Asian Americans have noted that attacks targeting women are more prominent than those against men. Additionally, perpetrators sometimes use immigration status to threaten and control victims. Faced with the risk of deportation, some Asian American women choose compromise in hopes of securing a relatively stable legal citizenship status. Finally, unlike the elite images often portrayed in American films and television, real-life Asian American women are not a small, economically privileged group and are more likely to face unemployment. According to data from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, in 2020, 44% of Asian American women experienced unemployment lasting six months or longer, compared to 40.8% of Black women and 38.3% of Latina women (Department of Labor, 2021). According to the 2021 Women in the Workplace study jointly published by Lean In and McKinsey & Company, Asian American women make up one in fifteen in entry-level positions but only one in fifty in senior leadership roles (McKinsey, 2022). When the abuser holds economic dominance, the abused partner may remain silent out of fear of losing their primary financial support.


(4) Research Questions

This article, set against the backdrop of the post-pandemic era, focuses on the group of Asian American women suffering from domestic violence in U.S. society. Centered on the theme of the current living conditions of victimized Asian American women and the existing governmental and societal support systems in the United States, it attempts to answer the following specific questions: What is the lifetime prevalence of intimate partner violence (IPV) among Asian American women in the U.S. after the pandemic? What are the characteristics of a typical victim? What domestic violence assistance systems currently exist within the U.S. government, and how do they collaborate with non-governmental organizations? From the perspective of the needs of victimized Asian American women, what shortcomings exist in the current support systems? By answering these questions, the author hopes to offer insightful reflections for observers who are similarly interested in this research topic or concerned about the survival challenges faced by ethnic minorities.


II. Current Situation of Victims


(1) Proportion of Asian Population


According to the United States Census Bureau, "Asian" is defined as "a person having origins in any of the original peoples of the Far East, Southeast Asia, or the Indian subcontinent, such as India, China, the Philippines, Japan, Korea, or Vietnam." As of 2021, the Asian population in the United States was estimated at approximately 22.9 million, accounting for about 6.1% of the total population (the United States Census Bureau, 2020). The Asian population in the U.S. is diverse, including people from many different countries and regions, in addition to those listed above, such as Pakistan, Cambodia, Thailand, Bangladesh, and many others. In 2020, the estimated number of Chinese (excluding Taiwanese) Asian alone or in combination population in the U.S. was the largest Asian group, followed by Asian Indians (4.5 million), Filipinos (4.1 million), Vietnamese (2.2 million), Koreans (1.9 million), and Japanese (1.6 million). These estimates represent people who reported a specific detailed Asian group alone, as well as those who reported that detailed Asian group in combination with one or more other detailed Asian groups or other races.


The proportion of Asian Americans varies by state and region, with some areas having a higher percentage of Asian residents than others. For example, Hawaii has the highest Asian population of any U.S. state, with Asians making up 37.2% of its total population; California ranks second, with an estimated 6.4 million Asians, accounting for 15.4% of the state's population; and Washington State ranks third, with Asians comprising 12.2% of its total population (2020 Census Redistricting Data (Public Law 94-171) Summary File, the United States Census Bureau, 2020).

(2) Proportion of Asian American Female Victims


According to a 2010 national survey by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the lifetime prevalence of intimate partner violence (IPV) among Asian American women was 21.2%, slightly lower than the national average of 24.3% (Black, M. C., et al., 2011). In a report provided by the Asian Pacific Institute on Gender-Based Violence, 21–55% of Asian American women reported experiencing physical or sexual violence in intimate relationships (Yoshihama & Dabby, 2015). The following focuses on the victimization rates of Asian women in the three states with the largest Asian populations:


Hawaii

In the Hawaii Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) survey, 13.0% of adults self-reported experiencing violence in intimate relationships (*Note: intimate relationship violence is defined as being punched, kicked, or injured by any form of violence). Among the total population, women reported a rate of 15.8%, higher than the male reporting rate of 10.2%. In these cases, Asian victims (including Chinese, Filipino, Japanese, and other Asian ethnicities) accounted for 24.7% of all reported domestic violence cases in the state (Vergara R, et al., 2013).


California

Research by the California Department of Public Health (CDPH) indicates that in 2019, the rate of intimate partner violence (IPV) among Asian women in California was 0.83%, slightly lower than the overall IPV rate in California of 0.87% (2021). The report also provides information on the characteristics of IPV in California, including the types of violence experienced by survivors. Specifically, physical violence was the most commonly reported form of IPV, followed by emotional/psychological abuse, sexual violence, and stalking. The California Women's Health Survey reported that approximately 6% of women in California (641,000) experienced at least one incident of psychological or physical domestic violence in the 12 months prior to participating in the survey (2008).


New Jersey

Domestic violence data in New Jersey is collected and reported by the Division of Criminal Justice, New Jersey Department of Law and Public Safety. In 2019, New Jersey reported 29,834 domestic violence incidents, including physical abuse, sexual abuse, and psychological abuse. Among these cases, 2,264 involved Asian female victims, accounting for 7.6% of all reported domestic violence cases in the state. In the same year, the rate of domestic violence against Asian women in New Jersey was 1.2 per 1,000 people. This is lower than the overall domestic violence victimization rate in New Jersey, which was 2.1 per 1,000 people.


Proportions of Abuse Types

Regarding the proportions of different types of domestic violence experienced by Asian women, psychological abuse is the most common form, including tactics such as verbal threats, humiliation, and behavioral control (The Asian Pacific Institute on Gender-Based Violence, 2014). For example, in 2014, the Asian Pacific Institute on Gender-Based Violence conducted a national online survey of Asian American and Pacific Islander domestic violence survivors. The study found that 63% of respondents reported experiencing emotional abuse; a study published in the Journal of Interpersonal Violence found that among a sample of Chinese immigrant women in New York City, 47% reported experiencing emotional abuse, the highest proportion among all types of violence (Yick, et al., 2016); the National Latino and Asian American Study (NLAAS) reported that 43% of Asian American women in the sample reported experiencing psychological violence from a partner (Cho, et al., 2013).


Additionally, sexual violence and economic abuse are also common forms of abuse experienced by Asian women. The 2010 National Intimate Partner and Sexual Violence Survey (NISVS) conducted by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) found that 7.2% of Asian American women reported experiencing sexual violence by an intimate partner in their lifetime, while 14.7% of Asian women reported experiencing economic abuse by an intimate partner (Black, M. C., et al., 2011); a study of Korean American survivors of intimate partner violence in New York City found that 58% of participants reported experiencing sexual abuse and 68% reported experiencing economic abuse (Lee, M. Y., et al., 2015); a study of South Asian women in the U.S. found that 30% of participants reported experiencing sexual violence and 57% reported experiencing economic abuse (Raj, A., et al., 2015).


These studies indicate that, in addition to physical violence, emotional abuse, sexual abuse, and economic abuse are common forms of abuse experienced by Asian women. However, most studies rely on self-reports from victims, which may be subject to individual differences or social desirability bias. For example, each woman's perception and measurement of emotional violence may differ, making it difficult for researchers to standardize self-reports, thus reducing the validity of the surveys and limiting their representativeness of the entire population of Asian American women experiencing domestic violence in the U.S. Nevertheless, they still provide valuable data and insights into the experiences of Asian women facing domestic violence.


(3) Characteristics of Domestic Violence Cases Among Asian American Victims


The Asian Pacific Institute on Gender-Based Violence has summarized the common situations and characteristics of gender-based violence against most Asian Pacific American women. In East Asian communities, in cases of domestic violence among Chinese American women, the severity of abuse is not significantly related to the abuser's language ability or legal status (U.S. resident or permanent resident). Women without legal U.S. status or with limited language proficiency are more likely to experience severe abuse. However, women with legal citizenship or permanent residency are more likely to experience long-term abuse. Abusers who are employed and have education beyond high school are more likely to severely abuse their female partners. On the other hand, Chinese American women who are employed are more likely to be abused, while higher levels of education are associated with lower likelihood of abuse (Yoshihama, 2020).


Additionally, a 2006–2007 health survey conducted by telephone interviewed 592 Korean American women living in California (70% response rate), finding that younger women (18–39 years old) were more likely to experience psychological abuse by an intimate partner in the past year (34.5%), compared to 26.3% among those aged 40–54 and 18.3% among those aged 55 and older. Korean American women aged 18–39 and 40–54 were also more likely to experience sexual coercion by a partner, at rates of 19.6% and 20.5%, respectively (Yoshihama, 2020).


Research in South Asian communities has also revealed similar characteristics. A study interviewed 87 Filipino American women and 56 Indian and Pakistani American women, all aged 18–60, finding that Filipino American women born in the U.S. or who immigrated before adolescence (1.5 generation or later) were more likely to experience physical and sexual violence in intimate relationships (excluding stalking) compared to those born outside the U.S. or who immigrated after adolescence (first generation). The same pattern was observed among Indian and Pakistani American women, across all three forms of intimate partner violence—physical violence, sexual assault, and stalking. Younger South Asian women were more likely to experience stalking by intimate partners. Younger South Asian women were also more likely to seek help from police, legal institutions, and domestic violence agencies. Regardless of immigration or generational status, Filipino American women were less likely to seek medical care in cases of domestic violence. In contrast, Indian and Pakistani American women born in the U.S. or belonging to the 1.5 generation or later were more likely to seek medical care compared to first-generation women (Yoshihama, 2020).


Furthermore, a study of 160 married or heterosexual South Asian women in the Boston area, Massachusetts, found that arranged versus non-arranged marriages had no significant effect on the occurrence of intimate partner violence. Although not statistically significant, non-U.S.-born South Asian women were 3.5 times more likely to report domestic violence and 4 times more likely to report injury from domestic violence than others. Forty-five Nepali American female community leaders in New York City reported that their partners restricted their freedom of movement, requiring permission to visit friends or relatives, or forbidding them from working or attending school (Yoshihama, 2020).


A paper-and-pencil survey was conducted among 200 male respondents recruited from Vietnamese churches, temples, and community organizations in the Dallas and Fort Worth areas, finding that younger men, those with lower education levels, and those holding the attitude that "abuse of a partner is acceptable under certain circumstances" were more likely to have perpetrated physical abuse against an intimate partner in the past year. However, frequency of alcohol consumption and level of alcohol dependence (measured by the Michigan Alcohol Screening Test, MAST) were not associated with the tendency to perpetrate physical abuse (Yoshihama, 2020).


Domestic violence cases against Asian Pacific American women across different regions show striking similarities in the socioeconomic status of both perpetrators and victims, while also differing due to cultural backgrounds. The following cases fully reflect the characteristics documented in previous reports.


1. Case One

In May 2022, the Asian Law Caucus published a case article about an Asian American survivor who experienced human trafficking and domestic violence. Due to concerns about victim privacy and risks of secondary harm and retaliation, she shared her story using the pseudonym Z.


Z was born in Pakistan into a happy and harmonious family. She studied hard with the dream of becoming a nurse, and originally expected to follow a conventional path of marriage and motherhood like most women. However, misfortune in 2015 completely altered her life trajectory. That year, a Pakistani woman living in the United States returned to her hometown and met Z and her family. Soon after, Z became engaged to the woman's son. They maintained regular phone contact and dreamed together of a bright future in America. The prospective mother-in-law also poured out her heart to Z, sharing visions of living together and running a business side by side. Z was completely enveloped in this rosy bubble of sweet talk, completely unaware of the hell that awaited her.


After marriage, her husband returned to the U.S. first, while Z spent two years in Pakistan waiting for her green card. During this time, her husband gradually grew distant, but Z naively believed this was just a normal consequence of long-term separation. Two years later, when she finally arrived in the U.S., she suddenly discovered her mother-in-law had drastically changed—shouting at her at the airport, confiscating all her identification documents including her crucial passport, and worse, her husband already had a new lover. Upon arrival in America, Z was forced to work for free in her mother-in-law's company, from 9 a.m. to 6 p.m., Monday through Saturday. At home, she was made to do housework, wash dishes, and do laundry for six household members, sometimes even going without food for an entire day. Later, she was also forced to care for her sister-in-law's child and her husband's child with his new lover, and was threatened that any mistake would result in deportation to immigration authorities. Over the years, Z was forbidden from leaving the house or making friends, and was not allowed to tell anyone about her situation. Once, after speaking with a coworker at work, she was discovered by her mother-in-law and received a death threat.


Eventually, the situation escalated further. Z changed her phone and managed to escape. She received help from various organizations and stayed in a shelter until she could afford to rent a place. She also began working with a therapist. She reached out to Narika and Asian Pacific Islander Legal Outreach—two nonprofit organizations serving immigrant women—and received assistance with immigration matters and divorce proceedings. Additionally, the Asian Law Caucus guided Z in understanding her rights to unpaid wages and holding her abusers accountable. Currently, Z is awaiting a T visa to gain protection and remain in the United States.


Z hopes her story and experience can inspire and help more people. In seeking help, she realized the critical role shelters play. Shelters should have sufficient resources to ensure victims' housing security, preventing them from being forced to leave prematurely. Moreover, language assistance is also crucial—for example, she needed English-speaking professionals to help her apply for a housing lease. Z also believes that shelter staff must possess adequate cultural competence, especially when serving South Asian Muslim individuals like herself, to avoid causing secondary harm. More importantly, Z emphasizes the need for a clear accountability system within the support system to ensure that aid funds reach victims directly and promptly (Asian Americans Advancing Justice, 2022).


In the above case, we see that Z is a young South Asian woman from a harmonious, happy family, well-educated, with her own dreams and aspirations—fitting the typical profile of an Asian American and Pacific Islander woman experiencing domestic violence. Meanwhile, various reports indicate that perpetrators are often unpredictable, but it is certain that domestic violence in heterosexual relationships stems from one party's desire to control and exert power over the other. Abusers typically exhibit intense anger, aggression, authority, and dominance. Z's mother-in-law lured the victim with sweet words and ultimately controlled her through confinement, exploitation, humiliation, and threats—classic forms of emotional abuse, economic abuse, and physical abuse (Nations, 2022). When seeking help, Z mentioned that due to economic abuse by her husband's family, she was unable to receive fair wages for her labor. She faced significant challenges in renting housing due to language barriers and sometimes could not access direct assistance. Therefore, direct financial aid and language support provided by shelters are extremely important. At the same time, research shows that harm caused by intimate partner violence often occurs without witnesses (Wu et al., 2010), and due to shame and fear, non-traumatic medical visits become the primary form of seeking care. Even after domestic violence occurs, only 23% of victims report their injuries (Stark et al., 1979). Studies show that 78.4% of victims made medical complaints, yet 74% of injuries were not identified as abuse-related (Rhodes et al., 2011). Often, medical institutions fail to provide effective injury documentation, leaving victims feeling neglected and helpless ("Violence against Women," 1992). As a result, psychological factors and repeated setbacks may lead victims to abandon seeking help, causing intimate partner violence to escalate.


2. Case Two

Another case fully illustrates the psychological dependency of victims on their abusers. Mandeep Kaur, a 30-year-old South Asian woman living in South Richmond Hill, posted a video online on August 3, 2022, in Punjabi (a language spoken in India), outlining how her husband had mercilessly tortured her. In the video, Kaur cried while showing her bruises, stating that her husband had started beating her in India and continued the abuse in the U.S., attacking her daily regardless of whether he had been drinking. Over eight years, she had tried every possible way to change the situation but failed to stop the daily violence. She also accused her husband of having an affair and once locked her inside a truck for five days. Unable to bear the pressure, Kaur died by suicide at home on August 4, 2022.


In response, some people may wonder why these victims do not leave or divorce in time. In reality, the reasons victims cannot escape the cycle of violence are far more complex than we imagine. First, abusers often apologize after violent episodes, expressing remorse in a seemingly "sincere" manner (Chen Shuang et al., 2019): in the video, Kaur stated that her father had once helped her call the police, but she ultimately dropped the charges because her husband begged her. Additionally, according to a report by the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime, victims choose to endure in silence for reasons including: fear of retaliation; economic and psychological dependence; belief that the police will not intervene; and the perception that domestic violence is a private matter. One of the reasons Kaur endured the abuse was her economic dependence on her husband, particularly regarding the care of their two children. Kaur's father, Jaspal Singh, stated that Kaur married Sandhu in 2015 and moved to the U.S. together in 2018. "But she said she wouldn't leave him because she couldn't support her two daughters. We understand" (Jaspal et al., 2022). Kaur also stated in the video that she chose to endure domestic violence for the sake of her two daughters.


Among these factors, psychological dependency is particularly noteworthy. It must be understood that domestic violence is not only physical but also includes psychological abuse, such as frequent insults and verbal abuse (Chen Shuang et al., 2019). Due to constant humiliation and degradation, victims gradually lose confidence and self-worth, diminishing their motivation to escape the abusive situation. In the video, Kaur said that over eight years, she repeatedly suffered physical and psychological abuse from her husband, Ranjodhbeer Singh Sandhu, because she gave birth to two daughters aged four and six but not a son. When Sandhu pushed her onto the bed and suffocated her, she said: "I refuse to accept this anymore." In the video, her two children can be heard crying out, "Papa, no maaro mumma nu (Dad, don't hit Mom anymore)." This case involved numerous forms of psychological abuse, including being blamed for "failing to bear" a son, the shame of being beaten repeatedly in front of her own children, the pain of seeing her children suffer, her husband's infidelity, and the despair of eight years of failed attempts to escape. All of these repeatedly degraded Kaur's dignity, deepening her psychological dependence on her husband and reinforcing her belief that without him, she would have no way to raise her two children alone.


Furthermore, the lack of public assistance is another major reason why women struggle to escape domestic violence. In another video clip, Kaur publicly shared footage of her being beaten and sought help online, hoping someone would come to her aid—but even after her death, she did not receive effective support. Her brother, Sandeep Singh, said they were trying to apply for visas to bring her body home but received no help or response from any officials. According to Sandeep, they wanted to travel to the U.S. to perform her final rites and seek custody of her two minor daughters. They had applied for visas for Kaur's father, mother, and sister, but received no response. "We don't know the status of our visas or what will happen to the children. We have no Indian government officials we can talk to for detailed information. We are trying to contact everyone. It's been a week since she passed, and we still don't know what will happen to her body" (Sandeep Singh et al., 2022).


3. Case Three


In addition to young Asian women, elderly Asian women in the United States also face the threat of domestic violence, and the perpetrators are often not husbands but their own children. This situation has worsened since the pandemic. The COVID-19 pandemic forced millions of young people in the U.S. to return to their parents' homes. According to data from the Pew Research Center, during the pandemic, 52% of young adults aged 18 to 29—including members of Generation Z and Millennials—were forced to move back into their nuclear families. The Pew Research Center reported that approximately 26.6 million people lived in their parents' homes, more than ten times the number in 2021. The "boomerang generation" phenomenon also exists within New York's Chinese community, where some individuals neither pursue education, employment, nor further training, and in more extreme cases, become perpetrators of domestic violence.


Ms. V, a Chinese-American elderly woman living in New York City, stated on June 15, 2022, that her daughter and grandson moved into her home several years ago under the pretense of caring for her. However, they rarely shared household responsibilities, and after her grandson reached adulthood, he refused to continue his education or seek employment, instead staying at home as a "boomerang child." Not only did he verbally abuse her, but he also once suffocated her with a pillow and secretly recorded her crying to falsely claim she had dementia. She had no choice but to file a lawsuit in court, seeking eviction of the two as a landlord.


One major reason Ms. V could not escape the domestic violence was police inaction . Ms. V said she had repeatedly tried to discipline her daughter and grandson at home, but these attempts led to intense conflicts. She recalled one argument with her grandson during which he first covered her mouth with his hand and then pressed a pillow over her nose and mouth. Fearing for her life, she immediately called the police, but the officers took no meaningful action. Despite reporting the abuse multiple times, Ms. V said her grandson secretly recorded her crying and their arguments, then used these recordings to falsely claim she had dementia in front of the police. "One officer believed his side of the story and told me that if I continued like this, they would send me to a psychiatric hospital" (V, et al., 2022). To prevent the situation from worsening, she sought help from multiple city agencies and planned to file a complaint in housing court to evict the two as a landlord. "My heart is in pain" (V, et al., 2022).


Additionally, John Shin from the Korean American Community Service Center stated at the panel discussion that language barriers and cultural differences are other reasons why elderly Asian women often remain silent after experiencing abuse. Using his own community center as an example, he noted that over 14 years, the center had received fewer than five complaints. "This does not mean elderly Asians are living happily in their later years. A medical journal shows that, compared to other ethnic groups, Korean American elders have the least happy later lives." (John Shin et al., 2022)


Traditional beliefs also lead Asian American domestic violence victims to avoid exposing "family shame" to outsiders in order to maintain the appearance of family harmony. Another participant, Roger Chu (Zhu George, transliterated), shared that his sister suffers from mental illness and has repeatedly threatened their parents with a knife. However, due to the influence of traditional Chinese culture, his parents demanded he remain completely silent about it—unaware that this very action allowed dangerous incidents to recur.


Zheng Shi, director of the Fushou Elderly Care Center, said that in addition to physical abuse, many Chinese elders suffer verbal or financial abuse from their children. He gave an example of an elderly Chinese person who spent their life savings to buy a home for their child, only to be driven out of the house once the property was transferred, left homeless. He urged more victims to speak out about their experiences of domestic violence and to bravely seek help, to prevent greater tragedies.


III. Existing Support Systems


(1) Framework of Domestic Violence Support Systems


1. Government Support Systems


Official domestic violence assistance provided by the U.S. federal government is primarily delivered through agencies such as the U.S. Department of Justice, the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, and the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD). Centered on legal frameworks, this system has established support mechanisms covering emergency hotlines, housing placement, financial assistance, and technical aid.


(1) Legal Frameworks


In 1994, the U.S. government enacted the Violence Against Women Act (VAWA) to address domestic and sexual violence nationwide ("Violence Against Women Act," n.d.). VAWA was the first federal law in the U.S. to criminalize domestic violence and sexual assault, and the first federal law to encourage coordinated community responses to violence. In 2000, during President Bill Clinton's reauthorization of VAWA, Congress expanded its scope to include dating violence and stalking, established legal aid programs for victims, and created U and T visas for abused immigrants, allowing them to remain in the United States. The 2005 reauthorization of VAWA provided further protections for immigrants and increased funding for outreach, assistance, and prevention efforts for victims, establishing violence prevention strategies. Additionally, VAWA stipulated that it is illegal to evict victims of domestic violence or stalking from federal housing. In 2013, President Obama reauthorized VAWA, expanding protections for Native American women, immigrants, LGBTQ+ individuals, college students and youth, and public housing residents. In 2018, due to a federal government shutdown, the law expired on December 21, 2018. Although briefly restored, it expired again on February 15, 2019, during the Trump administration. Republicans in Congress opposed the bill because it included a provision restricting individuals convicted of domestic violence, assault, or stalking from purchasing or possessing firearms. In 2022, VAWA was reauthorized and strengthened in several ways: including increased funding for VAWA grant programs, expanding tribal courts' special criminal jurisdiction to include non-Native perpetrators of violence, and increasing resources for underserved and marginalized communities—including LGBTQ+ survivors of violence (mwright, 2022). This reauthorization also introduced new civil rights protections for survivors of intimate partner violence to meet the needs of our increasingly digital world.

In addition to VAWA, Congress passed the Domestic Violence Offender Gun Ban in 1996 (commonly known as the Lautenberg Amendment). This law prohibits individuals convicted of misdemeanor domestic violence or those subject to restraining or protection orders for domestic violence from transporting, transferring, or possessing firearms and ammunition.


(2) Emergency Hotlines


National Domestic Violence Hotline (About, n.d.): In 1994, when the VAWA was signed into law, President Bill Clinton authorized the establishment of the National Domestic Violence Hotline. Since then, continuous funding has been invested in its development. In 2005, the hotline raised $2.7 million to enhance its technical capacity, enabling it to answer more calls and provide better support to survivors. In 2006, the major U.S. telecommunications company Verizon launched a direct connection to the hotline through its #HOPE Initiative, allowing any call to #HOPE from a Verizon wireless phone to immediately connect to the hotline.


National Dating Abuse Helpline: In 2007, the National Domestic Violence Hotline and Liz Claiborne Inc. launched a project called "love is respect," which operates as a national helpline for teen dating abuse. In 2011, love is respect expanded to include a text messaging service, offering 24-hour text-based support to those seeking help.


(3) Housing Placement


The Federal Housing Choice Voucher Program, administered by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD), provides housing assistance to low-income families. In 2013, VAWA was reauthorized and mandated HUD to expand housing protections for victims of domestic violence, dating violence, sexual assault, and stalking within its housing programs (Office of the Secretary, HUD, 2016).


(4) Financial Support and Technical Assistance


The Office on Violence Against Women (OVW) currently administers 19 grant programs authorized by VAWA. Four of these programs have funding allocations specified by law, while the remaining programs are funded at OVW's discretion under VAWA guidelines. These grant programs do not provide direct financial aid to victims, but instead support the development of local government agencies and NGOs, aiming to reduce domestic violence, dating violence, sexual violence, and stalking by enhancing services for victims and improving accountability for perpetrators.


Additionally, OVW manages a technical assistance program that provides training, expertise, and problem-solving strategies to OVW grantees. This program offers in-person and online educational opportunities, peer consultation, on-site visits, and targeted support to help grantees and potential grantees address sexual violence, domestic violence, dating violence, and stalking. Under limited resources, the OVW technical assistance program delivers support to a small number of pilot sites through demonstration initiatives or evaluations of newly developed training curricula or tools.


At the local level, each municipal government has its own domestic violence assistance programs and resources, typically including police departments, women and children protection service centers, community service centers, and domestic violence shelters. These institutions provide safe housing, food, medical care, legal aid, and psychological counseling to individuals affected by domestic violence. They work in coordination with the federal domestic violence support system to jointly address the issue. For example, local women and children protection centers can assist victims in applying for the Federal Housing Choice Voucher Program, while the federal support system can provide technical and financial assistance to local institutions to strengthen their capacity and effectiveness in domestic violence response.


2. NGO-Government Collaboration System


In the United States, non-governmental, nonprofit organizations (NGOs) complement government agencies by providing domestic violence support services. NGOs are typically funded through private donations and government grants, with the mission of supporting and assisting individuals affected by domestic violence. NGOs can establish partnerships with government agencies to jointly address domestic violence.

NGO-government collaboration primarily includes the following aspects:


Government funding assistance: The government provides funding to NGOs for operating and delivering domestic violence support services. Government funding is typically contingent upon NGOs meeting certain requirements and standards, such as regular reporting, audits, and oversight. For example, the 19 grant programs administered by the aforementioned OVW.


Government technical assistance: NGOs can seek help and technical support from government agencies, such as the technical assistance programs offered by OVW.

NGOs assist governments in providing aid: when government agencies lack sufficient capacity, they can refer survivors to NGOs, which can then assist in supporting survivors of domestic violence.


NGOs serve as an extension of government agencies, further enhancing the effectiveness of domestic violence support services and expanding their reach, thereby providing more comprehensive and effective support and assistance to those affected by domestic violence.


3. Immigration status and support for Asian women


VAWA stipulates that if a domestic violence victim is a child, parent, or current/former spouse of a U.S. citizen or lawful permanent resident (green card holder), and has suffered abuse by that U.S. citizen or permanent resident, they may be eligible to self-petition for a green card without requiring the abuser to apply for immigration benefits on their behalf (U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services, 2020). This means that Asian women who have come to the U.S. through marriage, adoption, or other family ties do not have to endure abuse due to immigration status concerns. At the same time, according to regulations, undocumented immigrant women and children brought to the U.S. through human trafficking may also apply for green cards if they meet certain conditions. Both legal and undocumented immigrant victims of crime are protected under various U.S. laws, yet victims often lack legal knowledge and are unaware of these protections, which further exacerbates their vulnerability—making frontline support organizations even more critical.


There are many NGOs in the U.S. that provide domestic violence support specifically for Asian women, such as the Asian Task Force Against Domestic Violence and the Asian/Pacific Islander Domestic Violence Resource Project. These organizations operate in different regions across the U.S., offering free assistance to local domestic violence survivors in areas such as housing, health, education, immigration, and language services. It should be noted, however, that due to limitations in staffing and funding, the websites of these organizations serving Asian communities are often not user-friendly—a shortcoming that needs improvement in the future.


(ii) Updates to federal and state government domestic violence policies during the pandemic and their implementation


In the early stages of the COVID-19 outbreak in the U.S., the government's legal response was relatively slow. Some scholars suggested that the U.S. should emulate measures taken by certain European countries, with governments authorizing local departments or agencies to requisition hotel resources as temporary shelters for domestic violence survivors (Soled, 2021). However, after the pandemic began, many government agencies were slow to update information about domestic violence shelters. For example, New Jersey's primary anti-domestic violence organization, the New Jersey Coalition to End Domestic Violence, did not provide any information on its website about how or where victims could access help; instead, it only included links redirecting to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the New Jersey COVID-19 Information Hub. As a result, victims seeking help through the organization's website during the pandemic would have found it difficult to obtain practical and effective assistance. Many support organizations also emphasized that during the pandemic, survivors of gender-based violence experienced a significant increase in needs related to food, clothing, shelter, transportation, and financial support, while the services and resources available from relevant agencies significantly decreased (Bettinger-Lopez et al., 2022). For instance, shelter beds for domestic violence survivors were in short supply, and many people faced economic hardship due to job loss, making it difficult to afford basic living expenses. Due to the suspension of public transportation or the risk of disease transmission, many victims found it difficult to leave violent environments and seek help. These challenges were even more severe for marginalized groups such as immigrant populations, racial minorities, and LGBTQ+ communities.


Soled (2021) recommended that during the pandemic, courts should proactively expand their capacity to handle emergency domestic violence cases—for example, by assigning more judges to conduct hearings—thereby protecting victims from further violence while accelerating case processing and helping survivors escape violent environments sooner. Although as of now there has been no nationwide systematic study on the specific policy and legal changes during the pandemic and their impact on domestic violence cases, some scholars have conducted online surveys, phone interviews, email exchanges, and observations of community meetings and found that from April to July 2020, local courts and agencies in South Florida attempted various measures in response to the pandemic's impact (Bettinger-Lopez et al., 2022). For example, court staff made reasonable adjustments to judicial procedures: to conduct hearings and trials during lockdowns, courts adopted phone or video conferencing (such as Zoom) to replace traditional in-person appearances. Many court systems also extended civil, criminal, and temporary protection orders that were set to expire during the national lockdown, thereby providing continued protection for survivors and their children.


On the surface, these adjustments appear to lower the barriers for ordinary people to access justice through the judicial system—for instance, victims no longer need to worry about commuting, childcare, or taking time off work to attend hearings or court proceedings. However, many vulnerable groups—such as the elderly, racial minorities, immigrants, LGBTQI+ individuals, low-income individuals, and people with disabilities—may be excluded from this "online justice system" due to unfamiliarity with the technology or inability to afford the associated costs. At the same time, hearings conducted via platforms like Zoom are often delayed due to technical issues, making it difficult to deliver swift rulings on a large number of cases. This, in turn, has reduced the efficiency of handling domestic violence cases to some extent and increased the risk of survivors remaining in unsafe environments (Bettinger-Lopez et al., 2022).


IV. Current shortcomings in the support system


(i) Shortcomings in government support

Whether women experiencing domestic violence seek help depends on their understanding of what constitutes abuse and changes in their assessment of their own situation and external environment. Generally, the support available to victims can be divided into formal and informal support. Interpersonal networks such as friends and relatives are the primary source of informal support for these women (Horton & Johnson, 1993). Formal support is primarily provided by police, workers within judicial institutions, staff from social service organizations, medical professionals, crisis hotline workers, mental health professionals, clergy, domestic violence advocates, and staff at shelters for abused women (Bowker, 1988; Goodman, Dutton, Weinfurt, & Cook, 2003). In research on intimate partner violence (IPV), many studies have found that women experiencing domestic violence generally tend to lack social support, and many victims are reluctant to accept help from existing informal and formal support networks (Dunham & Senn, 2000). However, research with a more intersectional perspective has found that due to distrust of local authorities and fear of racial discrimination, domestic violence victims from racial minority backgrounds are more resistant to seeking help from formal services and are relatively more inclined to rely on informal support from family, friends, and community leaders (Ragavan et al., 2020). Kim and Hogge (2015) found that even after categorizing the types of IPV experienced by 152 Asian Indian women, they were still more likely to seek help from informal sources (such as family and friends) rather than formal ones.


However, government assistance is undoubtedly a more professional and legally enforceable measure compared to informal support, making it a crucial component in the process of helping domestic violence survivors. Yet in reality, even when abused Asian women resolve to seek formal services, a series of barriers may hinder their access to help and result in low success rates of external intervention. First, due to the abuse they have endured, survivors may harbor fear toward government, police, and other authoritative institutions symbolizing power. During Trump's presidency, the U.S. adopted strict measures against undocumented immigrants. Although the Democratic Party later regained control of the White House, the political and legal security status of immigrants in the U.S. has already faced severe challenges. Because immigration status follows a hierarchical system, women's position within this hierarchy is linked to their vulnerability to abuse (Raj & Silverman, 2002). Undocumented immigrant women often lack financial resources, have limited language skills, and possess little knowledge of local American culture (Lee & Chen, 2000); deportation becomes an ongoing threat that abusers can use against them. Research has found that when victims lack residency or citizenship, they are more likely to suffer more severe abuse (Kim & Sung, 2016). Although the 1994 Violence Against Women Act (VAWA) provides protections for undocumented abused women and has been updated over time, many immigrant women remain unaware of these legal provisions. Moreover, even women with legal immigration status may fear losing custody of their children due to legal conflicts with their abusers. Second, in recent years, incidents of police violence against racial minorities have increasingly entered public discourse, further intensifying Asian women's distrust. Finally, formal service providers—such as counseling agencies—often lack professionals with cross-cultural competencies (Das Dasgupta, 2000), making Asian women, especially those from less developed regions, reluctant to seek more specialized help. According to Kim and Hogge (2015), the acculturation level of abused Asian Indian women is positively correlated with their willingness to seek counseling for verbal and sexual intimate partner violence (IPV), but not for physical IPV. When Asian Indian women experience verbal or sexual abuse from their partners, their likelihood of seeking informal help is lower compared to other forms of violence. Thus, acculturation level is closely linked to their ability to access more professional assistance. This suggests that outreach and psychoeducational programs targeting less acculturated and newly arrived Asian immigrant populations have become urgent. These individuals may hold negative views toward Western-oriented therapeutic approaches and have very limited access to resources.


Currently, numerous seemingly invisible barriers still exist between the existing support systems and Asian women experiencing domestic violence. At the root of this issue is the lack of substantive political voice for minority women within the American political system, causing core issues concerning minority women's rights to remain marginalized. Gender quotas have been widely adopted in many countries as a means to correct representational imbalances and promote women's political participation (Murray, 2014). A quota refers to a required proportion set for certain candidates in elections. Since the introduction of gender quotas, debates over this system have continued: advocates argue that quotas enhance both substantive and symbolic representation for women, while critics claim they prioritize gender identity at the expense of meritocracy. However, men have traditionally been seen as the preferred candidates for public office and benefit from their dominant group status, as they are both rule-makers and participants in the established political game. Therefore, increasing the proportion of female representatives is undoubtedly important. While the proportion of women in U.S. politics is steadily rising, Asian American women remain underrepresented. In November 2021, Michelle Wu was officially sworn in as mayor of Boston, becoming the first woman of color to hold this position and breaking the long-standing historical monopoly of white men in Boston mayoral elections—a change celebrated as encouraging by the Asian American community.


It can be said that in recent years, Asian American political engagement has indeed significantly increased. Whether reflected in the growing number of Asian American faces among congressional candidates or the rising voter turnout among ordinary Asian American citizens, this burgeoning political trend is clearly on the rise. Nevertheless, the political representation of Asian Americans, especially Asian American women, remains far from adequate. Asian Americans currently make up 6% of the U.S. population, yet they hold only 0.9% of elected offices at the federal, state, and local levels nationwide—the lowest level of political representation among all ethnic groups (Lin, 2022). Since the first Asian American U.S. Senator was elected in 1959, the number of Asian American members in Congress has grown to 17 in the current 117th Congress. However, among the 535 members of Congress, there are currently 59 Black members and 46 Latino members, while Black and Latino populations constitute 12.4% and 18.7% of the U.S. population respectively (Manning, 2021). In comparison, Asian American representation in Congress still significantly lags behind their 6% share of the national population. Representation at the state and local government levels is even more inadequate. In New Jersey and Nevada—states with the fourth and fifth largest Asian American populations—there are only two and one Asian American legislators respectively. Hawaii is currently the only state where Asian American legislative representation matches their population proportion. For Asian American women, opportunities to hold public office in state and local governments, legislative bodies, federal agencies, and Congress are even scarcer. Michelle Wu is one of only three Asian American female mayors among the 100 largest cities in the U.S. Among the approximately 160 Asian American state legislators, Asian American women account for only about one-third. In the current U.S. electoral system, Asian American and Black women represented by figures like Michelle Wu and Kim Janey, Boston's former acting mayor, have fewer political opportunities than men of color, white women, and white men (Phillips, 2021).


(2) Room for Collaboration between NGOs and Government


Currently, there remains significant room for collaboration between non-governmental organizations (NGOs) serving Asian American women experiencing domestic violence and U.S. government agencies. Compared to government institutions, many NGOs focused on domestic violence assistance are inherently more closely connected to communities. For Asian American survivors seeking help, NGOs are more likely than family or friends to offer professional support, while posing lower access costs and risks than approaching government agencies. Currently, with the overall deterioration of social security in the U.S., Asian Americans face growing challenges. In this context, Asian women—especially those with relatively unstable immigration status—do not view seeking help from government agencies as their first option. Therefore, exploring how NGOs can serve as a bridge between survivors and formal support networks is a meaningful and practical direction. When frontline NGO workers effectively establish connections with survivors and provide detailed answers to their concerns, survivors are more likely to access legally protected assistance through government institutions. On the other hand, the nature of NGOs means they are highly likely to face challenges such as insufficient funding and inadequate human resources; sustainable NGO operations require government support.
