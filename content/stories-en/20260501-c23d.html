Every May Day, society's spotlight habitually falls on scenes of labor where sweat drips and muscles tense. We pay tribute to the day-and-night shifts on assembly lines, and praise the hands that build cities under the scorching sun. This visible, physically measurable effort forms our most basic understanding of "labor."


But imagine this scene: in a university office, an Asian woman scholar gently hands tissues to a crying student, then softly calms their anxiety caused by academic stress.


What she is doing is not part of her official job duties. Yet, due to her Asian background and female identity, she is often perceived by colleagues and supervisors as "good at listening" and "skilled in conflict resolution," and thus expected to take on such tasks. Her labor requires no physical strength, has no fixed working hours, is not written into employment contracts, nor counted toward performance evaluations. For her, it is an endless, hidden exhaustion that wears down her spirit day after day.


This kind of labor has an academic name:


emotional labor


Today, we want to explore, through books and research, the "emotional labor" experienced by Asian women scholars in academia. We want to see, behind the label of "model minority," how many Asian women are constantly self-monitoring? How is their anxiety concealed beneath polite smiles? More importantly, after enduring long-term emotional labor, how do they begin to awaken and gradually reclaim their voices within the cracks of the system?


On this holiday for all workers, let us begin by first "seeing them."

PART 01

Why are Asian women

more likely to bear emotional labor in academia?


Emotional labor is not a spontaneous expression of a particular personality, but rather a institutionalized work requirement.


In the classic study *Working in America*, the authors point out that in a service economy, employees are required not only to complete specific tasks, but also to "manage their own emotions and influence the feelings of others." Smiles, patience, and care—emotional expressions that originally belong to the personal realm—are transformed into part of work, even becoming something that can be "sold."


This kind of labor does not occur naturally, but is explicitly trained and regulated. For example, flight attendants are required to remain friendly at all times, treating passengers as "friends" or "people in need of care," and even when facing unreasonable demands, must regulate their own emotions to maintain a "comfortable atmosphere." In such a system, emotions are no longer merely personal experiences, but become a resource that is organized and managed.


Source: https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003199588


However, when this institutionalized emotional labor is layered with gender and race, it is not distributed equally.


In the 1990s, as diversity policies emerged in U.S. higher education, research on faculty from minority racial backgrounds received broader attention. Studies found that minority faculty members face not only academic and teaching responsibilities, but also additional psychological and social pressures tied to their racial identities.


This "emotional labor" is often overlooked by institutions, yet profoundly affects faculty members' career development and emotional well-being. In *Ethnic Minority Scholars, Research, and Mentoring: Current and Future Issues*, Padilla draws on emotional labor and cultural capital theory and extends it into an analysis of power structures within educational institutions. He points out that Asian faculty are often expected to make "emotional investments" in student advising, community service, and campus diversity initiatives to bridge institutional racial gaps.


Source: https://doi.org/10.2307/1176259


When these expectations accumulate, emotional labor is often implicitly shifted onto Asian women faculty—whether in classrooms, offices, or everyday interpersonal interactions. This means that much of the emotional labor carried by Asian women is not formally included in their job responsibilities. These tasks often lack clear evaluation criteria, yet continuously drain the worker's time and energy.


Under such a structure, emotional labor is no longer merely an individual choice, but a "default responsibility" shaped jointly by gender and race.


In research on the image of Asian women, *Myth of the Model Minority: Asian Americans Facing Racism* focuses on the specific stereotypes and biases Asian women face in American society. Chou and Feagin argue that stereotypes of Asian women are not simply misunderstandings from a single culture, but are jointly constructed by long-standing colonial discourses, immigration histories, and media representations.


The study summarizes several dominant stereotypes, including the "submissive Eastern woman," the "exotic sexual object," and the "model minority woman." Women have long been expected to be more considerate, patient, and nurturing, while Asian women are often assigned additional stereotypes of being "docile," "non-confrontational," and "emotionally stable."


Source: https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315090634




PART 02

The impact of emotional labor on Asian women scholars


Although some studies note that emotional labor by Asian women scholars may play an extremely important positive role in building student trust, expanding social networks, and enhancing teaching relationships, these "positive effects" come at a cost. When emotional labor becomes a continuous expectation, it can also turn into a new burden, affecting women's mental health and career development.


1. Emotional exhaustion (emotional burnout)


In the late 1970s, the rapid expansion of service and interpersonal-helping professions sparked public concern about practitioners' mental health. In *The measurement of experienced burnout*, Maslach and Jackson, through empirical studies of nurses, teachers, social workers, and other occupational groups, revealed that sustained emotional labor can lead to psychological resource depletion and personal detachment, resulting in the so-called phenomenon of "occupational burnout."


Due to job demands, Asian women in academia are required to consistently provide high emotional value and maintain intense interpersonal relationships. This may not only lead to emotional energy depletion, but also cause indifference or detachment toward those they serve, and weaken their sense of self-efficacy.


Source: https://doi.org/10.1002/job.4030020205


2. Cultural taxation


In *Ethnic Minority Scholars, Research, and Mentoring: Current and Future Issues*, Padilla first introduced the concept of "cultural taxation." He observed that faculty from non-dominant racial groups are often expected to take on tasks such as promoting diversity, mentoring minority students, and representing their ethnic communities.


*A Seat at the Table: Strategic Engagement in Service Activities for Early Career Faculty From Underrepresented Groups in the Academy*, based on large-scale data from U.S. universities, analyzes differences in service workload by gender and ethnicity. The study points out that although these service tasks have social value, they are often not counted toward formal promotion or evaluation systems. These tasks consume time and emotional resources, creating an invisible academic labor burden that affects the research and career development of Asian women in academia.


Source: https://doi.org/10.1097/acm.0000000000002603


3. Suppressed expression and identity confusion: "Who am I?"


In daily interactions, many Asian women not only need to regulate others' emotions, but also constantly manage their own reactions. The study *Racial Microaggressions in Everyday Life: Implications for Clinical Practice* finds that racial microaggressions often appear in subtle, everyday forms—difficult to pinpoint clearly, yet impossible to ignore. In such situations, Asian women in academia, or Asian women in general, often choose self-regulation and conflict avoidance to maintain relational stability.


Source: https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066x.62.4.271


However, this sustained emotional management brings deeper consequences. As *Working in America* states: when emotions become part of work, people may gradually lose connection with their authentic feelings, and even begin to wonder: which version of me is real? Meanwhile, emotions that are "not encouraged to be expressed"—such as anger, discomfort, or doubt—are further suppressed. When a person is expected to always understand others, they are more likely to lose space to express their own discomfort.




PART 03

The real voices of Asian women:

reclaiming themselves from invisible labor


When we truly listen to the voices of these Asian women scholars, we find they are not merely passive recipients of fate. Beneath those hidden pressures lies an incredibly resilient vitality. Their experiences may span different countries and generations, yet in facing prejudice and self-doubt, they produce a resonance that feels almost miraculous.


1. A "non-native" accent, and the self that must disappear


For many Asian women scholars with immigrant backgrounds, their first major battle upon entering academia often begins with "speaking" In the "elitist" environment of higher education, language is not merely a communication tool, but also an invisible entry ticket. Professor Yea-Wen Chen from San Diego State University once documented the inner journeys of a group of Asian women scholars.


They possess top-tier talent in their respective fields, yet the moment they speak, they are instantly categorized as "outsiders." A professor from Hong Kong, China, recalled that no matter how brilliant her papers were, in daily discussions or lectures, colleagues and students would fixate their gaze on her accent. That look carried a kind of preconceived arrogance, as if "non-standard" English equated to "impure" thinking.


To appear "more professional," to erase the layer of "foreignness" from their identity, they had to repeatedly rehearse pronunciation before speaking, remain tense during meetings, and desperately try to conform to mainstream auditory expectations. This "pruning" of their cultural roots brings an indescribable internal drain. As one scholar put it:


"I feel trapped in a massive vortex, not knowing how to respond, slowly withering under this silent discipline." "I feel myself trapped in some enormous vortex, not knowing at all how to respond, slowly withering away under this silent discipline."

2. After "crossing social classes," the guilt that has nowhere to rest


Many Asian women scholars are the first in their families to attend university, rising from working-class backgrounds into the ivory tower through extraordinary struggle. This should be an inspiring story, but reality is filled with emotional rupture.


Sociologist Allison Hurst has keenly captured this sentiment. She found that for these "new elites," success in academia often comes with a sense of loneliness. They seem to linger between two worlds, "dual agents": at school, they learn to speak and act like the middle class, proving their integration; at home, they carefully translate their lives, trying to bridge the gap created by class differences.


This "crossing" brings not only achievement, but also a "survivor's guilt"——as if they have walked alone into the light, leaving their families behind in the dark. This psychological drain makes them appear unusually calm, even distant, in public. Especially for those from communities accustomed to "not burdening others," this "silent struggle" often continues until their mental defenses completely collapse.


3. If the rules are too cold, then return to the podium


Yet, the tone of these stories is not entirely bleak. After prolonged inner exhaustion and self-awareness, a gentle yet firm resistance unique to Asian women is emerging.


Faced with systemic deprivation, some scholars are consciously adopting a strategy of "self-preservation." To outsiders, they may seem less "driven" than before, even displaying a kind of "quiet quitting." But this is by no means passive disengagement. Rather, after experiencing total burnout, they have decided to draw boundaries—refusing to pour all their life energy into filling institutional gaps.


They are beginning to redefine their worth beyond the cold evaluation system. Since publications and funding have become tickets to the arena of prestige, they retreat to the most authentic core of education: the podium.


In one survey, respondent Wei firmly stated that no matter how the external evaluation context changes, teaching should always remain the conscience of a university professor. Another scholar, Wen, spoke even more powerfully, "In the eyes of leadership, I may be completely 'invisible' due to average research output. But in the teaching I pour my heart into, students recognize my value and offer me gratitude. It is this faint light that illuminates the true dignity of me as an educator."


Blindly praising Asian women's "resilience," "tolerance," and "gentleness" can sometimes itself be a cruel moral burden. We must not rely solely on individual Asian women scholars to endlessly cultivate their "psychological resilience," or to rigidly imitate cold, patriarchal models of success.


What we truly need is structural awakening and institutional reconstruction of the entire academic environment. Academic institutions should establish more diverse, inclusive, and humane evaluation systems that formally recognize invisible emotional labor—such as mentoring ethnic minority students and supporting the mental health of academic communities—as part of academic value and criteria for promotion.


At the same time, we must completely dismantle the stereotypes imposed on Asian women, and restore to them their full rights as human beings—allowing them to express anger, to show sharpness, to say, when utterly exhausted:

"I'm very tired today. I don't want to smile."


At this moment, we honor those laborers who change the world with their hands, and we must not forget those who quietly till the wilderness of the human soul. Emotional labor is labor too. It is profoundly valuable psychological resonance and life energy.
