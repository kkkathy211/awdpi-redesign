Leisha Beardmore is a respected advocate in the fields of gender equality and women's rights, with 15 years of experience working with organizations such as Amnesty International, Save the Children, and the International Rescue Committee. She has focused on preventing and responding to gender-based violence, protecting children, and supporting women's mental health and social well-being, gaining a deep understanding of how gender equality and women's rights are essential to building a just and equitable society. Below is a conversation between Avoice Overseas Chinese Women Protection Project and Leisha Beardmore.


Special note:

Leisha Beardmore uses she/they pronouns, so TA is used to refer to Leisha in the following text. We respect everyone's gender identity and are happy to use the gender pronouns individuals prefer, to avoid any possible misunderstanding or offense.


" "Fighting for the rights of marginalized people"


Leisha says she became interested in human rights issues at a very young age and has always wanted to work in this field. She firmly believes that protection in emergencies, responses to gender-based violence, and the safeguarding of women and children are concrete expressions of human rights protection. "Understanding the needs of different individuals, analyzing how their rights are violated, and seeking solutions from a rights-based perspective—this is what truly meaningful work looks like, the kind of work we've always been doing."


Driven by this passion, Leisha has worked with multiple organizations. During her time at Amnesty International, she was responsible for advocacy and research, promoting human rights principles and working to expose and oppose human rights violations, fighting for fairness and justice for those who have suffered persecution. At the International Rescue Committee, she learned that different groups have different needs, and services must be tailored to real-life circumstances. She collaborated with local residents and communities, listened to their voices and experiences, and co-designed and implemented more practical solutions to meet the needs of those they served.



Although her roles in these two organizations differed slightly, Leisha has always held the same belief: fighting for the rights of marginalized people. She firmly believes that to drive policy change, one must listen to the voices of local people and learn from their experiences. Moreover, when addressing issues related to vulnerability, exclusion, and abuse, it is essential to consciously consider human rights and cultural factors. These principles have driven her human rights work and helped her achieve significant results across different fields.


The harsh realities faced by ethnic minority women in the United States


Leisha's extensive experience in human rights protection has given her deep insights into gender-based violence and the challenges faced by ethnic minority women in the U.S., bringing a unique perspective to discussions on gender equality and women's rights.


Leisha believes that gender equality and women's rights are linked to broader social issues such as social and economic inequality and unequal political power structures. Only by extending discussions on gender equality and women's rights to a deeper level is true social transformation and justice possible. " "Power dynamics lie beneath both physical and sexual violence, as well as unequal access to resources." On the deeply entrenched issue of gender-based violence, Leisha believes it is crucial to understand its oppressive nature—only then can we truly recognize it as a tool used by patriarchy to maintain its own power.



Currently, gender-based violence in the United States is extremely severe, and ethnic minority women in the U.S. face discrimination and exclusion due to race, gender, and other factors, making it difficult for them to access necessary resources and support, leaving them more vulnerable to sexual violence. A U.S. Department of Justice survey report[1] shows that, on average, 5,900 sexual assaults are experienced annually by American Indians aged 12 and older. Compared to all other racial groups, Native Americans are twice as likely to experience sexual assault. According to the 2010–2012 National Intimate Partner and Sexual Violence Survey[2], 18% of Asian or Pacific Islander women have experienced rape, physical violence, and/or stalking by an intimate partner in their lifetime, compared to 48% of American Indian or Alaska Native women, 45% of Black women, 34% of Hispanic women, and 37% of White women.


"Marginalized identities affect the reporting of these incidents. We know that up to one in three women experience sexual violence in their lifetime, but in reality, when the victim holds a marginalized identity—such as being an ethnic minority woman—this number is far higher than one in three."



Leisha says addressing this situation requires considering "intersectionality." Different gender-based violence survivors have different life experiences, and they need tailored support and services. Therefore, solutions must be co-created with these survivors, making them co-creators in the process of their own healing and support.


Call to action, take action, create change


Despite the severe living conditions of ethnic minority women in the U.S., current policies and relief systems are insufficient to solve their problems. Leisha believes that in the U.S., the biggest challenge today is the lack of resources and support for marginalized groups, and government funding allocated to combating gender-based violence is also inadequate. Therefore, it is necessary to actively expand avenues to provide sustainable and flexible financial support for anti-gender-based violence organizations and those serving ethnic minority women.


However, she cautions that nonprofit organizations must consider the achievement of their own mission and the diversity of their funders, and no individual or department should unilaterally decide how funds are allocated and used, as this could compromise the neutrality and independence of the organization. Leisha gives an example: "Amnesty International conducts very strict reviews and screenings of its grant sources to ensure that donor requirements do not conflict with their values and independence."



To address the dire living conditions of ethnic minority women in the U.S., Leisha proposes multiple measures: first, the U.S. government must change relevant policies and strengthen efforts to combat and prevent gender-based violence; second, public awareness must be raised through advocacy, education, and training to deepen understanding of gender-based violence and foster public anti-gender-based violence consciousness; additionally, grassroots campaigns should be widely launched to encourage more groups to participate in efforts against gender-based violence and in protecting the rights of ethnic minority women.


Leisha believes that solving gender-based violence requires cultural recognition and attention to these issues, to help ethnic minority women improve their living conditions. She emphasizes that gender-based violence is an ongoing reality, and we should not spend excessive energy collecting data to prove the importance of addressing it, but instead investigate and analyze the root causes of gender-based violence. At the same time, she stresses that the U.S. government and all sectors of society must work together to provide more resources and support for ethnic minority women, ensuring they can access the rights and opportunities they deserve.


"Many people hold the mistaken belief that women already have power, that women already possess power. We must correct this misconception, support women, and help them truly gain power and recognize their own strength."


Thanks to the efforts of feminist activists like Leisha Beardmore, the world has already seen positive changes toward being more women-friendly. In the future, we hope governments, civil society, and individuals in more countries will work together and take strong measures to provide greater protection and support for women, promoting gender equality and the development of multiculturalism.
