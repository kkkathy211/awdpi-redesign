Observation and Research Report on New Anti-Domestic Violence Policies in Overseas Countries since the Pandemic

(2021 Edition)

April 2021‍




Key Findings of the Report

With the global outbreak of COVID-19, various forms of existing "inequality" in society have become increasingly evident. The issue of domestic violence is a concrete manifestation of many such "inequalities" within family life. Under pandemic conditions, for many women and children in some countries, "home" has also become a highly dangerous place. The worsening problem of domestic violence is gradually attracting attention and concern from society and international organizations.

Main Report



On November 23, 2020, Josep Borrell, Vice-President of the European Commission, issued a statement on domestic violence against women in Europe ahead of the International Day for the Elimination of Violence against Women. He stated that since EU countries implemented lockdown measures, violence against women and children has clearly increased: nearly one-third of women in EU member states have experienced violence, and domestic violence exists in almost every country, race, and community; regional lockdowns and home isolation measures brought by the pandemic have heightened both the likelihood and severity of domestic violence incidents.

01

Domestic Violence Worsens under the Global Pandemic


In practice across countries, cases of domestic violence have increased during the pandemic. Since the beginning of this year, the global fight against the pandemic remains severe. At the onset of the COVID-19 outbreak, domestic violence cases against women in Brazil surged by 50%, nearly 30% in France and New Zealand, about 25% in the UK, and around 20% in Spain. A survey by the European Institute for Gender Equality (EIGE) found that the average daily number of domestic violence hotline calls during the COVID-19 pandemic was significantly higher than in the same period in previous years. Italy's Ministry of Health, citing data from the National Institute of Statistics (ISTAT), reported a sharp increase in calls to domestic violence hotlines during lockdown, rising by 75% compared to the same period in 2019. Between March and June alone, calls and messages to the country's domestic violence hotline more than doubled, increasing by 119.6%.


During the first nationwide lockdown, domestic abuse cases in England and Wales accounted for one-fifth of all criminal cases. According to data from the UK Office for National Statistics (ONS), between April and June, crimes related to domestic abuse accounted for one-quarter of all cases. Between April and June, the UK helpline Refuge received a total of 40,397 calls, a 65% increase compared to the previous three months.


In terms of gender, women are the primary victims of domestic violence. According to data disclosed by the European Institute for Gender Equality, women accounted for 91% of the above-mentioned callers seeking help, while men accounted for 9%[1]. At the same time, the forms of domestic violence have also slightly changed compared to pre-pandemic times. A study released in July by the EU Agency for Law Enforcement Training (CEPOL) showed that domestic violence crime patterns in 23 European countries were associated with the spread of COVID-19, accounting for 72% of the surveyed countries. In Portugal and Spain, there has been a growing trend of sexual violence via social media and the internet. Due to lockdown policies, respondents also reported derivative issues of traditional domestic violence such as online fraud, online sexual violence, psychological abuse, and economic exploitation. In France, sexual harassment, rape, and attempted rape are the most common forms of sexual violence in domestic violence. During home isolation, children have witnessed violence against their mothers (such as beating, smashing objects, shouting, threats, and insults), which will undoubtedly trigger new chain reactions[2].

02

Crisis Events Are Linked to Domestic Violence


The incidence of domestic violence typically remains high for several months following disaster events. A study on the aftermath of natural disasters in the United States and Canada confirmed that domestic violence rates were significantly higher throughout the entire year following a disaster. During and even long after the COVID-19 pandemic, the likelihood of sustained high rates of domestic violence victims remains large[3].


After Hurricane Katrina in 2005, the probability of domestic violence among women affected in Mississippi increased nearly fourfold (Anastario, Shehab, & Lawry, 2009). After the 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake and tsunami (Fisher, 2010) and Australia's Black Saturday bushfires (Parkinson, 2019), domestic violence incidents rose rapidly to varying degrees in the short term. Evidence shows that the increase in domestic violence cases is not limited to the initial phase of disasters but remains high over the long term during post-disaster recovery periods.


The above literature indicates that the rise in domestic violence cases may stem from pre-existing social problems, as well as psychological stress, economic downturns, and displacement caused by disasters. The COVID-19 pandemic has caused significant impacts on the social ecology worldwide. Resulting issues such as unemployment, school dropouts, illness, hunger, economic hardship, and social isolation may exacerbate domestic violence. The 2020 World Health Summit estimated that approximately 500 million jobs would be lost globally, about 24 million children would drop out of school, and the global economy could lose up to $375 billion per month.

03

Key Triggers of Domestic Violence during the Pandemic


The World Health Organization (WHO) has provided a clear definition of "Intimate Partner Violence" (IPV): IPV refers to acts causing physical, sexual, or psychological harm by a current or former intimate partner, including physical assault, forced sexual acts, psychological abuse, and controlling behaviors. The United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA) predicted in 2020 that during the COVID-19 pandemic, "intimate partner violence" could increase twentyfold across the 193 UN member states. The main reasons stem from the following three aspects:


a.

Pandemic isolation policies create difficulties for victims seeking help and for supporters providing assistance. Affected by lockdown orders, victims are forced to stay in the same household as their abusers, making violent acts by partners more unavoidable. In more extreme cases, abusers may obstruct women's access to communication tools and prohibit victims from seeking help from the outside world. The pandemic has also prevented many social support organizations from operating normally, reducing the likelihood of victims receiving formal or informal help. Layered isolation makes it difficult for external forces to rescue victims.

b.

The pandemic acts as a catalyst, intensifying existing violent behaviors. A study conducted in Nigeria revealed that in most cases, "intimate partner violence" had already shown signs before pandemic lockdowns; the pandemic and lockdown policies increased both the frequency and types of violence. Some victims had already suffered physical violence before the outbreak, and under the pandemic, the psychological, economic, and sexual violence they experienced worsened. Abusers may also threaten to expel female victims from the home, and vulnerable women naturally worry about losing access to essential transportation, social, and medical services during the pandemic.

c.

The economic burden brought by the pandemic has become another trigger for violence. The pandemic has affected income sources for certain groups. Rising unemployment and economic recession during the pandemic mean many women cannot support themselves or their children if they leave their partner's residence. At the same time, men may feel frustrated due to job loss and take it out on their partners.


04

Possible Factors Contributing to the Emergence and Escalation of Domestic Violence


A series of studies prior to the pandemic outbreak showed that domestic violence is often directly linked to women's personal experiences and family relationships.


Women's personal experiences are closely associated with intimate partner violence. For example, women who experienced abuse in childhood, or have histories of smoking, alcohol abuse, or drug misuse, are more likely to suffer intimate partner violence. Harmful habits such as alcohol abuse can lead to physical weakness and mental distraction, weakening women's ability to avoid violence. Alcohol abuse may also lead to neglect of partners, increasing tension in relationships. Women's level of education is also linked to intimate partner violence—higher education levels correlate with lower risk of victimization. The reason is obvious: education can provide women with necessary knowledge and skills to boost their confidence in relationships, thereby reducing the likelihood of physical violence; Compared to partners without education, educated partners may receive greater attention and respect, further preventing violence. Violence also has an intergenerational transmission effect—women who experienced violence in childhood are more likely to suffer violence in adulthood. Violent events between parents also affect their children's future marital lives.


On the other hand, societal intervention methods also significantly impact victim groups. Abused women are often confined at home, in a relatively isolated social state. Existing social intervention approaches often urge victims to immediately leave their abusers, artificially creating a safe distance. However, this may cause victims to drift away from their only social contacts—family and friends. Abused women often carry psychological burdens and struggle to express their feelings appropriately. Current social intervention mechanisms fail to provide diverse support tailored to different victims' needs, potentially causing secondary harm to victims.


In addition, economic status, social status, and emotional factors are also possible causes contributing to domestic violence.

05

Anti-domestic violence mechanisms in traditional contexts


From existing practices, current anti-domestic violence mechanisms can be divided into three levels: public education, early warning mechanisms, and assistance mechanisms. These three levels of prevention and protection work before, during, and after incidents of violence to prevent, intervene in, and correct violent behaviors, collectively serving to protect victims and deter domestic violence.



(1)

Public education

is a primary prevention mechanism

Many factors that may lead to intimate partner violence first emerge during adolescence.


Behaviors such as fighting, drug use, and gambling often plant the seeds of domestic violence at this stage. Therefore, promoting awareness of domestic violence through school education can not only effectively reduce violent behaviors among youth but also lower the likelihood of continued criminal behavior in adulthood. As a primary preventive measure, public education has a pre-emptive effect—by raising adolescents' awareness of domestic violence and informing them about available support after victimization, it helps prevent problems before they occur.


As early as 1998, the UK government launched educational programs on preventing violence against women, gradually establishing school-based education as a primary preventive measure against domestic violence. Many primary and secondary schools in the UK have introduced science-based courses on domestic violence, aiming to raise students' awareness, foster correct understandings of intimate relationships, promote healthy attitudes toward violence, and cultivate healthy emotional perspectives. In form, the UK education authorities also use youth counseling services, drama performances on related themes, and other methods to prevent domestic violence. Evidence shows that these activities not only instill correct methods for resolving violent conflicts but also reduce gender discriminatory attitudes and behaviors. This helps strengthen young people's deeper understanding of topics such as "intimate relationships" and "violence."


However, the effectiveness and significance of public education are also subject to debate. Some argue that the impact of such programs is only temporary: on one hand, a few classes struggle to directly change deep-rooted traditional beliefs, and most interventions fail to sustain long-term; on the other hand, most school teachers lack systematic training on the topic, leaving them well-intentioned but under-equipped, which affects the quality and effectiveness of instruction. Public education often yields slow results and is difficult to show significant outcomes in the short term. Yet among various measures to prevent domestic violence, educating youth is undoubtedly extremely important. By promoting healthy ways to build relationships and resolve violent conflicts, young people can better master healthy methods of expressing emotions and opinions, which to some extent can challenge outdated beliefs and incorrect attitudes of previous generations, thereby generating immeasurable positive impacts on the future.


(2)

Early warning mechanisms

are secondary prevention mechanisms

The early stages, when signs of domestic violence first appear, are critical windows for effective intervention.


Timely police response to domestic violence can not only directly reduce or even eliminate violent incidents but also indirectly educate the public, playing a positive preventive role. In view of this, governments worldwide have established corresponding early warning and punitive mechanisms for domestic violence.


In Merseyside, UK, local police have established an emergency alarm system for domestic violence victims. Victims can use a pendant system to quickly trigger an alarm. Local police have also created a dedicated telephone database for such "domestic" incidents. When police receive a call, they can check the address's prior incident records. The abuser's file is recorded in local social service agencies' databases, allowing employers to screen job applicants accordingly, thus deterring potential abusers. Laws in all U.S. states authorize police to arrest suspected domestic violence offenders without a warrant. Police are obligated to inform victims of their available protections, escort victims to medical facilities, and explain the procedures for applying for civil protection orders.


(3)

Assistance mechanisms

constitute tertiary prevention mechanisms

Tertiary prevention refers to rescue measures taken after domestic violence has occurred, aiming to improve the relationship between parties, mitigate harm, and prevent reoffending. It serves as a remedial and secondary preventive measure complementing the first two levels. Protection order policies and shelter systems are key components of this mechanism.


The civil protection order system originated in the United States, used to prohibit abusers from contacting victims in specific times and places, from having custody of children, or from possessing firearms for a certain period. In the U.S., protection orders typically last 1 to 2 years and are relatively easy to obtain. Victims can apply directly via phone or by filing a complaint at court. Regarding shelters, grassroots efforts in the U.S. to assist domestic violence victims began relatively early.


In 1974, the U.S. established its first shelter dedicated specifically to women victims of domestic violence. To date, the U.S. has over ten national non-governmental organizations dedicated to combating domestic violence, running numerous related projects. France has a similar "protection order" policy, lasting four months, with extensions available if the victim decides to divorce during that period.

06

Recent anti-domestic violence policies in selected European countries


At the national level, several European countries have introduced emergency relief policies.



France is one of the European countries with more serious domestic violence problems. Hans Kluge, WHO Regional Director for Europe, believes France is one of the countries most affected by domestic violence recently, with a sharp rise in violent cases linked to unemployment, alcohol abuse, drug use, and growing fear. Within one month of the lockdown policy introduced on March 17, reports of domestic violence across France increased by 30%. In Paris alone, violence against women rose by 36%. The social media account of French women's rights organization feminicide revealed that by October 2020, there had been 70 cases of female domestic violence deaths nationwide.


  • France relies on both government and civil society forces to provide shelters and confidential channels for victims to seek help.

Last March, to address the severe domestic violence situation, the French government pledged 20,000 hotel rooms for sheltering abused women and donated 1 million euros (approximately 7 million RMB) to anti-domestic violence organizations, setting up aid points in supermarkets and pharmacies nationwide. In September, French President Macron announced an additional 1,000 emergency shelters and enhanced police training. Victims of domestic violence in France can call 3919 for help. Authorities have expanded the capacity of existing hotlines, allowing women suffering domestic violence to send a text message to trigger an alert, ensuring they can seek help privately and safely.


In addition, since the lockdown began, pharmacies have been designated as safe spaces where women can speak freely. By using the code "Mask 19," women can subtly signal for help from pharmacists and receive assistance from authorities. Germany, Italy, Spain, Norway, and Argentina have also adopted similar approaches to better serve victim groups.


  • The German government continues to provide support services and shelters for women and maintains close contact with women's organizations.


The German government has allocated 120 million euros (approximately 960 million RMB) specifically to create and support the "Against Violence towards Women" project during the COVID-19 crisis. Measures such as expanding women's shelters provide assistance to vulnerable women. The government-funded "Violence Against Women" project will continue to offer 365-day, round-the-clock support. Victims can anonymously contact over eighty professionally trained support staff via phone, message, or email to receive free professional guidance. In response to Germany's current multicultural, multi-ethnic, and multilingual society, the project also offers multilingual support in 17 languages.


The German government also provides social basic security for women's housing and daily life: recent legislation makes it easier for women to access social welfare services. In some German cities, government and civil organizations have rented hotel and vacation homes for women experiencing domestic violence, enabling victims to seek help and emergency shelter nearby.


In addition, the German government continues to conduct online anti-domestic violence campaigns, launching a dedicated website Stärker als Gewalt (stronger than violence) to provide practical and effective solutions for potential domestic violence victims.


  • The UK has adjusted its pandemic control measures to provide more accessible assistance pathways for domestic violence victims.


In response to the nationwide surge in domestic violence incidents during the pandemic, the UK government allocated 76 million pounds during the first lockdown to support vulnerable groups, including domestic violence victims. Of this, 1 million pounds was allocated in September last year to 25 domestic violence charities to provide additional shelter beds. The UK government, in partnership with charities, has allocated an additional 2 million pounds to launch a rescue hotline for victims of "domestic violence" and launched the "You Are Not Alone" campaign for people at risk of domestic violence, providing support services. The UK government stated that if victims need to leave home to escape domestic violence, the stay-at-home policy does not apply, and has provided extensive service information including online support, hotlines, and shelters.

07

Globally the latest developments in anti-domestic violence policies


UNDP's database shows that since the pandemic, a total of 704 policies targeting domestic violence have been issued globally, accounting for 28% of all gender-related policies. Based on the three-tier anti-domestic violence mechanism mentioned earlier, these policies can be further divided into preventive policies, assistance-seeking policies, and protective policies.



Preventive policies mainly increase public awareness of anti-domestic violence through social media and community outreach.


Globally, 88 countries have issued 126 preventive policies, making up 18% of all domestic violence policies. Greece used government-run social media platforms to raise public awareness about domestic violence during the pandemic. The Portuguese government partnered with local institutions, gas stations, and pharmacies to provide information on domestic violence support. Civil society organizations in Poland launched the "Safe Plan" campaign on social media, promoting emergency responses when danger arises, thus preparing for domestic violence in advance. Germany created an anti-domestic violence website called Stärker als Gewalt (Stronger than Violence). Online anti-domestic violence campaigns were conducted. Countries including Kenya, Ireland, Peru, and Colombia also promoted help-seeking channels through media.


Assistance-seeking policies refer to ways victims can seek help from outside after experiencing domestic violence, including calling hotlines, online consultations, and in-person medical services.


Many countries have provided financial support to domestic violence support agencies to ensure their normal operation. Spain added a new feature to AlertCops (a mobile app). It locates women experiencing domestic violence and records the scene for 10 seconds after the emergency button is pressed, preserving evidence. France and Greece enhanced their official hotlines by adding psychological and medical counseling services to assist women in seeking help remotely. Malaysia's Women's Aid Organisation extended its hotline service to 24 hours a day and expanded its online resources to radio, television, and the internet.


Protective policies refer to the protection women receive after seeking help, such as shelter services and follow-ups on legal appeals.


During the pandemic, French courts prioritized domestic violence cases, effectively protecting women and holding perpetrators accountable through hearings or issuing protection orders. The Belgian government required police to conduct follow-up phone calls to women who reported domestic violence, ensuring their safety during isolation. Greece and Portugal ensured shelters remained operational during the pandemic, providing temporary housing. The UK and Spain allowed women needing to leave home for shelter to be exempt from pandemic-related quarantine measures to safeguard their personal safety. Some German cities rented hotels and vacation homes for female victims, enabling them to seek urgent help and emergency shelter nearby. Serbia provided hygiene products and household essentials to 11 safe houses nationwide.

08

Summary and Reflection


Unemployment, social isolation, and psychological stress brought by the pandemic are the main causes of domestic violence emergence and escalation. Domestic violence often intensifies during crises, making anti-domestic violence efforts during the pandemic especially valuable. Previous research indicates that domestic violence is associated with women's personal experiences, societal intervention methods, and many other factors.


Under pandemic conditions, weakened connections for victims, pre-existing violent behaviors within families, and economic burdens have become key triggers for domestic violence. Current anti-domestic violence mechanisms can be divided into three levels: public education, early warning systems, and assistance mechanisms, which work respectively to prevent, intervene, and correct violent behaviors before, during, and after incidents. These three elements complement each other and jointly contribute to mitigating and eliminating domestic violence. From a national perspective, several European countries have introduced emergency relief policies to address the sharp rise in domestic violence cases. Globally, governments have made significant efforts in combating domestic violence, with policies broadly categorized into preventive, assistance-seeking, and protective types, corresponding to the three levels of the anti-domestic violence mechanism.


However, these policies are not flawless. Their gender focus is primarily on women, which may to some extent overlook support mechanisms for male victims. A 2017 WHO report pointed out that men can also experience violence from their partners. Statistics show that economic violence (32.8%) is common among men, followed by emotional violence (22.2%), physical violence (25.2%), and sexual violence (17.7%). In terms of domestic violence intervention, support services for men are limited. However, as societal power relations, norms, and values are changing, domestic violence should be recognized as a public health issue, and both men and women should have equal rights and access to appropriate interventions.


On the other hand, intimate relationships may often conceal the reality of domestic violence. A previous study in France revealed that about 90% of the surveyed women would disclose the violence they suffered to a third party, potentially leading to reporting to the police; however, if the perpetrator had an intimate relationship with the respondent, only 55% of the respondents would choose to disclose it to a third party or report to the police. If the sample is narrowed down to women who are foreign immigrants, the probability of reporting to the police is even smaller. The underlying reasons may include fear that the partner will find out about the report or help-seeking, leading to relationship deterioration; female immigrants overseas may also fear that police will show bias toward male perpetrators from the same ethnic group. Additionally, some immigrant women may feel they are not taken seriously by the judicial system and are experiencing systemic oppression. Some North African cases suggest that if the woman's country of origin already has serious gender injustice, the likelihood of her enduring violence in silence increases.‍‍‍‍‍


The novel coronavirus has caused massive shocks across social, economic, and other domains. Domestic violence during the pandemic has significant negative impacts on individuals, groups, and society as a whole. Addressing domestic violence is also a crucial component of public health governance. How to enhance understanding of domestic violence and thereby suppress and resolve the issue remains a topic worthy of global attention for the foreseeable future.
