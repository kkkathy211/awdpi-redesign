"Has your life ever come to a halt at a place where 'everyone says it's good,' yet you still feel something is off?"



In mainstream narratives, we are taught to follow 「the right path 」: get into a good school, join a big company, become a daughter, partner, and colleague to be proud of. But when these "standard successes" no longer bring genuine fulfillment, when we feel suffocated in relationships and lost in choices, how can women redefine themselves?


On September 6, 2025, the AWDPI Overseas Chinese Women Protection Project will host a lecture titled "Redefining the Self: Her Practice in Life Transitions and Relationship Roles", inviting three outstanding women from the fields of psychology, anthropology, and counseling to share honestly across three dimensions: career transition, intimate relationships, and self-narrative. They did not offer


a "perfect solution," but through their stories, they showed us 」the possibility of 「becoming ourselves. 」This time, we will no longer follow old frameworks, but instead use a language that belongs to women to tell a different story of growth. Featured Speakers



One

Kara

Independent anthropological researcher and curator, founder of Kapila Studio, Master of Anthropology from Fudan University,

Master of Heritage Studies from Leiden University, the Netherlands

"Hate Every Job You Do? Create Your Own Instead:

The Journey of an Anthropologist Building a One-Person Company" From academic inquiry to questioning and turning point:



1

The instrumentalization of humanities and social sciences and value conflicts

Kara began her talk with a formal self-introduction, reflecting on her journey


from academia to the workplace, from confusion to founding her own studio. She had specialized in anthropology from undergraduate to doctoral studies, initially passionate about academia, but gradually realized during her PhD—"The papers we write seem to merely wrap common knowledge in fancy language." This long-standing confusion led her to question whether academic research could truly make a tangible impact, a realization that became a turning point in her life.. To explore paths beyond academia, she worked as a user researcher at design firms, iQiyi, and vivo. While these roles allowed her to experience the "practicality" of research, they also revealed structural issues—research was often marginalized, used merely to justify decisions, and sometimes even violated personal ethics (such as designing features to keep teenagers addicted to games). This made her realize, she said:


"I don't want to become a mere 'tool person' who only makes PowerPoint slides." Turning to art and public expression:


2

Reopening possibilities in life

After leaving large corporations, Kara returned to her passion for museums and art, and planned to pursue a master's in heritage studies in the Netherlands. While waiting to go abroad, she joined an artist-in-residence program and was first exposed to "field research by artists." This seemed to offer her another path:


she didn't have to become an artist herself, but could use anthropological methods to participate in the creative process. In the Netherlands, she co-organized a workshop with a female physical theater practitioner, using bodily expression to explore "What does 'Chinese' mean?" and engaged in deep conversations with local East Asian women. She was surprised to discover: even without relying on existing systems, she could still do meaningful work.


"I never imagined before that someone could truly be moved by what I do—moved to tears on the spot." Public pathways for humanities and social sciences:


3

How to balance values and survival

After returning to China, she began experimenting with new media for "research." She launched public accounts on WeChat and Xiaohongshu, sharing content on humanities and design, and initiated several experimental projects, such as


ecological narrative board games, reading community groups, co-written essay collections, exhibitions and in-depth field experiences, and ecological village practices. At the end of the lecture, Kara also addressed the audience's common concern: "How do you sustain a livelihood?" She admitted


At the end of the lecture, Cara also addressed the audience's common concern about "how to make a living." She frankly admitted Though her income is far below what she could earn at large corporations, she chooses a low-consumption lifestyle to preserve creative freedom. She insists on an evaluation framework of "interest-driven + user needs + sustainability" to avoid being swayed by the market. For humanities students uncertain about their future, she offered four suggestions:



Be honest with yourself:

1

Wanting to earn money is not shameful; being suited for a stable job is fine too. The key is finding your authentic priorities. Bridge information gaps:

2

Pay attention to industry cases and job information, and explore possibilities you don't yet know. Test with minimal units:

3

Start with part-time work, volunteering, or short-term projects, and use action to test your interests and abilities. Recognize transferable skills:

4

"Soft skills" like critical thinking, communication and coordination, and cultural understanding are the true treasures of humanities graduates. Featured Speakers


Two

Qiu Siyu

Master of Mental Health Psychology from the University of Edinburgh, UK; Head of the Psychological Division at AWDPI; Founder of "Moshengren," a global public welfare psychological counseling network for Chinese people

"I See You—But I Don't Want to See Myself":

A Psychological Dialogue on Relationships, Struggles, and "Awakening" Why can't women "see themselves"?



1

The expectations of gender roles

❝ Why is my talk titled "I See You—But I Don't Want to See Myself"? ❞


Siyu shared that this is one of the most common expressions she hears from female clients in counseling—they pour themselves into "seeing others" in relationships, yet often fear looking back at themselves. She pointed out:


"Not seeing oneself" is a psychological defense mechanism formed after long-term suppression, and also a product of societal gender role expectations. Siyu quoted a widely shared line from the movie *Barbie*: "You have to be thin, but you can't say you want to be thin… You have to be the boss, but not too assertive… You must stay grateful, even though the system is unfair…" She emphasized that


this is not an individual problem, but the long-term impact of an entire cultural system on women. Faced with these contradictions, what we need is not to suppress emotions, but first to allow ourselves: "I can have different thoughts." Family and intimate relationships:.


2

Women's self-neglect and emotional labor

In the second part of her talk, Siyu focused on women's role dilemmas in partnerships and intimate relationships. She noted that


many women carry excessive emotional labor in relationships, yet often receive unequal responses, and even habitually neglect themselves and suppress their needs. At the family level, she used the psychological concepts of


family conscience and intergenerational loyalty to reveal the emotional role displacement women experience in their families of origin. Family conscience represents the inherited values and aesthetics of sacrifice passed down through generations. Within this framework, caregiver, sacrificer, and peacemaker roles are often automatically assigned to women. While working hard outside, they are also expected to maintain family harmony. The emotional detachment required under such high-functioning states leaves many women overburdened, unable to express their true feelings. At the


intimate relationship level, she pointed out that women often become the maintainers of the relationship. They invest more emotional labor but receive unequal returns. After a relationship ends, women are more likely to fall into a "bankruptcy-style" compensatory mindset. She emphasized: "A relationship is co-created by two people, not a space for one person's emotional rescue." And she encouraged women to reflect: "Besides yourself, what else in life is worth your effort?" How can "she" reshape the self?


3

The courage and right to choose

Siyu used the psychological theory of "self-differentiation" to explain:


true growth is not about cutting off relationships, but about setting boundaries and finding balance between loyalty and selfhood. Self-differentiation means emotional independence, no longer being driven by the voice that says "I must." She especially emphasized that self-differentiation does not mean cutting ties, but establishing new boundaries and relational dynamics. "I still love my family, but I can live my own life.". At the end of her talk, Siyu invited the audience to try the



"Time Machine Blessing Exercise": Write to your past self at important life junctures and ask: "What do I want to say to you now? What blessing am I willing to give you?" Through this practice, women can learn to dialogue with and coexist with their past selves, and understand who they once were. She stressed:


"Choice is the beginning of power." Whether to stay as you are or take a new step, it should come from "I want to," not "I have to." Featured Speakers

Three

Grace

Grace

Double degree in Psychology and Economics from the University of Virginia; Narrative Therapy Counselor (with over 1,300 hours of counseling experience); Founder of Dodo | Du Space; Curator of "Liu Jing Yuan Xin," a mind-body practice community

"Thirty and Beyond: From Corporate Elite to Personal Venture—Exploring and Reflecting on Transition"



1

From linear success to inner calling:

The exploration and transformation of elite women


Grace's life path seemed like the textbook answer: a double degree in Psychology and Economics from the University of Virginia, followed by a swift entry into a top-tier management consulting firm, then moving to a tech startup and participating in projects worth tens of millions. To the outside world, she was the reliable, professional, and capable elite. Yet, years of accumulated high pressure and a lack of genuine achievement led her to reflect—what she had been striving to maintain, was it her true desire, or just a reflection of others' expectations?


Outside of work, she secretly wrote novels, took writing classes, and quietly enrolled in an introductory course on psychological counseling. The narrative therapy course made her realize: our understanding of our own lives is actually a story that has been designed. She realized that "elite" is also a narrative framework, we don't truly choose freely, but have long been conditioned about what constitutes a valuable life. So, she began gradually letting go of titles, KPIs, and time anxiety, stepping into the "ambiguous."


2

Creating structure within ambiguity: narrative healing and community practice


The first concept Grace learned in narrative therapy was: "The problem is not you; the problem is the problem. The relationship between you and the problem can change." She believes the mainstream "standard life script" does not fit everyone, and we need to create our own narrative space.


To help more people rewrite their life scripts, Grace developed several simple yet powerful narrative and embodied exploration tools. The first is Life Map p. Users map key life events (turning points, traumas, breakthroughs, losses), analyze the "hidden resources" that helped them through difficult times, and rediscover the strength they have always had. The second is Career Narrative Cards, which use "voice cards" to distinguish inner desires from others' expectations, and "symbol cards" to tell the career dreams they truly want but have never dared to voice. Through dialogue with themselves, users break free from the binary trap of "stability vs. success."


Grace also uses Body Memory Workshops to help participants release accumulated emotions and suppressed voices. She points out, women have long been trained to be "emotional managers" and "relationship maintainers," yet lack space to express their own desires and pain—and these activities are precisely created for that purpose.


3

Women's narratives and structural reflection:

From "self-blame" to "structural critique"


Grace reveals a common struggle she frequently encounters in counseling: women unable to make requests in intimate relationships, fearing they'll damage the connection; over-demanding of themselves at work, afraid of not being taken seriously, feeling shame when their efficiency dips; often asking, "Is it that I'm not good enough somewhere?" She points out that these are not "individual personality issues," but the result of societal narrative constraints on women.



Drawing from her clients' developmental paths, she summarizes five stages of women's narrative development:

Passive Acceptance Stage

Following the standard script, unaware of the predicament.

Awareness of Injustice Stage

Doubts arise, but expression may not be possible

Angry Awakening Stage

Beginning to resist, yet prone to internal exhaustion

Experience Integration Stage

Understanding past experiences, reconstructing meaning

Active Commitment Stage

Aiming to create impact, supporting others


Grace emphasizes finally, telling one's own story is not just psychological healing, but also a form of social practice. She believes "healing" is no longer a silent, individual effort, but a public act where women bear witness to one another.


In the life narratives of the three speakers, we see the tearing of frameworks, the loosening of roles, the rebuilding of boundaries, and also new practices growing from the cracks of our times.


They tell us: "Walking an uncertain path is far more worthwhile than completing a certain one."


In intimate relationships, daring to express needs; in career transitions, listening to inner callings; in life scripts, rewriting one's own name—this is precisely the meaning of "her" practice.
