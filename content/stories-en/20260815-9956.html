In 2022, Kylan was working at a design studio. After learning about his experience with AWDPI, his boss asked, "No money?"

Kylan replied, "No money."


"You're still doing it without pay?"

"Yes, I'm still doing it even without pay."


A year later, under a recruitment post shared by Kylan on social media, the boss commented again, "You're still doing it?"

This time, Kylan replied as always, "Yes, still doing it."


This year marks the fifth year since Kylan joined AWDPI. He brought his professional experience in brand design to the organization's communications team. As AWDPI's chief visual designer, his work spans from Lunar New Year red envelopes to the main visual designs for Women's Day events, appearing across AWDPI's social media platforms.


To this day, the header and footer designs he helped create continue to appear in every published article, becoming an indispensable visual signature.



Seeing the elephant in the room



Is there really an elephant in the room? What does it look like? Like pillars, like palm-leaf fans, or like long tubes?


For many people, the process of exploring feminism is like blind men touching an elephant. But before touching the elephant, one must first recognize and acknowledge its presence.

Image source: the book *The Elephant in the Room*


Kylan's introduction to feminism came through *Bafenban* and *Kan Li Xiang*. From Liang Wendao's repeated analyses of gender-related social issues to the articles on feminism published by *Kan Li Xiang*, he gradually pieced together his initial understanding of feminism.


"Feminism" was once a very unfamiliar term in Kylan's life. As the only son in his family, he grew up without contrast or guidance from the other gender. His older female cousins were over ten years his senior, and they had almost no common language. What their lives were truly like remained an unanswered question throughout his childhood.


And through films, Kylan found a mirror of real life.


In 2019, the film *Kim Ji-young, Born 1982*, adapted from the bestselling book, was released. The film focuses on the life struggles faced by a married South Korean woman born in the 1980s. In the film, Ji-young's life is filled with endless, invisible housework, and even at her mother-in-law's home, she cannot rest. When she finally receives a gift from her mother-in-law, she opens it to find only an apron for housework.


"That scene was so direct—it felt almost like a mirror reflecting my impression of the women elders in my own family." said Kylan.


In *The Republic*, Socrates uses the allegory of the cave to describe the journey toward truth: if a man were forced to look directly at the firelight itself, his eyes would hurt, and he would turn away, retreating back to the images he could see clearly and which he truly believed were clearer and more real than the things being pointed out to him.


Kylan also experienced the painful shock of facing the truth.


During university, he deeply admired a mentor, saying, "At the time, I thought this was what a designer should be like." The mentor, an alumnus from Kylan's high school, was very supportive, bringing Kylan—then only a sophomore—into events normally reserved for graduate students. He not only recognized Kylan's design talent but also frequently encouraged him to start a business and build a career of his own.


However, when speaking to female students, the mentor would sometimes casually say: "Anyway, in the end you'll either become a teacher or go back to your hometown."



Looking into the mentor's earnest eyes and hearing his patient advice, Kylan suddenly felt disoriented. The care, support, and opportunities he had received standing beside the mentor now felt, in that moment, as if they had been taken from someone else.


A similar story happened with Kylan's university girlfriend. As graduation approached, he, from Suzhou, wanted to return home for work, while his girlfriend wanted to stay in Shanghai to pursue her career. This real-life dilemma led to their breakup. Yet after graduation, with his parents' support, Kylan stayed in Shanghai, while his girlfriend returned to her hometown to become a teacher.


"Why must men always succeed?" Kylan wondered.


In countless nights of self-reflection, Kylan set up the mentor who encouraged him to "achieve greatness" as a figure to dialogue with, questioning the direction of his future life.


But this self-reflection did not bring him closer to worldly success; instead, the resulting anxiety and pressure shattered his previously peaceful inner world.


"It wasn't until these past few years, or maybe after graduation when I'd distanced myself a bit from him, that my feelings became clearer. I realized this kind of encouragement was very male-oriented, and the pressure it brought me was immense. So later, I gradually removed him from my habits and mindset," he said frankly.



The distance between seeing and doing



"Seeing the elephant in the room" is only the first step.


After learning about women's struggles, some men instinctively fall into a paternalistic "savior" mindset, while others feel powerless due to the scale of social issues. Kylan once wavered between these two mindsets.


"The word 'savior' is well put," Kylan admitted. At first, when he noticed unfairness at home, he would habitually say to his mother: "You don't have to keep washing dishes. You can go do your own things, plan your retirement life, and stop centering everything around others."


But later, he developed a different perspective: "My mother grew up in a traditional family environment. If I use so-called rationality to demand change from her, I'm actually erasing and negating the value of her decades of sacrifice. That would be a kind of indirect harm."


After that, he changed his approach and positioned himself as a "co-creator" and "supporter." Once, his mother told him she wanted to rent a courtyard in a small village near Suzhou after retirement to live a pastoral life. This time, Kylan didn't talk about "independence" or "self-fulfillment." Instead, he gently said to his mother, "If you like it, I'll help you pay the rent."


Rather than rushing to overturn everything with anger, Kylan chose to offer his mother material support and emotional encouragement. Helping her improve her life while respecting her choices—that, he realized, was a more powerful and natural way to act as an ally.


Kylan's further growth in mindset was also deeply influenced by other AWDPI volunteers. Among them, the person who impacted Kylan the most was Yimar, the organization's founder.


When Kylan first met Yimar, he was shocked by her youth, but was quickly moved by her extraordinary determination and execution ability. No matter which time zone she was in or how heavy her academic workload, Yimar pursued excellence in every campaign and every piece of material, communicating details with responsible team members early every morning.


At an offline homestay meetup, while everyone else was casually eating and having fun, Yimar was the only one pulling person after person into corners to go over work content and details. "A girl with such high energy and drive," Kylan remarked.


You get what you pay for—spend banana money and you can only hire monkeys. Yet in a team that didn't even have bananas, relying entirely on volunteers, everyone not only avoided slacking off but showed a fearless determination to treat this public welfare work like a real "career." At offline gatherings, Kylan naturally grew close to his teammates through shared values and a common cause, gradually becoming close friends who could talk about anything.


Does design have gender?



Over the years volunteering at AWDPI, Kylan gradually became aware of his own limitations as a designer.



Once, when he was in charge of designing the organization's logo, Kylan instinctively used classic elements like bows and traditionally "feminine" pink colors. When other team members saw the design, they immediately asked him, "Should we remove this element?"


Kylan's use of traditional feminine elements and colors had been completely subconscious, but this volunteer experience made him more sensitive to such "automatic" choices. Later, when designing a handbook against domestic violence, he created a powerful female figure holding a flag, with a color scheme no longer the "girly pink" of before, but a bold orange—burning like summer sunlight.


Does design have gender?


As a designer with years of industry experience, Kylan believes


that design indeed leverages certain differences between genders to shape brands and guide people's imagination about different genders. For example, some women's clothing brands use lace or bows to create a cool, vintage ladylike image; some cosmetics brands use specific tableware or jewelry elements to portray a dazzling, refined lifestyle.


In Kylan's view, these carefully packaged styles essentially use ready-made stereotypes to construct dreams that trigger consumer desire.



This approach is effective, but it also silently makes women passively accept these symbols, rather than exploring their own truly comfortable states in reality. Yet in companies with higher price points and greater aesthetic sensibility, Kylan noticed a different, somewhat remarkable phenomenon—these workplaces often have many female, transgender, and queer colleagues, who are also more likely to be entrusted with key design roles.  


He attributes this phenomenon to different shopping sensitivities across groups. Straight men often prioritize efficiency and cost-effectiveness when buying things. "Like buying a razor—just pick one that has the most features within budget and looks decent enough, pay, and leave. The whole process is clean and quick. They want something functional, no extra romance needed."


But non-straight male groups, when shopping, have more nuanced perceptions of things beyond functionality. They pay attention to the atmosphere, emotional value, and psychological experience a product might bring.


But non-heterosexual male groups have a more nuanced perception of things beyond functionality when shopping. They pay attention to the atmosphere, emotional value, and psychological experience a product might bring.


For example, when buying a cosmetic product, some people pursue the pleasurable feeling of "I am wealthy and refined" during its use. "Straight men may find this unrealistic, but for many, this psychological experience during usage is often the key factor that elevates the product's perceived value and willingness to pay."


The straight male market is actually narrow; when cost-effectiveness becomes the top priority, the space for premium pricing is squeezed to the minimum. In contrast, non-straight-male user groups are more willing to pay for emotional and aesthetic added value beyond functionality, and these extra added values are precisely the most profitable, high-premium segments in the commercial market.



"If you're pursuing business, what you're actually pursuing is this high-premium segment," said Kylan. Therefore, because it requires a keen ability to capture and design works with high emotional value and commercial benefit, women and queer individuals are often more likely to be recognized and valued in design and creative industries.


However, in women-related nonprofit organizations, male volunteers are still relatively rare.


Misunderstanding and curiosity from others are common, but there are rarely malicious attacks or sarcasm. "Engaging in public welfare actually leaves little room for people's malice to manifest," Kylan said. For him, joining AWDPI and building a new community—finding like-minded companions in an atomized society—is almost entirely beneficial with no drawbacks.


For those hesitating about whether to join a nonprofit organization, Kylan's advice is: Let go of "grand ideals"—"If a single nonprofit organization could fulfill your ideals, then it would be too easy."


Kylan said that as long as a person consistently persists in doing something positive, they will eventually reach the place they wish to go.


"Creating one more graphic, revising one more layout—perhaps that could help more people see it, let more survivors of sexual violence know about existing rescue channels, make their suffering visible, and ensure our efforts are not overlooked. Perhaps ten, fifty, or a hundred years from now, the world will be slightly better because someone a century ago had the courage to speak up—and this is precisely the meaning of our existence."
