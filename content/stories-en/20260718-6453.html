One

Spotlight: The Rare Appearance of Chinese Women





Since their establishment in 1936, the six top mathematical honors—Fields Medal, Abel Prize, Shaw Prize, Wolf Prize, Crafoord Prize, and Breakthrough Prize—have awarded a total of 251 honors, with 242 going to men and 9 to women, accounting for 3.59%. Among them, Claire Voisin has won twice, meaning only eight women have truly received these awards, and all of their awards were concentrated in or after 2014. This means that throughout the 78-year history, not a single woman had ever stood on these podiums.



On April 18, 2026, the annual Breakthrough Prize list was announced, with Wang Hong and Tang Yunqing winning the New Horizons in Mathematics Prize. Meanwhile, Zhang Mingjia received the Maryam Mirzakhani New Frontiers Prize—an award specifically established for women mathematicians who have recently obtained their PhDs. All three graduated from the School of Mathematical Sciences at Peking University and are under the age of 40.

Wang Hong, born in 1991 in Guilin, Guangxi, shook the mathematics world by proving the three-dimensional Kakeya conjecture and is considered a strong candidate for the 2026 Fields Medal. Tang Yunqing is the first Chinese woman to win the Cole Prize in Number Theory, an award that for the first time since its establishment in 1931 has been awarded to a Chinese woman. Zhang Mingjia, a post-95s researcher, studied under Fields Medalist Peter Scholze.


The simultaneous appearance of three Chinese women mathematicians on stage created an extremely rare moment: the spotlight of mathematics had never before shone on multiple women at once, let alone three young Chinese women. This breakthrough by Chinese women mathematicians is far more than just an inspiring story of genius. Their emergence is like a mirror, reflecting the invisible structural problems beyond the spotlight.



Two

The Baton: Chasing a 40-Year Time Gap



In the arena of mathematical research, Chinese men have led Chinese women by nearly four decades. Since 1936, these six major mathematics prizes have awarded a total of 251 honors, with ethnic Chinese recipients winning 17 times across 11 individuals. Among them, Shing-Tung Yau (4 times), Shiing-Shen Chern (2 times), and Terence Tao (3 times) appear multiple times. Before 2026, none of these 17 honors went to a woman.


The stage for Chinese men opened grandly in the 1980s, while the curtain for Chinese women only began to rise in the 2020s. In 1982, Shing-Tung Yau won the Fields Medal, becoming the first ethnic Chinese recipient. Forty-four years later, Wang Hong and Tang Yunqing stood on the same level of podium as him, as the first Chinese women to do so.




In fact, beyond these six major awards, the journey of Chinese women mathematicians had quietly begun earlier. In 1986, Sheng-Tung Yau invited Shengrong Zhang to deliver a one-hour academic lecture at the International Congress of Mathematicians (ICM), making her the first Chinese woman to receive this honor. In 2007, Qiuju Liu won the ICCM Silver Prize, the only woman among seven awardees that year. In 2010, Sijue Wu won the ICCM Gold Prize, becoming the only Chinese woman mathematician to date to win both ICCM Gold and Silver Prizes.


The baton was then passed to a younger generation: in 2022, Tang Yunqing became the first Chinese woman to win the Srinivasa Ramanujan Prize. In 2025, Wang Hong won four major awards in succession, including the Salem Prize and the Ostrowski Prize. In 2026, Tang Yunqing won the Frank Nelson Cole Prize in Number Theory—the first time since the prize's establishment in 1931 that a Chinese woman had received it.


From Zhang Shengrong's ICM lecture in 1986 to the three women jointly winning the Breakthrough Prize in 2026, this baton has been passed for a full forty years. Chinese women mathematicians were nearly isolated cases before the 21st century, and only in the past decade have they begun to emerge rapidly. This is not a sudden explosion of one generation, but a qualitative leap built upon generations of accumulation.




Three

Ecosystem: The Ones Who Were "Filtered Out"





Academia has a term called "leaky pipeline", used to describe how women are more likely than men to leave science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) fields. The "leaky pipeline" reveals a harsh reality: women do not fail to reach the top; rather, they drop out at each career stage at a higher rate than men [1].


Research data shows that since 1999, the proportion of women earning bachelor's degrees in mathematics and statistics has been declining. By 2013, this proportion reached 42%, equaling the level of 1979, indicating a regression of over thirty years. At the doctoral level, although the total number of women earning PhDs has increased, the proportion in mathematical sciences has stagnated over the past decade, fluctuating around 29%. Although the overall proportion of women faculty at PhD-granting institutions has improved in the past decade, in 2013, women with tenure in mathematical sciences accounted for only 15% of faculty positions. Even more strikingly, among 13,067 editorial board seats across 435 mathematics journals, women held only 8.9%; in half of the journals, the proportion of women on editorial boards was less than 7.6%, and 51 journals had no female editorial members at all [2].


Up to 2026, across 90 years of the six major mathematics prizes and 251 awarded honors, women accounted for only 3.59%. From 42% to 3.59%, the academic ladder in mathematics filters out batches of women at every level. As Zhang Shengrong said: "It's not an issue of ability, but of environmental constraints." From bachelor's degree to award recipient, each "leak" is not a failure of ability, but a result of environmental filtering.


Due to gender bias, women receive far less support than men throughout their development. Narratives such as "women are not suited for science" and "women are not good at math" have long constrained women's development in high-tech fields. Gender bias expects women to be gentle, quiet, and fragile "altruists," objects to be observed and gazed upon, rather than subjects with analytical capabilities. Thus, when women strive to break through such biases and enter the male-dominated field of mathematics, what they first face is collective exclusion from the "gazers." Therefore, the few women who persist must endure age-old gender biases, advance with minimal resources, and rely solely on exceptional ability to take even one step forward.


Not to mention that mathematical research has long been dominated by the West, and the authority to define mathematics still lies in the hands of Western scholars. Chinese women scholars must not only confront gender bias but also break through invisible cultural barriers, enduring far more pain and pressure than others to stand on the podium of mathematical excellence.



Four

Coordinates: Defined by Themselves





"I've always felt quite lost, and I really did give up before." Wang Hong admitted in an interview. While in France, she stopped doing mathematics for half a year and tried studying architecture, only to find architecture was also difficult, so she returned to mathematics. She couldn't clearly explain what brought her back—perhaps she realized at the time that mathematics wasn't as hard to learn as architecture.


This is not a genius's modesty, but the real state of a generation of Chinese women mathematicians. Liu Qiuju, during her third year at Harvard, found she had accomplished nothing and once considered changing advisors, worrying whether she could even graduate. Wu Sijue was even more direct: "I always thought mathematics wasn't what I wanted to do, until recently realizing it's my true passion." Zhang Shengrong, seeking stability, gave up her dream of literature in high school. She once imagined returning to literature after mastering mathematics, but later found that excelling in mathematics left no energy to pursue other interests.


They all once deviated from their paths: Wang Hong switched majors across departments, Zhang Shengrong moved from harmonic analysis to geometric analysis, and Wu Sijue wandered alone searching for problems until she found answers in water waves. These experiences collectively sketch a truthful academic portrait: they did not follow a predetermined genius script, but instead repeatedly re-anchored their academic coordinates through detours and setbacks.


Wang Hong said: "When I encounter a problem, I try hard to learn it; if I can't understand it, I study it again; if still not, I switch directions." When facing complex mathematical challenges, the core strategy lies in continuous learning and flexible redirection. Moreover, when progress stalls in a single direction, finding collaborators becomes key to breaking through.


Zhang Shengrong emphasized: "Good mathematical research isn't about pushing blindly down one path." Excellent mathematical research requires avoiding intellectual dead ends; timely pauses and cross-disciplinary exchanges often spark new inspiration. This shift from solitary struggle to collaborative breakthrough reflects the methodological maturity and pragmatism of a new generation of women mathematicians.


When they stop clinging to identity labels imposed by society and instead focus on specific mathematical problems, true breakthroughs emerge. Wu Sijue said: "When you understand the problem, you have a starting point."


Whether it took Wang Hong ten years to solve the Kakeya conjecture or Tang Yunqing five years to verify a hypothesis, their achievements are built upon deep exploration of specific problems. From harmonic analysis to geometric analysis, from water wave research to interdisciplinary exploration, these seemingly off-track cross-field attempts ultimately converge into powerful momentum driving the frontier of mathematics.




From Zhang Shengrong's first appearance on the ICM stage in 1986 to Wang Hong, Tang Yunqing, and Zhang Mingjia jointly winning the Breakthrough Prize in 2026, this forty-year timeline clearly outlines the generational succession of Chinese women mathematicians.




Zhang Shengrong once recalled that in the 1960s, Chen-Ning Yang's remark—"If I were young, I would study mathematics"—directly inspired seven or eight female students in one class at National Taiwan University to pursue mathematics. This culture of "collective discussion" broke the "island effect" among female scholars, proving that mutual encouragement within a community is key for women to build academic confidence.


Research shows that cross-disciplinary thinking is a crucial driver for breaking through academic bottlenecks.


At her husband's suggestion to "draw some pictures," Zhang Shengrong successfully transitioned from harmonic analysis to the field of geometric analysis; Wu Sijue, relying on her keen intuition for nonlinear water wave equations, achieved world-class breakthroughs in the field of partial differential equations; Tang Yunqing cleverly applied algebraic geometry tools to successfully break a long-standing deadlock in number theory. This fusion of diverse backgrounds is making the boundaries of mathematical research richer and more multi-dimensional.


In July 2026, the International Congress of Mathematicians will announce the new recipients of the Fields Medal. Wang Hong is widely regarded as one of the top contenders—if she wins, she will become the second woman in the award's history to receive it, and the first Chinese woman.


From Zhang Shengrong stepping onto the ICM stage in 1986, to three Chinese women jointly receiving breakthrough awards in 2026, exactly forty years have passed. On this path, they were not the first, nor will they be the last. As a page of manuscript turns, the next pen is already in the hands of the next generation.
