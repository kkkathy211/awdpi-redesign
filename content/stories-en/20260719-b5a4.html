Has "feminism" become the most hypocritical and self-serving slogan for the younger generation of women?


Behind this grand concept, what kind of real-life situations are women actually facing? Do we truly understand the difficulties women endure?


We call ourselves "feminists," yet we love the label more than we love real, concrete people; we discuss systemic discrimination and structural injustice with like-minded friends, yet we are unwilling to admit that we are often women from middle-class families who hold privilege; we possess sharp emotional sensitivity, frequently moved by the suffering of women on the other side of the world, yet at the same time we remain cold and calculating, skillfully using rules and power in schools and workplaces, leveraging every platform to construct an "elite" image for ourselves.


We are overconfident, yet often filled with self-doubt; we want to say something, but in the end only utter vague theories; we want to do something, yet only dare to circle within safe boundaries; we try to change something, yet have never truly changed the lives of survivors of gender-based violence.


Our stories as volunteers at AWDPI are sometimes neither grand nor about dedication. We are more often a group of people holding hope—still trying to reflect on ourselves, grow stronger, and find allies, even when we cannot find space for our doubts, anger, loneliness, and vulnerability. We grasp each other's hands and shout loudly toward a blurry horizon, longing to hear some echo.


This interview records our most authentic voices and reflections. This time, we want to face ourselves honestly.


Selling "middle-class identity":

Has modern feminism abandoned women from lower classes?

Legal Support Division, Xia Yan (Irene)

Volunteer service duration: 4 years and 2 months


What we see today in public spaces as women's resistance is often merely the resistance of a small group of relatively resource-privileged women.


When I first started doing public welfare work, I thought the differences in women's circumstances were mainly ideological. But as I encountered more and more cases, I observed a rather harsh reality: Those who can cross vast information gaps to find us, and effectively utilize the legal support we provide, largely already possess certain economic means and educational backgrounds. In the business world, many highly successful female lawyers I've met, although women themselves, often highly identify with and skillfully apply the rules of patriarchal society.


Putting these two points together, I realized a rather disheartening truth: What we see today in public spaces as women's resistance is often merely the resistance of a small group of relatively resource-privileged women.


I feel that much of today's discussion about feminism, including my own, unconsciously falls into "class blindness." We are very active in condemning gender inequality on macro-level issues, yet often turn a blind eye to real hardships. After the market co-opts feminist narratives for marketing purposes, the original questioning of structural injustice is stripped away, eventually simplified into a posture called "independence."


When social media is full of talk about mental independence and bravely leaving abusers, there is actually an unconscious meritocratic arrogance lurking beneath. We easily attribute the inability of working-class women to escape violence or exploitation to personal flaws such as lack of independent thinking or insufficient clarity, while remaining deaf to their extreme lack of survival resources and structural despair.


As a legal professional, I used to take for granted that the standard solution to violence was to report it to the police and rely on state power to severely punish the perpetrator.


But for women from lower classes and marginalized groups, hastily involving the judicial system does not necessarily mean protection; instead, it may mean instantly losing the only source of income that sustains their survival, or even placing themselves at greater risk of further marginalization by the system. In the absence of substantial economic and community support, blindly believing in incarceration and punitive justice is often just a form of moral gratification for us as bystanders; in reality, it may only push lower-class women into even deeper desperation.


These real differences make me cautious about mainstream feminist discourse today. I may not yet have the ability to completely break through this blindness, But I think recognizing our own limitations and realizing that our feminism often stays within safe zones is the first step toward staying awake.


Only by first acknowledging that women truly on the margins—those without even a channel to speak—are being forgotten by this discourse, can we possibly one day begin to truly reach them.



Human Resources Department, Shi Jiaxin

Volunteer service duration: 2 years


"What I hope for is a world where even a 'low-value' person can be equally respected."


Ten years ago, the term "feminism" was almost absent from public discourse; now it repeatedly appears in consumer contexts, marketing copy, and social platforms—this, to some extent, is proof of its influence. As for the parts that are merely using the label for ulterior motives, viewed optimistically, at least it shows the topic has traffic and attention.


Of course, dilution and deviation are real. But as more people pay attention, others will step forward to explain and expose these distortions. Dilution is an inevitable side effect of amplifying voices, but it is not the final outcome.


The transformation of International Women's Day into "Goddess Day" or "Queen's Day" is the most typical example—turning a day commemorating women's liberation into an excuse to push women to keep consuming. And various "feminist co-branded" products or "girl power limited editions" are essentially selling an identity of "how progressive I am." But I won't dismiss its value because of this, because I also see another direction of change: more and more people are resisting the term "Goddess Day," actively correcting others and educating the public about the true meaning of March 8th.


I myself have also been influenced and shaped by this environment of "feminism." Not everyone will read Simone de Beauvoir or Chizuko Ueno, but seeing a post saying "don't call yourself a 'leftover woman'," or reading a March 8th message unrelated to consumerism—these small details allow feminism to gradually seep into our thinking.


I used to be obsessed with distinguishing "true feminism" from "insufficient feminism," but now I lean toward "full affirmation."


Everyone is fighting for voice and space in their own way: some write academic papers, some say in live streams, "Sisters, don't settle for less," some simply challenge the saying "girls don't need to study so much" in family chat groups. These are all doing the same thing, just on different battlefields—why should we judge who is more "correct"?


As for the future, I hope for a world where even a "low-value" person can be equally respected.


This society is accustomed to grading everyone: low position, low income, low education, not conforming to mainstream aesthetics, poor social skills, not "refined"—once these labels are attached, no one takes your words seriously. And women are often more easily pushed to the bottom in this value hierarchy.


So for me, the ultimate destination of feminism is not just a world where "women become strong," but a world where "weakness" is no longer punished.


The "feminist" ideal torn apart by reality:

empty slogans, extreme incidents, traffic above all

Legal Support Division, Xia Yan (Irene)

Volunteer service duration: 4 years and 2 months


"Limited public attention is either absorbed by safe consumerist narratives, or repeatedly pulled between trauma and helplessness surrounding extreme incidents."


Discussions about women's issues today seem to be forced into two completely opposite extremes.


The first extreme, I usually see as the highly "softened" and commercialized side. Under today's logic of traffic, the original feminist questioning of structural injustice may be quietly stripped away, simplified into a posture called "independence," no longer demanding that you confront real institutional hardships, only asking you to please yourself and spend for yourself.


The other extreme is discussions centered around extreme malicious incidents. For example, the recent case of the German international student drugged and sexually assaulted, or the previous large-scale hidden camera scandal involving hundreds of thousands.


Whenever a glimpse of such extreme systemic violence is revealed, it naturally sparks immense anger among women. But at the same time, the process of seeking truth and demanding structural change, when blocked, causes collective anger to eventually mix with an extremely deep sense of helplessness. It seems we can only briefly resonate when facing extreme harm, only to be drained again by inevitable silence.


Limited public attention is either absorbed by safe consumerist narratives, or repeatedly pulled between trauma and helplessness surrounding extreme incidents.


I hope feminism can step back slightly from these two extremes, and land on a more stable footing—taking root in corners that may lack explosive virality, but genuinely concern women's fates.


The kind of feminism I hope for is one that no longer needs to constantly justify itself. If one day, calling out gender inequality could naturally become a basic common sense, rather than a stance that requires constant defense, I believe that in itself would be tremendous progress.


Also, I hope it can reconnect individual daily experiences with large-scale structural issues. In these discussions, the middle layer—the most critical and hardest infrastructure work—is often overlooked.


For example, how exactly should specific systems be changed, how laws should be revised, how evidentiary rules should be adjusted to truly accommodate the particularities of sexual crimes, or—as we at AWDPI are doing—how to improve the coordination mechanisms for cross-border assistance. I hope ideal feminism can take deeper root in this middle layer, because usually, it is only at this level that the fate of a specific woman can truly be changed.



Human Resources Department, Nong Chunhua

Volunteer service duration: 2 years


"We don't necessarily need to make grand gestures. Helping others within our own capacity is already good enough."


Judging from social media, feminism has a louder voice than before. But I don't feel the situation has improved; feminism is facing more and more challenges.


Let me give an example I've observed.


I once followed a women-only store that promoted itself with feminist-friendly slogans to attract customers. But when I inquired about the details of their part-time job recruitment, I found the night shift hourly wage was much lower than the average daytime wage in the market, and employees were required to take on multiple roles.


The shop was full of feminist elements—its decor, books, and films—all creating a great atmosphere. But as soon as I thought about their exploitation of employees, I felt deeply conflicted. It felt like "feminism" had become a tool for consumerism.


When the slogan "girls help girls" first became popular, I actually tended to see it as a rather floating slogan. Later, as its usage context expanded, more and more girls began proving through concrete actions that this phrase wasn't just empty rhetoric. We don't necessarily need to make grand gestures. Helping others within our own capacity is already good enough.


I always recall a scene from the movie *Good Things*, where Tie Mei falls asleep on the subway after work, accidentally leaning her head on a male passenger's shoulder. A female passenger sitting nearby gently shifts her head toward herself.


I joined AWDPI in 2023, during what I suppose was my exploratory phase regarding feminism. Seeing a tweet made me realize I could actually help other women, and I didn't want to miss the opportunity to move from theory to practice. But back then, my understanding was probably still rather abstract—I thought feminism was about loving women, but more in the sense of loving the abstract idea of women.


Now, the feminism I envision is one that makes the world gentler. Neither big nor small matters in life would become so sharp; people could reach consensus on issues and, within reasonable limits, minimize harm to others, while everyone could have as many opportunities for development as possible.


Feminism is about helping people connect better, not driving them apart.


Class differences, gender opposition, racial conflicts:

How do we begin the dialogue?

Human Resources Department, Zhang Sihan

Volunteer service duration: 2 years


"The way I envision advancing women's rights is no longer just about women fighting alone, but also about fostering understanding and dialogue between genders—helping men see women's real circumstances and become equal co-creators, rather than excluded targets."


My understanding of feminism is very simple: It's not an "ism," but merely a wish—because I've seen some of the injustices women around me have faced, I hope women can live better lives and be treated more fairly. It is this simple wish that led me into the field of women's public welfare.


Through my participation in public welfare and daily observations, my core dilemma has never been whether to promote gender equality, but rather, what approach truly works.


After joining AWDPI, I gained deeper insights. I once interviewed an outstanding male volunteer from 2023. He mentioned that many current feminist theories are framed from a female perspective, with little research focusing on how men view these issues—yet men are precisely the group most in need of outreach and mobilization. This idea greatly inspired me and directly shaped my thesis topic—studying the differences in how men and women perceive gender discrimination in the workplace.


Through my research, I realized that whether it's hidden discrimination and occupational gender segregation during hiring, the "glass ceiling" and pay gaps in promotions, or the widespread existence of sexual harassment at work, all indicate that women still face structural challenges in the workplace.


After conducting extensive surveys and interviews, I realized that many men are not intentionally ignoring women's injustices. They do have some awareness of women's struggles—perhaps from their families, such as their wives losing promotion opportunities for no clear reason, or worrying their daughters might face sexual harassment at work; or from their jobs, such as witnessing firsthand the hidden discrimination against women in hiring as recruiters. Personal experiences have a huge impact.


As that volunteer said, only when more men recognize the seriousness of the discrimination women face will they spontaneously protect and respect women, and ultimately help advance women's status.


I work in a male-dominated industry that requires frequent overnight shifts and rotating schedules. Department leaders often prioritize men, citing "men can stay up later and have stronger physical endurance," meaning women usually have to be much more outstanding than men to secure the same position.


This led me to further question: Is women's difficulty in sustaining certain roles due to objective physiological limitations, or is it the result of social-cultural beliefs like "women don't need to work so hard" leading to mental fatigue and eventual withdrawal? Similarly, is women's greater tendency to focus on family after childbirth due to maternal instincts, or is it socially constructed?


Gender disparities are never as simple as external discrimination; they are woven from multiple factors, requiring us to think carefully and speak cautiously.


The way I envision advancing women's rights is no longer just about women's unilateral efforts, but about simultaneously promoting mutual understanding and dialogue between genders, helping men see women's real circumstances and become equal co-creators, rather than excluded targets.



Legal Affairs Department, Chen Dan (Dan)

Volunteer service duration: 7 months


"The kind of social life I envision is one that does not emphasize, highlight, or imply gender differences or various stereotypes—where every individual can explore all kinds of possibilities, while also paying attention to protecting the vulnerable."


In the recent film *Grandma's Love Letters*, the mutual support between two female characters made me truly experience a concrete example of "girls help girls." I'm also from the Chaoshan region, and I think back to the grandmothers and mothers of previous generations. Sometimes I feel this kind of help and support comes from women's innate empathy and compassion. Such actions aren't always intentional or goal-oriented; they simply arise from sympathy toward another human being of the same gender and similar circumstances—without ulterior motives, and very pure.


In practice, I've always wanted to do things I find meaningful, driven by a strong internal motivation to empathize with others or offer help. Before joining AWDPI, I learned about the organization through some public channels. At the time, I was studying in Europe. I strongly identified with the organization's founding mission—I understood that many women indeed face difficult situations in foreign, unfamiliar environments, and I hoped to offer some help to women in need.


While in Europe, I often felt the inclusiveness brought by cultural diversity. There wasn't just gender difference, but also differences in race, ethnicity, and skin color. But because everyone had to live on the same land, these differences had to be digested and integrated. In this state of seeking common ground while respecting differences, I felt human differences weren't so great after all, and sometimes I could deeply resonate with people of other races.


In an ideal environment, there would be no deliberate emphasis on differences. I envision a social life that does not emphasize, highlight, or imply gender differences or various stereotypes—where every individual can explore all kinds of possibilities, while also paying attention to protecting the vulnerable.


I think it's important to allow and respect vulnerability and dignity—allowing every individual to have moments of weakness might prevent structural discrimination caused by admiration for strength.



Community Support Division, Huang Yuxuan

Volunteer service duration: 4 years


"I believe women already have strength. Let's find this strength together."


When I first joined AWDPI, I thought the key word of feminism was "help." At the time, I had just started university and was absorbing a lot of information from online media. I gradually saw many women speaking up about the injustices and gender biases they had experienced. I wanted to help in whatever way I could, and I also wanted to better understand women's current situation in society, so I chose to join.


In volunteering, we also encounter some challenges, and the most common confusion might be: "Does what we're doing actually make a difference?"—doubting the value of our work. Since my role in the community mainly involves community management and outreach, occasionally some teammates feel they can't see their own contributions and would prefer to do work that feels more directly meaningful. I don't dismiss these feelings, but it's precisely because we are present, because we are spreading messages, because we are creating a safe space, that women in the community can access diverse information and resources, and also gain a bit of courage.


In public discussions, we must also remember to keep our focus on real, specific individuals.


When we discuss feminism on social media, the conversation often assumes a premise: that you already have basic safety, time, and economic autonomy. In reality, many women are still fighting to secure these very "premises." Now, I try to maintain two perspectives simultaneously: I don't deny the value of theoretical discussions, but I constantly remind myself not to let labels obscure real-life circumstances.


After my experience with AWDPI, if I were to define feminism now, I would say it is: I believe women already possess strength—let's find that strength together.
