Introduction




In August, gender equality moved from advocacy to hard legal constraints. Australia's new law against gender-based violence in higher education, the exposure of misogynistic culture and accountability failures within Scotland's police force, and women in Papua New Guinea taking positive action to rebuild safety, together illustrate a layered progression—from institutions to accountability to community—demonstrating collective progress. Yet violence and discrimination against Asians continue to raise alarms, with a domestic violence murder case in Southern California exposing invisible harms beneath seemingly respectable marriages, a wave of attacks shaking immigrant communities in Ireland, and a Broadway casting controversy reflecting renewed struggles over Asian bodies and dignity in public spaces—reminding us that safety and anti-bias education must go hand in hand. At the same time, Asian voices are gaining weight across media, politics, and education, as Lucy Liu receives AAJA's "Visibility Award," South Asian coalitions organize political mobilization in New Jersey, and 23 U.S. states have now incorporated Asian American history into K–12 curricula. From institutions to culture, from campuses to communities, only by writing safety and respect into the rules can the dignity and voice of Asians and all people be truly seen and protected in public spaces.

 Australia Enforces Strict Measures in Higher Education

to Prevent Gender-Based Violence with New Regulations


On August 25, 2025, the Australian Federal Parliament formally passed legislation establishing the national *Higher Education Code to Prevent and Respond to Gender-Based Violence*. The Code requires all higher education institutions and their leaders not only to respond appropriately to acts of violence but also to effectively prevent gender-based violence.


The significance of this Code lies not only in legal regulation but also in driving cultural change within universities. Advocacy group Our Watch praised the legislation as a historic turning point after years of effort, emphasizing that "prevention is not optional, but a core responsibility of every institution." The measures aim to address systemic and structural drivers such as gender inequality, stereotypical biases, and sociocultural pressures. This means campus safety is expanding from mere response to actively shaping an environment of respect, safety, and equality.

Scotland's Police Force Exposed for Sexism


A deep investigative report by an independent interviewing organization reveals systemic sexism and misogynistic culture within Police Scotland. The report highlights a prevalent "frat culture" within the force, where female officers are frequently subjected to inappropriate jokes, comments, and dismissiveness from male superiors. More seriously, women who speak up often fear retaliation or blocked promotions, creating a vicious cycle of silence and compromise.


The investigation also found that some senior officers turn a blind eye to sexist behavior or even condone it. Instances include male subordinates refusing to follow orders from female superiors and some high-ranking male officers forming "predatory relationships" with young female officers. This culture leads female officers to constantly question whether their appearance might "invite" gendered remarks, further reinforcing insecurity.


Under external pressure, Police Scotland has acknowledged the severity of the problem and pledged to pursue "meaningful cultural reform." However, the suppression and delayed release of the report have sparked public concern over transparency and police governance. Legal experts and advocacy groups stress that without public accountability, public trust in law enforcement will further erode.

Women in Papua New Guinea

Breaking the Cycle of Violence


In Papua New Guinea, about two-thirds of women experience violence in their lifetime. Domestic violence, sexual assault, and killings based on "witchcraft" accusations are widespread. Although laws exist, enforcement is weak, and society often treats such violence as a "private matter." Several recent cases of women being brutally murdered have sparked national attention and protests.


Facing deep-rooted cultural and institutional challenges, several women leaders and survivors are launching grassroots actions. Former athlete Tahina Booth uses sports programs through the "Grass Skirt Project" to promote gender equality and conflict resolution, aiming to impact one million youth by 2050. Betty Awo, volunteering in Morobe Province, travels from village to village writing testimonies for victims, providing temporary shelter, and holding anti-violence workshops, empowering children and communities to speak out. Maureen Mokai organizes the "Peace Program" in Hela Province to help survivors of violence and youth rebuild their lives and reduce conflict.


Gender-based violence is not merely a personal tragedy but a systemic failure. Prime Minister James Marape has called it a "pandemic threatening the nation," urging that respect for women become the foundation of social development over the next 50 years. Despite limited resources, the efforts of women advocates show society is gradually changing: more community leaders and families are beginning to openly discuss violence and challenge long-accepted norms.

Korean-American Man in Southern California

Kills and Burns Wife of Chinese-American Doctor with a Frying Pan


On January 6, 2025, in San Marino, California, 63-year-old Korean-American man Yoon "Kevin" Lai brutally murdered Irene Gaw-Lai, a Chinese-American doctor and his wife of 22 years. Police found that he beat her to death with a blunt object and then set her body on fire. A blood-stained frying pan and a metal baseball bat were left at the scene as murder weapons.


Dr. Gaw was a well-respected internal medicine physician in the community. The family appeared affluent, living in a $2.6 million mansion. They had twin 16-year-old children. However, their marriage had long been fraught with conflict. Prior to the incident, Dr. Gaw had discovered that Lai had hidden property assets and had filed in court to void their prenuptial agreement and initiate divorce proceedings. She had confided in family members, saying, "I don't feel safe being alone with him."


After the murder, Lai was initially released on a $2.25 million bail and denied all charges, but he has since been charged with first-degree murder and arson. The case has shocked the Chinese-American community in Southern California, prompting deep reflection on domestic violence. Reports emphasize that abusers often hide behind the façade of a "respectable marriage," highlighting the hidden and destructive nature of domestic violence.

Irish Immigrant Communities Face Wave of Violent Attacks


Recently, Ireland has seen a series of violent incidents targeting Indian and other immigrant communities, sparking social panic. In Tallaght, Dublin, an Indian man was surrounded and beaten by teenagers, partially stripped, and filmed; shortly after, another Indian man in Clondalkin was attacked by multiple people, resulting in fractures and multiple injuries; a few days later, in Ballymun, two passengers assaulted an Indian-origin taxi driver, hitting his face with bottles and shouting "Go back to your country"; and in County Waterford, a group of young men assaulted a 6-year-old Indian-origin girl, punching her and hitting her genital area with a bicycle, while also yelling "Go back to India."


These violent incidents have shocked immigrant communities. The Indian Embassy has advised citizens to enhance personal safety and avoid remote areas. The Indian community held a silent protest outside the Department of Justice. Indian media have prominently covered the events, and many immigrants in Ireland say they once believed the country was safe and welcoming, but now feel deeply insecure.


Reports indicate that since the 1990s, Ireland's immigrant population has grown significantly, now making up one-fifth of the national population. Recent housing crises and rising social tensions have fueled xenophobic sentiments. Human rights advocates are calling for stronger police action, improved hate crime legislation, and the promotion of anti-racism education. Meanwhile, although far-right groups remain a minority, their rallies and attack videos circulating on social media have intensified an atmosphere of fear.

Broadway's "Maybe Happy Ending" Sparks Controversy Over Casting White Actors in Asian Roles


Broadway's romantic comedy musical *Maybe Happy Ending* has sparked major controversy over casting decisions. After the original lead actor, Darren Criss of Asian descent, temporarily stepped down, the production announced that white actor Andrew Barth Feldman would take over the male lead role. This decision was criticized as "a slap in the face to the artistic community," triggering strong backlash from artists.


The Asian American Performers Action Coalition (AAPAC), the Consortium of Asian American Theaters & Artists (CAATA), and several prominent actors—including BD Wong, Conrad Ricamora, and Ruthie Ann Miles—publicly condemned the move, calling it an "insult" to the Asian American community. Over 2,400 people signed an open letter urging the producers to reconsider, including well-known figures such as Awkwafina, Bowen Yang, and Donna Murphy.


Critics pointed out that the play is set in a futuristic Seoul, and having Asian actors in the lead roles carried strong symbolic meaning. At the same time, some industry insiders argue this reveals the broader lack of Asian representation on Broadway: although Asians make up 14.5% of New York City's population, they account for only 3.7% of Broadway actors and just 2.8% of musical leads.

Lucy Liu Receives the "Visibility Award" at the Asian American Journalists Association (AAJA)

Annual Conference


On August 2, 2025, actress and UNICEF Ambassador Lucy Liu received the "Visibility Award" at the annual conference of the Asian American Journalists Association (AAJA) in Seattle, USA. In her speech, she emphasized that since 1981, AAJA has not only amplified Asian voices but also protected them within news and social narratives. She stated: "We are not footnotes in someone else's story, but the authors of our own."


In her remarks, Liu said AAJA's efforts have made Asian Americans impossible to ignore in society, reminding Asian American and Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander (AANHPI) creators that they do not need "permission" to exist in public narratives. This statement reinforced the importance of Asian representation in media and cultural industries.


The conference also honored other distinguished individuals, including Pulitzer Prize-finalist Filipino journalist Nicole Dungca, as well as several pioneering Asian American journalists and writers.

South Asian Coalition's political mobilization in New Jersey


The South Asian Coalition held a "Meet & Greet" event on August 25 in Pennington, New Jersey, inviting gubernatorial candidate Jack Ciattarelli. The event drew over 300 attendees, including independent voters, community leaders, and members of South Asian diaspora organizations, demonstrating the growing political influence of the South Asian community locally.


Coalition Chair Sridhar Chillara emphasized in his speech that South Asians should actively vote and speak up, avoiding marginalization due to low voter turnout. Executive Director Pavan Kuchana also noted that only collective action can truly shape New Jersey's future. The event also announced the formal inclusion of the Sri Lankan community into the coalition, further expanding its representation.


Ciattarelli presented several policy visions at the event, focusing on improving education and healthcare, and stated he would prioritize initiatives beneficial to the South Asian community. This event not only demonstrated the unity and political mobilization capacity of the South Asian community but also marked their growing presence as a force to be reckoned with in New Jersey's political arena.

Asian American history education has been included in

the K–12 public school curriculum systems in 23 U.S. states


As of August 2025, 23 U.S. states have incorporated Asian American history into their K–12 public school curriculum systems through legislation or academic standards. Among them, 13 states have explicit legal requirements, while another 10 states mandate the teaching of Asian American history through academic standards. This progress reflects unprecedented institutional support for Asian American history education across the nation.


Paul Cheung, Chair of the Committee of 100, emphasized that Asian Americans have been an essential part of American history for over 200 years, yet have often been overlooked by the education system due to the "perpetual foreigner" stereotype. He pointed out that public education is key to cultivating informed citizens, and omitting Asian American history prevents students from fully understanding America's diverse experiences.


Meanwhile, recent public surveys show that approximately 70% of Americans believe Asian American history should be included in school curricula. This social consensus has driven policy changes and indicates that the public increasingly recognizes the rightful place of Asian Americans in the American narrative.
