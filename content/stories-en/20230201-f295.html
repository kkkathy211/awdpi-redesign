 Introduction:


As people gradually emerge from the initial storm of the pandemic, the long-term issues brought by COVID-19 are increasingly coming to light. Women and girls make up nearly half of the world's population, and analyzing, reflecting on, and summarizing problems from a gender perspective plays a crucial role in fully harnessing humanity's potential, achieving sustainable development, and building a fair and just society. Currently, globally, violence against women and girls continues to rise. Home isolation policies during the pandemic, coupled with large-scale economic downturns and rising unemployment, have made violence occurring within households more privatized and de-politicized. These heavy statistics expose the reality of "inequality" in our societies, reveal the inadequacies in national support systems, and highlight the urgency of addressing domestic violence and eliminating intersecting social inequalities caused by gender, race/citizenship, and class. Therefore, this article takes the COVID-19 pandemic as a starting point to analyze the connection between the pandemic and domestic violence and explore optimal solutions for protecting women during public health emergencies.


Image source: UN Women official WeChat account


Recently, the United Nations released the "Gender Snapshot 2022" report, revealing how gender gaps are worsening in the face of successive global crises, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, climate change, violent conflicts, humanitarian crises, and setbacks in women's sexual and reproductive health and rights. At the current pace of progress, it will still take nearly 300 years to fully achieve gender equality.


Violence against women is one of the key indicators for measuring progress toward gender equality goals. However, the report points out, globally, violence against women and girls continues to increase. In the past year, more than 1 in 10 women aged 15–49 experienced physical or sexual violence from an intimate partner (12.5%), about 25% of women reported more frequent family conflicts during the pandemic, and calls to domestic violence helplines by women surged sharply. These shocking figures undoubtedly sound an alarm, making it necessary to discuss and analyze domestic violence within the broader context of the pandemic. We must also reflect on and analyze the problems exposed during the pandemic. In the face of public health emergencies like the pandemic, what measures and responses should be adopted to effectively protect women from domestic violence?



I

Domestic violence spreads rapidly in the context of the pandemic


Since March 17, 2020, domestic violence cases across France have increased by 30%, with a 36% rise in Paris alone. In Spain, emergency calls due to domestic violence increased by 20%, while in Brazil the increase reached 40–50%. In addition, media reports on domestic abuse and femicide cases have also risen. In Australia, online searches for "domestic violence help" increased by nearly 75%, reaching the highest level in nearly five years. Canada, Germany, and the United States also reported a rapid increase in demand for emergency shelters during the pandemic. These data all reflect the phenomenon that "the incidence of domestic violence has increased during the pandemic." The UN website points out that due to the COVID-19 pandemic, the limited progress made over decades in gender equality and women's rights is being eroded.



II

Multiple factors interact

to make the pandemic a trigger for domestic violence


01 Stress and risk go hand in hand


During the pandemic, on one hand, people spent prolonged periods at home, making the household the primary emotional outlet and target for family members; on the other hand, pandemic control required stricter and more difficult management of residents, leading to a shift in social control toward the household unit (although social management is mainly implemented through community-based systems, within each community, the units being managed are still individual households). The household has become a space for emotional release, so when family members feel pressure from various sources, they are more likely to vent their stress and emotions within the family, thereby triggering domestic violence. Women and children are in a vulnerable position within the household, which refers not only to physical weakness in the face of violence but also to a lack of living capital and resources, as well as lower resilience when facing various risks. This vulnerability is largely due to their marginalized social roles, which make their self-worth more easily confined within the family.


02 Economic hardship leads to more women losing their jobs


Under the pandemic, the employment situation has become severe, unemployment has surged, and social and household status disparities have intensified: Jacob, director of a UK charity, stated that unemployment not only makes women more susceptible to abuse but also increases their risk of long-term domestic violence after the pandemic. According to data from the UK domestic violence charity Shelter, since the lockdown policy was implemented, calls for domestic violence assistance in the UK increased by 25%, and website visits increased by 150%. Three weeks into the lockdown, this number was 49% higher than normal levels. In the first three weeks of the lockdown, at least 16 femicide cases involving women and children occurred. Liu Yuanfen, Executive Director of New York Grace Women and Children Care Center, an Asian domestic violence support group, stated that in 2020, due to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, domestic violence cases among Chinese families clearly rose. Since the "stay-at-home" order was issued in New York City in March, the number of domestic violence cases seeking help from Grace increased from dozens per month to over 300, several times higher than the same period last year.


In the context of global economic depression and tightened access to basic resources, unemployed women, as economically and physically weaker parties, further lose their status and voice within the household, making them even more powerless to resist after suffering violence and abuse.

03 Under home isolation policies, victims have no way to seek help


Since the implementation of home isolation orders, the number of calls to France's domestic violence helpline 3919 has decreased. Marlene Schiappa, Secretary of State for Gender Equality in France, admitted, "It's hard to make a phone call when you're locked in a room with your abuser." Charities in some countries also reported that calls to domestic violence hotlines have declined because women have fewer opportunities to make calls for help. Anna Bella Esteves, a domestic violence survivor who runs a support service in southern Spain, said, "It's difficult to talk with women because their abusers are hovering around them 24 hours a day." 


04 Limited social resources leave support organizations overwhelmed


The most direct way for victims to escape danger is to go to a shelter. However, during the pandemic, shelters in various countries are also struggling. The director of Seattle's largest shelter in the United States said that even before the city's first confirmed COVID-19 case, the shelter was already nearly full, and now they still have to accept about 25 new families each day. The shelter usually relies on 200 to 300 volunteers to operate. Due to the pandemic, the shelter needs more staff to regularly disinfect facilities and screen people coming and going, but now almost no volunteers are showing up. Val Kalei Kanuha, Vice Dean for Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion at the University of Washington School of Social Work, stated, "For those who want to harm their partners, this is a perfect time," because even before the spread of COVID-19, perpetrators of domestic violence often tried to isolate victims to exert physical and psychological control. Now, home isolation provides perpetrators with ideal time and space to abuse, leaving potential victims with nowhere to escape. Kanuha pointed out, "During the pandemic, even if victims want to leave their abusers, it is very difficult because they have nowhere to go."


III

Rapid-response new forms of assistance


01 Governments increase investment to provide solid support


The French government has arranged for domestic violence victims to stay in hotels and covers the related costs, and will open short-term counseling centers. The government also announced an additional €1 million in subsidies for anti-domestic abuse organizations to help them meet growing service demands. In Australia, although courts are currently not operating normally, urgent and serious cases are still being heard via video. If domestic violence causes severe harm, the court will definitely issue orders to control the perpetrator. Meanwhile, the government has allocated 150 million Australian dollars for domestic violence assistance during the pandemic. New South Wales has invested 34 million Australian dollars to provide temporary accommodation for domestic violence victims, helping them temporarily leave abusive environments.


02 Adapting flexibly to establish emergency assistance systems


During the pandemic, women experiencing domestic violence face a serious challenge: under national lockdown measures, their mobility is restricted, making it difficult for support systems to effectively reach them. However, public spaces such as supermarkets and pharmacies, which remain open to meet people's basic needs, have become important bridges connecting victims with aid organizations or government agencies.

Image source: Pexels

In some European countries, such as France, the UK, Italy, the Netherlands, and Spain, relevant policies have been introduced allowing women who are unable to openly discuss cases of domestic violence to go to a pharmacy and say a designated code word—"Mask 19" or "ANI (Action Needed)"—to seek help discreetly. When staff hear the code word, they immediately recognize that the woman is experiencing violence and promptly contact the police or social services on her behalf.


Domestic violence is not merely a private family conflict; it is a serious social issue. The participation of the entire society and the provision of a systematic support network are key factors in helping women who have experienced domestic violence overcome their difficulties. Although this emergency alert system emerged as a temporary measure during the pandemic, we hope that in the future, more social institutions such as chain restaurants and supermarkets will leverage their advantages in time, space, and personnel to build partnerships with domestic violence support organizations, becoming potential support points for survivors and offering them more pathways to access resources.


03 Online assistance: expanding pathways for victims to seek help


Before the pandemic, calling a domestic violence hotline or visiting a shelter was usually the most convenient way for victims to seek help. However, under the complex circumstances of lockdowns and isolation policies during the pandemic, support organizations gradually realized that the window of opportunity for abused women to seek help by phone had become extremely narrow.


 "Many women whisper as soon as the call connects, afraid of being overheard by their partner in the next room."


In response to this challenge, some service providers quickly adapted to the changing situation by establishing or expanding more discreet online assistance services, allowing victims to actively reach out for help through online chat, text messages, and other forms. To address the difficulty of making phone calls, the Brazilian government launched a reporting app called "Direitos Humanos BR," available on both Android and iOS systems. After a simple registration process, users can report violence against women, children, the elderly, or other vulnerable groups, and attach photos or videos to their complaints. Furthermore, in the context of the pandemic, new forms of assistance have gradually emerged through new media, such as the "Domestic Violence Help" mini-program, which includes functions like online consultation, emergency fund applications, and self-assessment of intimate relationship risks, as well as comprehensive online women's protection projects offering psychological, legal, and medical support. These tools use more accessible methods and broader functionalities to help women experiencing domestic violence receive assistance more quickly.


Four

Reflecting on the problem, seeking answers through reflection


01 Focus on marginalized groups and provide targeted support


"Most of the people I serve are immigrants, queer individuals, or transgender people. The pandemic has drastically reduced face-to-face interactions for most of society, and for these specific groups, the already limited social support they receive has become even scarcer, making their path to seeking help increasingly difficult." This quote from the director of a New York domestic violence support organization draws our attention to the challenges marginalized groups face in seeking help during the pandemic.


A study from Norway also shows that information about domestic violence support services on public websites is almost entirely published in Norwegian, and the national domestic violence hotline offers information only in Norwegian and, rarely, in English, with no support in other languages. This situation prevents many immigrant women from accessing effective information; some do not even know that shelters and other social support services remain available to women experiencing domestic violence even during lockdowns.


The pandemic crisis has exposed a fundamental weakness in the support system for domestic violence survivors: inequality. Without thoughtful consideration of the changing social environment, homogenized perspectives and measures risk marginalizing vulnerable and minority groups, trapping them in cycles of domestic violence without a way out.


Therefore, we urge government agencies and support organizations, when formulating protection policies and providing support measures, to focus on groups such as people with disabilities, ethnic minorities, and immigrants, consider the specific forms of violence these populations face, analyze the barriers they encounter in ending violence, understand their real needs, and provide targeted assistance.



02 Multi-party collaboration: expanding the availability and diversity of support services


The biggest obstacle domestic violence survivors face when seeking help is often not knowing who to turn to or what specific support services are available. This difficulty in accessing resources or information has intensified due to the pandemic. During the pandemic, normal life rhythms were disrupted, and policy restrictions prevented face-to-face communication, leaving many survivors uncertain whether support organizations were still operating or whether the services available remained the same as before.


To address this issue, many support organizations have adopted innovative strategies and actively pursued multi-party collaboration and outreach. A domestic violence support organization director from Florida explained: "We spent a lot of time connecting with local media and social media platforms, and even conducted live streams online, just to let more people know that our organization remains open during the pandemic, that staff are still answering help calls, and that we can still provide shelter." 


The problems revealed by the pandemic should remind us that it is essential and urgent to make available resources clear to domestic violence survivors. Therefore, providers of domestic violence support services should consider collaborating with other social organizations and actively promote their services on social media and online platforms to increase survivors' awareness of available resources, ensuring that no matter when they experience violence—even during emergencies like a pandemic—they can immediately find a way to get help.


03 Summarizing experience: improving support systems for public emergencies


The United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) and UN Women (UN WOMEN) compiled gender-responsive measures taken by governments worldwide in response to COVID-19, based on government documents, media reports, and existing policy repositories. These measures were published in a database on the UNDP's COVID-19 Socioeconomic Recovery Data Insights platform. UNDP and UN Women define "gender-responsive measures" as "measures directly addressing the risks and challenges faced by women and girls during the COVID-19 crisis, particularly violence against women and girls, unpaid care work, and economic insecurity." By analyzing this database, they further tallied and organized special measures taken globally during the pandemic to address domestic violence, identifying a total of 704 distinct government actions. The database fully presents the experiences accumulated during this pandemic regarding domestic violence, allowing us to draw lessons and quickly establish a comprehensive emergency response plan and support system to address domestic violence in the context of public emergencies and help victims overcome their difficulties. Five

Conclusion

The COVID-19 pandemic has exposed deep-rooted structural gender inequalities. In this public health crisis, women and children are the most vulnerable and most easily overlooked groups. The surge of violence incidents after the pandemic has sounded an alarm for all: without timely action, decades of progress made for women and girls could be undone by the pandemic. If we fail to reflect on and learn from the problems exposed by this crisis, we will only move further away from the goal of gender equality. Therefore, we call for increased global attention to the issue of violence against women during public health emergencies, and urge governments, civil society, and the media to work together to help affected women emerge from the shadows.
