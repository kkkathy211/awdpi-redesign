16 Days of Activism against Gender-Based Violence

(1) Event Summary

 Every year, November 25 marks the UN's International Day for the Elimination of Violence against Women. The 16 Days of Activism begins on this day and continues until December 10, Human Rights Day. The campaign aims to unite society, the public, governments, and UN agencies, maximizing impact to encourage action against gender-based violence. Orange is the campaign's signature color, symbolizing alertness to gender-based violence and the harm and cost it brings. At the same time, the warm and bright orange also symbolizes hope , encouragement for survivors, and the aspiration for a future society free from the threat of violence.



(2) Commentary Perspective

1. Social organizations are playing an increasingly important role in combating gender-based violence

Although gender-based violence is highly prevalent, growing evidence shows that social interventions can have a positive preventive effect. Women-led social organizations and other groups dedicated to preventing and responding to gender-based violence play a crucial role. For decades, these organizations have worked to move "violence against women and girls (VAWG)" from the margins to the center of the global agenda, making it a core element of the global women's rights movement.


2. The work of social organizations and service providers still faces many challenges

Despite significant efforts by these organizations to advance global women's rights, their contributions and impact are often underestimated or even ignored. Sometimes, due to insufficient public awareness of gender-based violence and new policies, their work faces resistance. Moreover, due to lack of funding and human resources, the implementation of women's rights policies and laws driven by these organizations remains inadequate in many countries, allowing perpetrators to escape punishment. The reality remains severe, and both organizations and individuals need more support.


3. Eliminating gender-based violence requires the joint efforts of multiple stakeholders—governments, society, and communities working together

Addressing domestic violence is a complex and systemic project that requires the joint efforts of government, society, and families. Shenzhen offers a notable example: in recent years, the number of domestic violence disputes in Shenzhen has continued to decline, with zero major cases escalating from civil to criminal in the marriage and family domain for consecutive years. Its collaborative governance model is particularly worth learning from, with the core being "co-governance as support, building a governance system involving government, society, and citizens."


1) Government-led, multi-party collaboration All 10 districts in Shenzhen have established anti-domestic violence cooperation mechanisms, including 10 family courts, 3 "domestic violence shelters," and 808 community police stations designated as "domestic violence reporting points," forming a comprehensive, multi-sectoral, and socially inclusive prevention and co-governance framework.

2) Professional services, case management Since 2007, the Shenzhen Women's Federation has built a hub platform for women's social organizations, establishing the "Shenzhen Women's Social Organization Service Base" in the Women and Children's Building, bringing together over 200 women's organizations to provide professional, social, and project-based services for women, children, and families, creating distinctive brands such as "Sunshine Mums" and "Sunshine Parents."

3) Community and neighborhood mutual support Establishing a "Three Staff, Two Leaders" (grid officer, security officer, property manager, building leader, store leader) reporting mechanism; setting up "Circles of Support" domestic violence survivor mutual aid groups in eligible communities; and building a "one-stop" platform for evidence collection and protection for minors. Follow-up visits and enhanced care help build a bottom-line social security system.

News


Susan Li, an Asian woman from Chengdu, officially becomes Meta's new CFO


Meta CEO Zuckerberg announced layoffs of 13%, affecting over 11,000 employees. The layoffs triggered a wave of unemployment, especially as the average age of Fortune 500 CFOs is 53. In November of the same year, Susan Li, an Asian woman from Chengdu, went against the tide and was officially appointed as Meta's new CFO at the age of 36. This signifies that more women are gaining influential positions in the industry. Susan immigrated to the US at age two, came from a modest background, and faced significant xenophobia. This Chengdu girl had to work extremely hard to finally earn recognition.

Asian Americans make history again in midterm elections, with hopes for more leaders to speak up for the community


On November 8, the US reached the peak of the 2022 midterm elections. In cities with large Asian populations like New York and Boston, voting stations displayed Chinese-language signs saying "Vote Here." On social media, Asian voters enthusiastically shared "I Voted" stickers on their clothing. Now that partial election results are in, several Asian Americans have made history, amplifying Asian voices in American politics. Since the 2020 election, marginalized Asian Americans have gradually moved toward the center of the electoral spotlight. However, Asian representation among elected officials remains severely inadequate. A 2021 report found that Asian Pacific Americans hold only 0.9% of elected offices nationwide, despite making up 6.1% of the US population. Under these conditions, Asian voices remain weak, and issues important to Asians continue to go unaddressed. At the same time, Asians are the fastest-growing racial group in the US and are projected to become the largest immigrant group by 2055. Historical experience and current equity movements show that only by seizing opportunities to speak up can they gain attention. The well-being and future of Asians must be improved through greater voice and representation.

BBC faces calls: Do not cut Black and Asian programming

According to The Independent on November 16, the BBC is facing growing calls to abandon its plan to cancel local radio programs for Black and Asian communities, with warnings that the move would alienate minority audiences. The corporation plans to reduce weekday programming on each local BBC radio station to just two shows—breakfast and lunchtime—with all programs after 2 p.m. at risk of being completely canceled or merged into regional and national programming. Cutting these programs would have a devastating impact on audiences and communities of color. Beyond the direct impact on workers' jobs, livelihoods, and families, it would also affect mental health. Zita Holbourne, national president and co-founder of BARAC UK and an activist, launched a petition against the BBC's plan, warning that the move could violate the mandatory race equality duty under the public sector equality duty.

Gender Equality

News


Scotland's First Minister dismisses UN concerns over Scotland's gender law reform

Previously, Scotland's gender recognition reform bill removed the requirement for a medical diagnosis of gender dysphoria when changing gender, reduced the period of living in a self-identified gender from two years to three months, and lowered the minimum application age from 18 to 16. A United Nations special rapporteur expressed concerns about Scotland's new rules on legal gender recognition. Meanwhile, Scottish Conservative leader Douglas Ross repeatedly objected, arguing that the bill could allow men to abuse the right to obtain gender recognition certificates and pose potential risks to women's safety. However, Scotland's First Minister Nicola Sturgeon stated that the bill would protect the rights of transgender people and emphasized that international human rights standards provide a solid foundation supporting the bill's goals and principles.

Reports show women of color are unable to attain senior leadership positions in media organizations

A report analyzing newsrooms and news coverage in six countries—Britain, Nigeria, India, South Africa, Kenya, and the United States—found that women from diverse backgrounds are significantly underrepresented in reporting and editorial leadership roles. The analysis revealed that for every female editor-in-chief, there are at least two—and in some places as many as twelve—men at the same level. Notably, in racially diverse countries such as South Africa, Britain, and the United States, women of color face even greater discrimination. The survey found that while 37% of media organizations in the UK have a female editor-in-chief, only 1% are led by women of color. The report also found that across all countries surveyed, respondents identified childbearing as a significant factor hindering women's career advancement.

UN agencies jointly speak out: violence against women remains the most widespread human rights violation

November 25 is the International Day for the Elimination of Violence against Women. Eleven United Nations agencies issued a joint statement declaring that violence against women and girls is not only one of the most serious forms of discrimination but remains the most widespread and pervasive human rights violation worldwide. Since the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic, 45% of women reported that they or women they know have experienced some form of violence. At the same time, as anti-women's rights movements grow stronger, existing forms of gender-based violence have also increased online. These issues have led to "a shrinking civic space, strong opposition to women's rights organizations, and increased attacks against women human rights defenders and activists." According to the UN statement, large-scale reductions in violence against women can be achieved through sustained feminist movements and advocacy, combined with evidence- and practice-based multi-sectoral action and investment.

WHO: Progress in reducing maternal and infant mortality rates in Africa is slowing

Latest data from the World Health Organization (WHO) indicates that by 2030, there will still be 390 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births in sub-Saharan Africa. To achieve the Sustainable Development Goals, Africa would need to reduce its maternal mortality rate by 86% from 2017 levels, but the current pace of progress makes this goal difficult to achieve. Meanwhile, the pandemic has hindered progress in this area, severely affecting critical health services such as postnatal care for mothers, newborn care, intensive care, and immunizations. The report notes that in Africa, only 65% of births are assisted by midwives, far below the 2030 target of 90%. Additionally, in WHO's survey of 47 African countries, the doctor-to-patient ratio is only 1.55 per 1,000 people, significantly below the WHO standard of 4.45 per 1,000. This is insufficient to meet basic healthcare needs or achieve universal health coverage.

Crimes targeting Asians

News


Motive revealed in murder of Chinese-American female dentist in the U.S.: suspect was her long-term boyfriend


According to ABC7, on the afternoon of August 21, Lili Xu, a Chinese-American woman in California, was shot dead in the Little Saigon community of Oakland. At the time, Xu was riding in a car driven by her long-term boyfriend, Nelson Chia, who had stopped by the roadside. A vehicle carrying 35-year-old suspect Hasheem Bason stopped beside them. Bason got out, walked around to the passenger side, and shot Xu. Xu's death prompted hundreds to hold vigils and mourn her. Oakland City Council President Nikki Fortunato Bas called on the public to seek justice for the murdered Asian woman.


Police placed particular emphasis on this case. In November, they discovered it was a contract killing: Chia had hired Bason to murder his girlfriend. According to court documents, Ms. Xu's estimated net worth was between $12 million and $14 million, leading law enforcement to conclude that Chia killed Ms. Xu for money. Tragically, Chia died by suicide in his jail cell shortly after being arrested. The other suspect, Bason, faces life imprisonment or the death penalty without the possibility of parole.

Spanish youth speeding in car kills 4-year-old Chinese girl


According to the WeChat public account "Huaren Xunxi Wang" (Chinese Information Network), on November 7, a 4-year-old Chinese girl in the town of Alsasua, Navarra region, Spain, was struck and killed by a speeding SUV while riding a scooter across a pedestrian crossing.

The driver, a 23-year-old local resident, tested negative for alcohol and drugs and has been arrested by police. Authorities are investigating the cause of the accident. Preliminary findings suggest the driver was speeding and failed to obey a STOP sign at the pedestrian crossing, directly running over the victim.

It is reported that the victim's family runs a dollar store locally and has an 8-year-old daughter. After the accident, the family collapsed emotionally and required psychological counseling.

Chinese man robbed at gunpoint by gang


According to 1010 Wins on November 9, a Chinese man was recently followed by three assailants on the streets of Flushing. After the man retrieved his car, a group of people dragged him out at gunpoint, placed a bag over his head, bound his hands with zip ties, and forced him to return home to retrieve money. They then took the victim to a remote location in Nassau County, left him there, and drove away.


The New York Police Department released photos of multiple suspects and noted that the gang is also suspected of using the same method to rob another victim earlier the same day. As of now, the suspects remain at large, and the NYPD is urging anyone with information to come forward.

Asian man from California murdered during date in Colombia


According to FOX 5 San Diego on November 15, an Asian family from Orange County, California, recently lost a loved one who was murdered during a trip to Colombia.


On November 10, 27-year-old Paul Nguyen was killed in Medellín, Colombia's second-largest city. His sister, Amy Nguyen, spoke on behalf of the family and launched a GoFundMe campaign to bring Paul's body home. Amy described her brother's death as a "brutal crime."


According to Peru's El Comercio, Paul met a woman through the dating app Tinder and met her at a nightclub. Paul was last seen getting into a car with the woman. Several hours later, his body was found beside some trash bins.


Colombian police suspect the victim was drugged and robbed. No arrests have been made in the case so far.

Seattle man charged with hate crime

According to King5 on November 28, suspect Anthony J. Ransford verbally attacked three Chinese students at Seattle University earlier this month, calling them "China virus." One victim reported seeing the suspect shouting at the three Chinese students on the street, and the suspect eventually headbutted one of them. A witness said he heard the man use the term "China virus" before headbutting the victim. According to court documents, one victim suffered a broken nose in the attack, and another suffered a dislocated shoulder and fractured humerus.


The suspect was convicted in California in 2017 for assault with a deadly weapon and has multiple other convictions in Iowa, including repeated assaults. The suspect has pleaded not guilty to the current charges. The state has set bail at $10,000, stating there is "probable cause to believe the defendant will commit a violent crime."
