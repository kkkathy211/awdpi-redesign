In 2025, AWDPI (Asian Women Development Plan International) marks the fifth anniversary of its founding.


Since its establishment in 2020, AWDPI has been committed to eliminating gender discrimination and sexual violence against Asian women overseas, building a transnational support and rescue system, and advancing community empowerment and advocacy.. Over the past five years, the organization has consistently intensified its efforts in combating sexual and domestic violence, expanding its services from online psychological and legal aid to policy advocacy, public education, and on-the-ground practical projects—helping survivor women rebuild their dignity and futures.


All of this would not have been possible without the quiet dedication and steadfast commitment of frontline volunteers. Through patience and action, they have listened to the voices of those in need, conveyed hope and courage, and helped AWDPI's mission take root and flourish.


On this occasion, we have specially invited AWDPI's outstanding volunteers of 2024 for interviews. This interview series is divided into three parts: upper, middle, and lower. As an introductory piece, this article documents their experiences facing the most difficult moments of seeking help, as well as their reflections and hopes regarding gender equality, community companionship, and transnational mutual aid networks.


Let us collectively honor their power of walking together, turning small lights into a blazing torch!




Xia Yan, Legal Support Division

3 years and 4 months as a volunteer




"We are not passively serving within the patriarchal system; we are using our expertise and passion to shake it, challenge

it from the outside, and bring light to the corners forgotten by this system."


When I first joined, the legal support department was still divided into three branches. I was in the Legal Academic Division, where my core work involved researching and organizing national legal systems against domestic violence, then writing popular legal handbooks. The workload was intense, but the nature of the work was closer to desk research.


At the time, I saw this more as a valuable opportunity to hone my skills in information retrieval, knowledge integration, and academic writing. It was deeply meaningful volunteer work, but felt more like a stepping stone in my academic journey.


The second phase was when I first felt the real meaning of "empowerment." After the department merger, I began to engage in case support work—reviewing research reports, and eventually independently handling a case from New Zealand. This marked the beginning of my shift in understanding. When I saw the legal information, procedural guidance, and contact details of local support organizations I had compiled being passed through case workers to the person in need, I truly felt for the first time that my writing was no longer just cold academic output. These words became practical tools—maps and keys handed directly into the hands of a woman struggling through hardship. I was no longer just a researcher; I had become an enabler.


The third phase made me realize this is a confrontational practice. ". This realization came after I formally entered the legal profession. I noticed that in many criminal discussions and practical forums on sexual offenses, the spotlight always follows the defendant, and applause seems reserved for sophisticated technical defenses. At times, a "highly patriarchal collusion" forms between male lawyers and perpetrators. They share a common discourse, deeply immersed in the rhetoric of "defendant's human rights protection," while the voices of victims are ignored, marginalized, and the professional legal support available to them is minimal.


Of course, this is also driven by the profit-oriented nature of the legal profession. But it is precisely this stark contrast that made me realize everything we do at AWDPI goes far beyond volunteer service. Faced with a legal system built on patriarchy and biased on a global scale, every act of legal education, every support, every empowerment, is a small yet resolute resistance.




Peng Shiyun, Head of Case Coordination Division

3 years as a volunteer




"This work has strengthened my belief in systemic change and the construction of community mutual aid networks."

When a recipient sincerely told me that our work had made a real difference in her life, I deeply understood the value of action. This is far more than just "volunteer work." it is truly helping individuals overcome crises and rebuild strength, showing me how small actions can create ripples of change.


Direct engagement with recipients gave me a deeper understanding of these issues. I profoundly felt the intersection of multiple challenges faced by women.

Asian women overseas experience violence, Overseas women often face structural barriers such as language difficulties, cultural gaps, identity-related anxieties, and weak social support networks, making their path to seeking help exceptionally difficult.


Gender-based violence combined with cultural notions of "family shame should not be aired publicly" often traps victims in a spiral of silence, making the problem more hidden and the harm more enduring. Therefore, I believe rescue work is not just crisis intervention, but also about empowering them with the courage to speak out and access channels for help, breaking their state of isolation.




Li Xuan, Head of Academic Division

2 years as a volunteer




"I hope that every person, regardless of gender, cultural background, education level, wealth, status, or profession, can escape the prison of structural inequality and breathe freely within the prevailing atmosphere of social Darwinism. I also hope my research can contribute to building a society that is more compassionate, more empathetic, and more respectful of human dignity."


I do not interact directly with recipients, but instead provide AWDPI with reports and written materials that are both academically rigorous and accessible. Unlike volunteers who work directly with recipients, the moments when I feel a sense of value are the moments when team members achieve something meaningful.


I hope the Academic Division can become a platform where undergraduate or graduate students passionate about researching women's issues can share information, conduct joint research, and support one another. This year, I created an online form for current Academic Division members to fill in their background, expertise, and research interests.


Looking back at the information members provided and reflecting on why they joined, I realized each member has their own academic background,

research interests, and even future plans. They come from philosophy, communication, psychology, sociology, political science, education, East Asian studies, gender studies, as well as business and environmental science—each bringing their own professional perspectives and ways of thinking about women's issues. Only when these scattered research interests are integrated can we generate fresh topics and research paradigms for women's studies.


I hope the Academic Division can inspire more members' individual research and serve as a springboard for their academic and professional paths. I will continue striving to build such a platform. I hope that one day, when members look back on their academic journeys, they will proudly say AWDPI was a great starting point.


To date, I have participated in writing, revising, reviewing, or are currently working on a total of 8 reports for AWDPI—covering topics and focuses such as Asian women in the U.S. workplace, minority ethnic groups in the U.S. healthcare sector, Palestinian women, Asian women in Brazil, divorced women in China, Chinese communities in the UK, and gender-based violence.


These topics have allowed me to engage with women across different eras and countries, and have made me realize that womanhood and race are inescapable frameworks of human existence. Culture and gender are not merely personal identities; they also shape people's social environments in invisible ways. They influence many areas of life—workplace dynamics, family environments, and community atmospheres—creating structural inequalities that are easily overlooked but deeply impactful.




Long Yao, Academic Research Division / Advocacy and Influence Department / U.S. Office

Long Yao

1 year as a volunteer




"This is not just volunteer work; it also represents a

potential connection and hope."


Once, while chatting with a friend, I mentioned my volunteer experience at AWDPI. Unexpectedly, she suddenly said seriously that a friend of hers was experiencing domestic violence but didn't know who to turn to for help. I immediately recommended AWDPI to her, explaining that we could offer support and resources.


Although I do not know the follow-up due to privacy protection, in that moment I deeply realized: our very existence is a form of power. Not all help yields immediate results, but as long as we are mentioned, trusted, and passed on, we may provide a possibility for a woman to escape violence and start a new life at a critical moment.


At AWDPI, I mainly participate in academic outputs and event planning. For example, I contributed to writing sections of the *Handbook for Coordinating Gender-Based Violence in the UK Chinese Community*, including analysis on the causes of gender-based violence and a chapter on the barriers faced by Asian women in U.S. academia. These topics complement my university studies in gender studies and have deepened my understanding of the challenges faced by Asian women overseas.


During the writing and data compilation process, I increasingly realized that the oppression experienced by Asian women is not simply the sum of "gender + race," but rather an intersectionality issue. They often face multiple structural challenges simultaneously—language barriers, visa systems, cultural stereotypes, and unequal treatment in academia and the workplace. This "1+1>2" marginalization effect often makes them harder to see, understand, and support when facing violence. These reflections constantly remind me that when advocating for gender equality, we must not overlook structural factors such as race, class, and immigration status that intersect and compound one another.




Li Yushan, Academic Division

1 year as a volunteer




"By understanding and writing about the sense of disenfranchisement they have experienced, we touch upon the universal human vulnerability. When people share vulnerability, they also feel a profound connection between each other's lives."


In studying the challenges Asian women face in American academia, we have noticed that under the banner of "diversity," while minority student enrollment has increased, responsibilities such as career guidance for these students are disproportionately assigned to female minority faculty. These additional tasks are neither visible nor counted toward promotion, representing yet another form of invisible labor women face in the workplace.


I think, this is not merely an ivory tower academic study—it is truly creating new narratives. AWDPI brings academic perspectives into contact with real-life experiences, continuously revealing the weight behind the spotlight and the disjuncture between declarations and lived realities. Perhaps this is what "narrative as resistance" means.


In our philosophical analysis of gender-based violence, we discussed Judith Butler's concept of "grievability"——"whether a life is considered worthy of mourning depends on whether it is recognized as a 'full' and 'livable' life." Under the multiple oppressions of patriarchy, racism, and immigration status, the suffering of abused Chinese women overseas risks being systematically excluded from the realm of "grievability." Therefore, what we do is to expand the boundaries of grievability. Data collection, policy advocacy, and public discourse are all ways of politicizing individual tragedies and broadening the boundaries of collective social emotion and ethical responsibility.




Lin Jiayue, Language Support Unit

Volunteer for 1 year




"This work has deeply taught me that every act of listening and companionship is actually repairing a long-denied "subject ivity." As an Asian, as a woman, as an activist, I constantly ask myself: how can I more deeply understand the stories and resistance hidden behind their 'silence,' while respecting their autonomy?"


Gender-based violence has never been a "private issue"—it is deeply embedded within larger structural challenges such as immigration status, cultural barriers, and insufficient legal support. Overseas Asian women often exist in a state of "triple vulnerability"—the intersection of gender, ethnicity, and immigration status makes their voices more easily ignored and their pathways to help more likely blocked. I have come to realize that so-called "help" is not just about providing information or resources, but also a process of removing shame and rebuilding a sense of safety. What we can do is not to speak for them, but to stand beside them, ensuring their voices are heard and their choices are respected.


During the translation process, I reviewed many materials and realized that many people do not understand, Asian women are not merely "gender-disadvantaged"—they are also often trapped in the gap between "stereotypes" and "institutional neglect."


On one hand, in mainstream societal discourse, Asian women are frequently portrayed as "obedient, quiet, and trouble-free" stereotypes. This "docile other" image makes them more likely to be overlooked or even disbelieved when they experience abuse; on the other hand, as foreign immigrants, they often face disadvantages in language, culture, and legal systems, lacking basic channels for complaint and self-protection.


All of this makes me reflect: for these women, "safety" is not just freedom from violence, but also the confidence of being supported by society. If we fail to acknowledge the unique vulnerability Asian women face in cross-cultural contexts, we cannot truly support them. Rescue is not just temporary protection—it requires structural change, including multilingual legal resources, culturally sensitive mental health support mechanisms, and public education that challenges gender and racial stereotypes.




Dolcia, Head of the Reporting and Writing Unit

Volunteer for 3 years




"I joined the volunteer team hoping to redeem myself, but ultimately became a frontline advocate, redeeming more women." Before becoming a department head, I participated in many frontline reporting and writing assignments. Through interviews, I could feel the warmth of the interviewees and listen to their most unique inner voices. Sometimes I touched the shadows rarely seen under the sun; other times, I could "see" a warm flame that comforts humanity.


From individual emotional expression to the spread of collective emotion, this public welfare cause has brought together more and more like-minded people.


In supporting each other, we are also creating and writing miracles. For example, when I was coordinating with a domestic violence survivor, the woman had developed depression due to the abuse inflicted by her partner. She cried during the interview. For me, this was just routine work.


But for her, this might be her only hope—using our platform to finally raise her voice. At that time, I collaborated with the psychological support and legal departments to provide her with assistance. I also invited several caring professionals to contribute written messages included in the interview report, to help more women in similar situations. Afterwards, she expressed deep gratitude and said she would gradually recover and return to normal life.


Through this experience, I deeply realized that this is not just ordinary volunteer work—it might be the


"last straw" for someone in utter despair. Our kindness may change someone else's fate from this moment on. Therefore, it is profoundly meaningful. Rather than calling it "volunteer work," I prefer to call it—"a volunteer cause." The sense of value and happiness this volunteer cause brings is precious beyond measure. I constantly reflect: what I most want to do now is to break people's fixed perceptions of women, and help women themselves realize the shackles imposed by social culture.


Social culture urges women to endure, to sacrifice themselves, to obey men—these are clearly unjust patriarchal narratives. I believe it will take a long time to correct these biased beliefs. He Xianglian, Reporting and Writing Unit




Volunteer for 1 year

"I hope all women can have the courage to experience their 'first time,' the ambition to be the 'first one,' and the courage to strive for countless 'firsts.'"




I have three moments that left a deep impression on me. The first was interviewing a woman who had experienced violence. She was a brave woman who, despite suffering injustice from fate, still dared to fight back with courage. I began the interview with an attitude of "empathy," but while listening to her story, this "empathy" transformed into "respect and admiration," because in her I saw endless strength, courage, and resilience.


The second moment was writing a data journalism piece about women's and children's rights protection during the Israel-Palestine conflict. In traditional historical narratives, war is "men's business"—led by men, fought by men, and destroying through men—yet the vast majority of actual war deaths are women and children,


the true silent majority. The fact that 70% of victims are women has haunted me for a long time. Because they are weak, they are oppressed, lose their lives, and are forced into silence. Weak nations have no diplomacy; the weak have no aid. Only by becoming strong can one barely protect oneself. The third moment was when I wrote the press release about our organization winning an award at a UN side event. While watching Director Sun Yi's speech video, I was deeply inspired—because she dared to take countless "first steps," she was not only able to care for herself, but also to care for the world. I hope women can grow from a marginalized group into a powerful one, thus being able to "care for the world"—this is also AWDPI's vision. Li Yue, Reporting and Writing Unit

A large part of my work is documenting the actions of my fellow team members. AWDPI colleagues go to different countries and regions, responding to complex realities. What I do is to present these actions—sometimes an online sharing session, sometimes interviews with outstanding volunteers.




When I see a volunteer feel understood through an interview, or another volunteer choose to join because of a shared story, I can fully feel

how my writing connects and responds to others.

Not long ago, I was reviewing applications for the 2024 spring recruitment of volunteers. As I reread their motivations and experiences—including my own—I kept returning to one sentence:


"Woman is a situation." For the Reporting and Writing Unit, we don't often directly interact with the women we support; most of the time, we can only reconstruct the scene through documents, reports, and secondhand accounts. But precisely because of this, every act of "retelling" must be handled with care. In these processes,


why we speak, how we speak, and for whom we speak—all must be thoughtfully considered. Qian Yan, National Office (USA)

1 year and 2 months as a volunteer




"Let the law become a tool they can use, not a daunting wall to fear."


I became acutely aware of this it wasn't just a volunteer job, during my first online meeting with the U.S. legal support team.


That meeting lasted a long time. We repeatedly refined the structure and wording of the U.S. legal guidebook, discussing how to express things accurately across different legal systems while ensuring victims could understand. That conversation made me realize for the first time our work wasn't just about completing a document, but about truly helping women with language and cultural barriers find clear, actionable answers in moments of despair or crisis.


Later, while doing legal research, I unexpectedly saw social media sharing the guidebook we had compiled. In that moment, I was deeply moved, and realized that the detailed guidebook we had carefully crafted was actually being read and used. Imagining that this guide might help someone find direction within an unfamiliar legal system, or give them a little courage to take action, I felt this was the most meaningful and valuable moment of my time at AWDPI.


While providing legal support in the U.S., I often helped victims organize case timelines, understand the process of applying for protection orders and filing police reports under the judicial system, and I also saw their deep distrust toward U.S. police, courts, or lawyers. Sometimes it was due to language and cultural barriers that prevented understanding of legal procedures; sometimes it was fear that divorce could invalidate their immigration status; sometimes it was concern about facing injustice in the legal system as Asian individuals.


Abusers might also exploit these fears to control victims, trapping them in deeper passivity and isolation. These women's situations made me realize gender-based violence faced by Asian women overseas is not merely harm within the private sphere, but often compounded by structural barriers related to race, immigration status, language, and legal-cultural differences. This constantly reminds me in my volunteer work to do my best to help remove these barriers, to bridge information gaps and procedural obstacles, and truly make the law a tool they can use, not a wall they fear.




Wang Leyi, External Outreach, Psychological Division

1 year and 3 months as a volunteer




"Domestic violence victim assistance is meaningful, complex, and challenging work. The reality faced by domestic violence victims must never be ignored."


Two months ago, I took on a client. Due to confidentiality, I won't elaborate on her case here. During our conversations, she revealed that her current physical and mental condition was extremely poor and that she urgently needed psychological counseling to improve her well-being.


In our first phone call, she shared many of her personal stories and specific experiences with me, which greatly helped me in the process of connecting her with a counselor. After repeated communication and several counseling sessions, she shared with me some recent insights and personal growth. These reflections gave me a fresh perspective, showing me that sometimes, a simple shift in viewpoint can suddenly make everything clearer.


As the client's condition continued to improve, I also felt a strange sense of shared strength, and my heart was filled with energy. From that moment, I realized that volunteering is not just a simple job, but a powerful bond that connects people across thousands of miles, enabling us to grow and improve together.


My initial understanding of domestic violence was rather naive—I thought this field should have clear "black-and-white" standards, or absolute justice. For example, if a woman was abused by her husband, we should help her completely leave him. But after working for some time and meeting various clients, I came to understand the complexity of reality.


What if helping the woman separate from her husband means she loses her sole source of income? What if it jeopardizes her visa status? These questions constantly remind us that real-life factors are always crucial and cannot be ignored in domestic violence victim assistance. Therefore, helpers may often need to mobilize various social resources (psychological, legal, financial support, etc.) in order to effectively assist victims in gradually overcoming their difficulties.



Over the past five years at AWDPI, countless volunteers like those featured in this article have, through selfless dedication and steadfast companionship, illuminated the lives of many women in crisis, and shown more people the possibility of resisting gender-based and domestic violence. For them, this is not just volunteer work, but an ongoing practice of belief and action.


In the future, AWDPI will continue to deepen community support and promote public education within cross-cultural contexts, working hand-in-hand with more volunteers and supporters to provide a solid foundation for overseas Asian women and others in need. We believe that when small lights come together, they can ignite many more beacons of hope.


If this force has touched you, welcome to join AWDPI. Let's take real action together to protect the safety and dignity of more women.
