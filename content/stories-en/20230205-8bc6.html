Introduction

introduction

In Australia, discussions around gender-based violence are increasingly focused on diversity. However, public policies and social services are still largely based on the experiences of Anglo-Australian women.


Our research, published in the Journal of Intercultural Studies, interviewed 31 frontline workers from mainstream domestic violence organisations, refugee settlement agencies, and migrant organisations supporting women who have experienced violence.



It's All Blamed on "Culture"

PART 01

Our research shows that domestic violence in refugee and migrant communities is frequently racialised, with the causes attributed to the "culture" of refugee and migrant groups..


Some workers claimed that the "culture" of refugee or ethnic minority women makes them silently "endure" violence, whereas Anglo-Australian women clearly would not tolerate such abuse. One Anglo worker from a mainstream domestic violence organisation said:


Perhaps they are not used to living with freedom, rights, and protection [...] I've recently noticed women from African or Middle Eastern countries, possibly refugee women [...] have a much higher tolerance for violence [...] They are already severely injured before seeking help.


When white women are seen "enduring" violence, their "cultural" background is not discussed; instead, the focus is on what barriers they might face in leaving violent situations.


These barriers include financial dependence, and homelessness,, fear for their own or their children's safety, and concerns that legal and police systems may not adequately protect them..


Many people directly ignore systemic issues and broader social inequalities, attributing the reasons why ethnic minority women do not seek mainstream social services to their own cultural backgrounds..


Despite evidence showing that migrant and refugee women experiencing violence often face specific barriers—such as the threat of deportation, economic or language difficulties.



Critically Examining the Term "Multicultural and Linguistically Diverse"

PART 02

" The category of "multicultural and linguistically diverse groups" reinforces this bias: "culture" is seen as something foreign, specific to refugees, migrants, or ethnic minorities—rather than a universal concept shared by all Australians.


Many social workers use "Australians" to refer specifically to Anglo-Australians, even though people from diverse racial and identity backgrounds are clearly also Australians.


The vague term "multicultural and linguistically diverse groups" separates racial and cultural minorities from the Anglo majority and homogenises these minority groups into a single, broad category, leading people to mistakenly assume that if an approach works for one minority group, it will work for all.


Domestic violence organisations are not required to collect clients' data on race, country of birth, or visa pathways—even those serving migrants. Refugees and migrants are often simply categorised as "multicultural and linguistically diverse groups." This limits our understanding of the unique experiences and needs of refugee and migrant women..


It is time to critically consider whether the term "multicultural and linguistically diverse groups" is still useful, or whether it merely exacerbates inequality.



Small-Scale Migrant Services Are Overwhelmed

PART 03

Rather than integrating diverse populations and needs into mainstream services and policies, a growing number of specialised services for multicultural and linguistically diverse groups have emerged.

"Mainstream" (typically Anglo) Australians are usually considered recipients of "mainstream services," while "multicultural and linguistically diverse" groups are increasingly seen as recipients of "multicultural and linguistically diverse" services..


Indeed, there are very few dedicated services specifically supporting culturally diverse groups, but we have also identified some potential consequences.


This approach appears to reinforce assumptions and stereotypes about "culture." Staff working with migrant clients said clients sought their help only because of limited English proficiency (even though interpreters can be hired separately for all services).


Some staff from ethnic minority backgrounds said they accept clients from similar cultural backgrounds because of shared experiences and histories.


Compared to mainstream organisations, specialised services for multicultural and linguistically diverse communities are typically smaller and underfunded.


This practice of referring refugee and migrant women to specialised services may overwhelm small-scale service providers. It also deprives mainstream workers of opportunities to learn from women of diverse backgrounds..


We should no longer view women through cultural stereotypes, nor assume that mainstream services should inherently exclude refugee and migrant women.



It's Time for Change

PART 04

Domestic violence experienced by refugee and migrant groups is often attributed to their culture.


The term "multicultural and linguistically diverse" continues to reinforce this assumption—that domestic violence in these groups ultimately stems from their cultural background. This leads to the "othering" of these groups within mainstream society, excludes them from mainstream services, and results in small-scale services being severely overburdened..


It is time for refugee and migrant women experiencing domestic violence to be heard and recognised within mainstream policies and programs. Policies and services should critically reflect the multicultural realities and inequalities within mainstream institutions.
