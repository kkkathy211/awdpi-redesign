Amid the Pandemic,

Gender Gaps in the Digital Age

UN Women states that since the pandemic, the digital economy has become a key force in the global economy. The flexible work models of the digital economy have created more diverse employment opportunities. However, women's participation and voice in the digital economy and technology sectors remain limited.


Data shows that only 10% of global artificial intelligence professionals are women, and only 29.2% of scholars in global information and communications technology fields are women. The insufficient number of women studying science and technology fields in secondary and higher education is a key reason for the structural challenges women face in the digital transformation. To address this gap, on July 21 this year, UN Women partnered with the Ant Foundation to launch the "Her Future" initiative, aiming to close gender gaps in the digital age and support women micro, small, and medium enterprise owners in developing their businesses, working to break gender inequality caused by unequal digital participation.

Spain Passes Law Emphasizing

That Sex Without "Consent" Constitutes Sexual Assault

On August 25, Spain's parliament incorporated "consent" into the legal standard for determining sexual assault. This means that in any circumstance, any forced sexual act without the individual's permission will be classified as sexual assault. Spain's Minister for Equality, Irene Montero, said on Thursday, "The feminist call—that only consent is consent—has finally become law in our country." Although the precise definition of "consent" is still awaiting further clarification, the passage of this bill marks a significant advancement in Spain's efforts to combat gender-based violence and promote gender equality.

# Anti-Asian Crimes


News


San Francisco's Richmond District,

Two Attacks Reported on the Same Day

According to NBC BAY AREA on August 12, two attacks were reported in San Francisco's Richmond District on August 10.


Surveillance footage shows a man wearing a black hoodie walking east on Geary Street making eye contact with a Chinese American woman. The man then turns, runs toward her, knocks her to the ground, and punches her in the face. The woman hits a parked car, triggering its alarm. The man then flees. Witnesses said the injured woman was a 65-year-old Asian woman, and the attack caused her lips and teeth to bleed and damaged her glasses.


Shockingly, this was the man's second attack that day, with the first occurring just minutes earlier. Surveillance shows he assaulted another woman while crossing the street, and the victim was also Asian.


San Francisco's Richmond District is considered one of the city's safest and quietest neighborhoods, yet it has now become the site of two recent attacks on the local Asian community. The San Francisco Police Department said the suspect in the case has not yet been arrested. Residents nearby have expressed fear and anxiety.

A Man in Times Square

Attacks a Woman with a Box Cutter


According to NBC on August 3, a 30-year-old man attacked a woman with a box cutter in Times Square. The victim, a 59-year-old Asian woman and a tailor by profession, suffered severe cuts to her right hand. After the attack, she was taken to a local hospital and has since been discharged.


The New York Police Department said this was a random attack. The suspect, Anthony Evans, was arrested on August 2 and charged with a hate crime.


According to the "Hate Crime Data Analysis" released earlier this year by the Center for the Study of Hate and Extremism, anti-Asian hate crimes increased by 339% last year compared to the previous year. Police reported that last month, a man headbutted a 27-year-old male victim in Long Island City, Queens, while making anti-Asian remarks. Around the same time, an Asian woman was told to "get out of this country."

St. Paul Police in Minnesota

Respond to Apparent Murder-Suicide Case

On the evening of August 16, St. Paul police in Minnesota responded to a call from a child who reported that his father and mother had been killed. At 9:15 p.m., officers arrived at the scene, where medical personnel pronounced the couple dead.


The St. Paul Police Department said the case was an apparent murder-suicide. 33-year-old Yia Xiong first shot and killed his wife, Ka Lor, then turned the gun on himself. Both were Hmong Asian Americans who had been together for about 10 years and had five children. Prior to the incident, friends and family reported no signs of trouble, and the St. Paul Police had no record of domestic violence involving the couple.


The Women's Rural Advocacy Program said Lor was the 14th person in Minnesota killed by an intimate partner in 2022. Paul Xiong, president of the Hmong 18 Council, said this was the second murder-suicide involving a Hmong family in the area this summer. The local organization "Transforming Generations" is working within the Hmong community to intervene and prevent potential family crises.

Illinois Begins Implementing Requirement for Public Schools to Teach

Asian American History

According to WTTW on August 22, Illinois has mandated that starting this school year, every public elementary and high school in the state must include a unit on Asian American history.


Last summer, Illinois Governor J.B. Pritzker signed the Teaching Equitable Asian American Community History Act (TEAACH), making Illinois the first state to implement such a requirement.


"We need to teach students America's honest history, showing Asian Americans not only as victims, but also as justice advocates within their own communities, standing in solidarity with others," said Grace Pai, executive director of Asian Americans Advancing Justice - Chicago.


The organization is hosting free professional development workshops for educators across Illinois to prepare them to teach Asian American history. Pai said the Illinois State Board of Education will survey all schools and enforce the mandate during the first three years of its annual survey. Given the board's limited authority, she hopes students, parents, teachers, and community members will proactively ask their schools and districts how they plan to implement the Act.


Mueze Bawany, an English teacher at North Grand High School in Chicago, said he plans to meet the new educational requirement by adding a graphic novel unit and a poetry unit to his curriculum.

Asian Residents Sue California County,

Alleging Officials Conducted Targeted Harassment Campaign

According to NBC on August 6, Northern California is facing a racism lawsuit. 


A class-action lawsuit filed Wednesday in federal court names Siskiyou County and Sheriff Jeremiah LaRue as defendants, alleging both conducted widespread harassment against Hmong residents.


The lawsuit, brought by the American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU) and Asian Americans Advancing Justice, states: "This campaign aims to drive out an unwanted minority population, rooted in 1800s anti-Asian racism in Siskiyou."


Local residents say the discrimination includes routine traffic stops, water restrictions, and liens designed to force Asians off their land. The complaint also alleges that county policies have disproportionately affected Hmong cannabis farmers.


The lawsuit notes that although Asians make up only 2.4% of the county's adult population, they accounted for over 28% of sheriff's department traffic stops in 2021. According to NBC's report, the county's population is nearly 45,000, with about 85% identifying as white.


Another lawsuit filed last year led to an injunction after the county was accused of targeting residents with water restrictions, but county officials denied the allegations.


Although marijuana cultivation is legal in California, the county has imposed stricter regulations on marijuana farms since the Hmong population began growing it, and the new laws have arguably been enforced almost exclusively in predominantly Asian areas. When the county enacted a new ordinance prohibiting the transportation of more than 100 gallons of water without a permit, they conducted enforcement only on roads surrounding Asian American communities. While it was intended to target marijuana growers, the lawsuit claims that non-growing Hmong residents were also subjected to increased stops. This has affected the daily lives of Hmong residents, who say they struggle to access water for basic needs.


The Hmong plaintiffs said they were treated entirely as outsiders. "In a striking example, the Board singled out Hmong attendees at a public meeting in 2015, first asking 'Hmong residents' to raise their hands on an issue, then asking 'county residents present' to vote, as if the Hmong were outsiders," the lawsuit stated.
