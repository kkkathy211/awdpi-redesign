On the afternoon of November 2, 2025, the anti-domestic violence film screening event "I Am What I Am" hosted by the Asian Women Development Plan International (AWDPI) Australia National Office was successfully held in Sydney. The event was moderated by the head of the Legal Support Division at AWDPI Australia Office, Zekun Yang and featured a special guest speaker—  Director and Principal Lawyer at Antu Law Firm Carroll Lin (Li-Yun Lin), who provided an in-depth analysis of the film's plot, real-life cases, and Australian family law, delivering a session that combined warmth and professional insight.



We thank TWT, BF Australia, and Hero's Journey Overseas Short Drama Series for their support with the venue and gifts for this event.


The event attracted nearly 50 participants both in-person and online, who engaged in open and heartfelt discussions about the complexity of domestic violence and cross-cultural challenges, sharing real-life difficulties they have faced.

Film and Theme Recap



"I Am What I Am"

The film documents a woman's struggle and rebirth amid domestic violence, revealing that domestic violence is not only about visible physical injuries, but also exposes hidden emotional control and psychological trauma behind abuse. It shows us:

domestic violence may manifest as verbal belittlement in daily language,

helplessness under economic control,

or silent fear from social isolation,

and ongoing psychological manipulation and threats.


These invisible forms of violence are also explicitly protected against under Australian family law.


Starting from several key scenes in the film, Lawyer Lin explained in detail the legal definition and scope of "family violence" under Australian family law:

family violence includes, but is not limited to: physical violence, verbal abuse, emotional abuse, sexual violence, economic control, unlawful deprivation of personal freedom, stalking, harassment, and psychological domination through threats or instilling fear.


In the Australian legal system, the key criterion for determining whether an act constitutes family violence does not depend on outward behavior or form, but rather—whether the victim feels fear, control, or threat as a result.


Five Key Points About Family Violence in the Australian Legal System



Drawing on her extensive experience in family law, Lawyer Lin connected real film scenes to explain how individuals involved in family violence cases—especially when children are affected—can legally protect themselves and their children.


1. If a child sees, hears, or senses family violence—they are a "victim."

Lawyer Lin pointed out that according to Australian family law: if a child "sees, hears, or senses" family violence, they are legally recognized as a "victim of violence." In her real cases, many children had sought help from neighbors or protected their mothers during conflicts, yet simultaneously suffered deep psychological trauma. Witnessing violence can have profound and unpredictable negative impacts on a child's mental health and personal development.



She emphasized: "A child's safety and mental health are always the primary considerations in court decisions."


2. Emotional manipulation involving children after divorce is also domestic violence.

⎡Common situations⎦

  • Using children for emotional manipulation and control

Lawyer Lin noted that during or after separation or divorce, many parents continue to control or retaliate against the other party through their children, for example:

￮ obstructing or interfering with the other party's normal visitation rights

￮ threatening or pressuring the other party using the child as leverage

￮ conveying negative messages through the child or creating fear

These behaviors are also recognized under Australian family law as family violence, because they exert control and instill fear through psychological and emotional means.

  • How courts balance visitation rights and safety

When deciding custody and visitation arrangements, Australian courts always uphold "the best interests of the child" as the core principle.
If there is a history of family violence or potential risk, the court will prioritize the safety of the child and the victim. For example:

￮ requiring supervised visitation for the perpetrator

￮ limiting the time and frequency of visitation

￮ or, in cases of serious ongoing threats, even suspending visitation entirely

All arrangements are made with the highest priority being the prevention of further harm.

  • How to prevent one parent from unilaterally taking the child

To reduce risks, Lawyer Lin recommended establishing a written parenting agreement early in the separation process, clearly specifying:

￮ the child's living arrangements

￮ visitation frequency and methods

￮ decision-making authority and communication methods

If one party takes the child without consent or prevents the other party from exercising legal visitation rights, they should immediately consult a lawyer and apply to the court for a Recovery Order (child recovery order), to prevent the child from being taken out of the city or even the country.


3. Legal evidence collection and protection orders

In situations of domestic violence, the two most important things for victims are safety and evidence. Lawyer Lin emphasized that evidence collection must be conducted with personal safety as the top priority, and information should be as authentic, accurate, and admissible as possible.


She summarized the types of evidence commonly accepted by Australian courts, including:

• text messages, WeChat chats, emails, and other communication records

• transfer records, bank statements, and other financial evidence

• medical reports, psychological evaluations

• police reports, child protection agency records

• factual statements attached to applications for domestic violence orders


Lawyer Lin particularly reminded: "Evidence collection must be legal and safe. Secret recordings or covert photos may be admissible in some extreme cases, but they also carry privacy risks—always seek professional legal advice."


When police confirm an immediate danger, a Provisional ADVO (Provisional Apprehended Domestic Violence Order) can be issued immediately, providing instant protection for the victim and child, and may be referred to the Local Court for further proceedings if necessary.


4. Response measures after violence escalates

Lawyer Lin emphasized that separation or divorce does not mean domestic violence ends. Based on her practical experience, many victims continue to face:

• stalking and tailing

• threatening or intimidating messages

• harassment via social media and text messages

• ongoing emotional control or psychological pressure


All of these behaviors fall under family violence in legal terms..



Lawyer Lin shared several real cases where victims successfully obtained comprehensive legal protection after reporting to police and applying for an ADVO (Apprehended Domestic Violence Order), effectively stopping further harassment by the perpetrator.


 ⎡How to respond to ongoing threats? The first step is always to call the police⎦

"In dangerous situations, the most effective and direct action is—call the police immediately."


Lawyer Lin introduced Australia's multi-layered domestic violence support system, enabling victims to quickly access help when facing threats:


• Emergency contact number: 000

• National domestic violence hotline 1800RESPECT: 1800 737 732

• Legal Aid NSW legal assistance hotline: 1300 888 529

• Translation and Interpreting Service (TIS): 131 450 (for those not fluent in English)

• Child Protection Helpline: 132 111

• Centrelink economic assistance and family welfare application channels (for those experiencing economic control)


These resources cover safety, legal aid, financial support, language assistance, and child protection, helping victims receive timely and comprehensive support during crises.


5. Major Reforms in Australian Family Law

Lawyer Lin emphasized that since 2024, two landmark reforms have been made to Australia's family law, profoundly impacting rulings on domestic violence, custody arrangements, and property division.

May 2024 Reform: Abolition of the presumption of "Equal Shared Parental Responsibility"

This principle was previously widely misunderstood as—"both parents must equally share custody." Over time, this principle was often misinterpreted as "both parents must equally share custody," but in cases involving family violence, this "formal equality" often undermined the victim's safety, the child's stability, and the actual needs of the primary caregiver.


After the reform,, courts will now place greater emphasis on who can provide the safest and most stable environment for the child's growth.

June 2025 Reform: Family violence impact formally included in property division

The second reform explicitly states for the first time that when conducting property division,. courts must consider the economic and psychological impacts of family violence on the parties involved. This means that if a victim has been economically controlled, forced to stop working, lost independent income for a long period, suffered severe psychological trauma, or had their life capabilities impaired due to violence, these harms are no longer seen as "invisible injuries," but will now be acknowledged and compensated for in property division..


Cross-cultural understanding and empathy



As AWDPI has long advocated, domestic violence is not only a legal issue, but is deeply embedded in culture, language, identity, and social structures. For many Asian women, language barriers, cultural differences, and the pressures of immigration status make seeking help more difficult, lonely, and burdensome.


The AWDPI Australia Office continues to provide psychological support, legal counseling, and culturally sensitive community companionship to Asian women's communities, ensuring that every voice can be heard, understood, and protected..



This film screening is not merely a sharing of a movie,
but rather a conversation about courage, understanding, and systemic change. The dialogue.


In the future, AWDPI will continue to collaborate with legal professionals, mental health institutions, multicultural organizations, and community partners to advance cross-cultural advocacy against domestic violence, creating a safe, equal, and dignified living environment for more women and families.
