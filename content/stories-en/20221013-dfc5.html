Book Review of "Jukyo" (The Scream)

"The day you were born—it was such a clear, sunny day! That's why your father decided to name you 'Yoko'."


Yoko Suzuki is the protagonist of *Jukyo*, a suspense novel written by Japanese social mystery novelist Makiuchi Akira.. On the day she was born, the sky was cloudless and sunlight filled the air, so she was given the name "Yoko," which seemed poetic and meaningful.


However, this name was chosen randomly by Yoko's parents. During Japan's second baby boom, "Yoko" was the most common name among 2.09 million baby girls, while her younger brother "Jun" was named only after her mother bought several baby-naming books and narrowed down over twenty candidate names. From the moment she was born, because of her gender, Yoko was destined to be an insignificant figure in the family, destined never to receive the love and expectations given to others.


She was like a goldfish unable to leap out of its fishbowl, surrounded by an ever-present sense of suffocation, and no matter how she turned or changed direction, she always met an invisible, impenetrable glass wall.



#1

Family: The Tearing Away of a Happy Facade


"To be honest, I really wanted a boy," Yoko's mother once told her bluntly and without hesitation. After Jun's birth, her mother repeatedly exclaimed, "I've finally had a son—I'm so happy! I even feel that giving birth to him was my life's ultimate mission!"


This was a family where a girl could never receive loving responses. From childhood on, Yoko was tormented by questions like, "Why am I never as good as my brother?" "Why can't I receive my family's love?" "Why is my mother so biased?"—and the answers remained elusive. These questions were tied to gender, tied to a patriarchal society. As a victim, as an innocent child growing up in an environment of suppression and neglect, Yoko could not grasp these deeper causes. As a child, she felt as if there was a hole inside her heart; because she couldn't meet her mother's expectations, she blamed herself, felt empty, and gradually became an emotionally starved, insecure, approval-seeking, and desperate person longing to escape.


Why did Yoko's mother act this way? Perhaps it was the transmission of patriarchal consciousness—even as a woman, she had unconsciously absorbed misogynistic ideas throughout her upbringing, and slowly projected this attitude directly onto her daughter.


Yoko's mother was born during Japan's first baby boom after World War II. Yoko's grandfather often told her, "No need to go to university! What's the point of a woman studying so much? A woman's lack of talent is her virtue!" In that era, women's workplaces were seen as training grounds for marriage—women would find partners at work, and jobs centered on pouring tea and running errands were widely regarded as another form of bride training. Many women left the workforce after finding a spouse and returned home. Yoko's mother was one of them. After marriage, she left her company and became a housewife, cooking, cleaning, and caring for children every day. She saw this as her life plan, believing that "men should work outside, women should stay at home" was the best arrangement.


But was this truly "the best"? Yoko didn't think so. Even though her mother constantly used "happiness" as a catchphrase and always told her to compare herself with children from slums to learn gratitude, Yoko still felt that "happiness" was merely a false mask covering up her family's reality. This wasn't just because Yoko suffered unfair treatment from her mother, but more crucially because as a child, she had personally witnessed her father's sexual violence against her mother.


The collapse of this false happiness was sudden and shocking. One night, Yoko walked in on her parents arguing because her father had cheated. The argument, which should have favored her mother, ended with her father violently and savagely committing sexual assault. Yoko saw her mother—always fake-smiling, never admitting fault—submit to the violence without calling for help or resisting. She suddenly felt a chill run down her spine. The concept of "family," already confusing to her, crumbled like an empty shell; the beautiful memories vanished—everything had been a pretense, and her mother's daily happy smiles were all fake.


As a series of events unfolded—her younger brother's accidental death, her father leaving home due to debt, the house being mortgaged, her mother moving in with relatives—Yoko finally left the family that had bound her since childhood, escaping the suffocating fishbowl. But her path ahead was not an open ocean for free swimming.



#2

Emotion: An Unreliable Bubble


Yoko left home and began living alone, just like the new-generation independent women portrayed in magazines. She entered a new phase of life, starting from scratch, which felt fresh and exciting. The most dreamlike part of the entire book occurs in this stage—Yoko reunites with Shuichi Yamazaki, and even more "luckily," she discovers that her past secret crush was mutual.


Shuichi was Yoko's first love. They met and bonded in their middle school art club. During junior high, they often drew together, chatted, and walked home together. Later, Yamazaki transferred schools due to moving, and they lost contact. He missed his chance to confess during their final meeting, and they were separated for ten years. Ten years later, they reunited at a convenience store. By then, Yamazaki was an unknown but rising manga artist, while Yoko was an ordinary part-time worker. Memories and the past drew them close again, and they saw the reunion as fate. "As long as I'm with you, I have the strength to face challenges. I need you. Please come with me," Yamazaki said. This feeling of being needed was exactly what Yoko had longed for—something her mother and family had never given her. Not long after reuniting, Yoko and Yamazaki quickly entered marriage.


Due to work requirements, Yamazaki had to live in Tokyo or its suburbs, which reignited Yoko's long-held dream of escaping her family's constraints and moving to Tokyo—the big city she once yearned for, even believing she'd never get a place there. Together, they rushed toward their dream city, Tokyo. There, Yoko initially worked a tedious job answering phones, constantly scolded but striving for a better future. Life was simple but happy. But two years later, due to Yamazaki's career setbacks and infidelity, their relationship cracked, and the happiness of their reunion faded away like the wind.


As for Yoko's later romantic experiences, as police officer Ayano Okunuki reluctantly told Yamazaki, after the divorce, she registered multiple marriages, but except for Yamazaki, none of her husbands survived. The man in her second marriage, who verbally abused and physically assaulted her while depending on her financially, and the boss she met in her next job, who treated her as a money-making tool—these people were all forces pushing Yoko further into the abyss. It could be said that Yoko, lacking self-rescue and deprived of help and understanding from others, repeatedly fell in the emotional world, gradually blurring and losing herself while chasing recognition and the feeling of being needed.



#3

Money: An Endless, Recurring Trap


Japan's marriage law only requires women to wait six months before remarrying after divorce. Yamazaki remarried seamlessly into a new family, while Yoko sought a higher-paying, more stable job to build a better future. Later, she joined a new company as an insurance salesperson, responsible for visiting clients and meeting sales targets, treating this as the starting point of a new life. But this starting point did not lead Yoko to a better future—it pushed her even deeper into the abyss.


At first, Yoko tasted the sweetness of high income. She spent all her earnings on clothes, makeup, and rent. But as new clients dwindled, she hit a career bottleneck. Facing mounting expenses, along with verbal abuse and psychological manipulation from her supervisor, Yoko blamed herself, believing her failure to perform was due to insufficient effort and that she had disappointed her boss. So she worked even harder, trying every possible way to gain her supervisor's approval and praise. Eventually, she stepped onto a path of selling her body to earn money for work, then spending that money on shopping and consumption—a one-way road into an endless cycle of "selling herself for money." Her methods of earning money increasingly turned toward crime and darkness. Sadly, despite giving everything to her supervisor, he saw her only as a trivial tool for profit. Yoko, whose name carried meanings of sun and light, step by step descended into the depths of darkness.


Yoko's gradual descent into darkness is a key plot element in this suspense novel. The book opens with the discovery of a woman's lonely death, which then leads into the unfolding story.



#4

Conclusion


Since *Jukyo* is a suspense novel, the above narrative briefly outlines only several key episodes showing how the protagonist Yoko falls into the abyss, avoiding spoilers of the thrilling plot. Overall, the novel's tightly interwoven storyline is highly engaging. Its content skillfully incorporates major elements of Japan's historical and social context—such as lonely deaths, earthquakes, and baby booms—and highlights women's lives under patriarchy. It is a story well worth reading, reflecting upon, and contemplating.




The female character Yuko in this book can serve as a starting point for us to observe women's survival and reflect on women's lives. We see that from birth, Yuko suffers injustice under the patriarchal system, leading her to constantly seek others' approval in life—and this extends to her emotional experiences as well. This is also the starting point for her decision to sell her body to advance her career and gain financial support from her boss.


Unfortunately, throughout Yuko's ongoing experiences of verbal abuse, physical violence, and psychological oppression, no one understands, no one helps, and she does not seek direct assistance. Therefore, even though Yuko has wanted since childhood to escape the suffocating world that feels like a fishbowl, she ultimately loses direction after repeated setbacks.


"Humans are merely natural phenomena, without reason," is a phrase repeatedly accepted in Yuko's mind. She uses it to downplay her own experiences and to conceal her own wrongdoings. But in real life, we are not "natural phenomena." Although we cannot personally experience Yuko's life, and thus cannot stand on an absolute moral high ground to blame or resent her, if similar things happen to us or to someone around us, even when facing pervasive external oppression, we can still strive to recognize the cage and the constraints, do our best to avoid them, seek help from external organizations or others, or unite women's strength to bring change, to exercise human agency, so that another Yuko will never appear again.



Women, due to their innate gender, are often defined and restricted from birth just like Yuko. We hope more women will awaken, recognize the constraints and injustices, and then make more attempts, gain more experiences, cultivate independent thinking, not depend on anyone, first become independent individuals, and first learn to love themselves.


A sound personality, a wise mind, a courageous attitude, and responsible, committed organizations are the best ways to change the current situation. We also hope more people will learn about women's protection organizations, harness women's strength, and seek help when necessary—this is also the original intention of Avoice.


Yuko is a name taken from a bright, clear sky. Bright places should also be where Yukos stand. In this constantly evolving era, let us use our efforts to truly place Yuko under the sunlight!
