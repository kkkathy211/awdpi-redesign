 Summary:


In a country like the United States, composed of immigrants from around the world, internal contradictions have always existed, with discrimination being particularly prominent. Especially for Chinese Americans, there has long been a "model minority" stereotype. This controversial concept, while seemingly a compliment to Chinese Americans on the surface, is in fact a form of veiled discrimination against the Chinese American community. However, as Chinese and Asian Americans become increasingly involved in political activities, this stereotype may soon begin to change. For example, Wei Hua Yan is running for office as a council member in Nassau County, becoming the first Chinese American candidate in the community. Meanwhile, the Chinese Railroad Workers Center in the United States made its public debut this month on the 154th anniversary of the completion of the first transcontinental railroad, commemorating the history of Chinese laborers' struggles in America. However, this month also saw multiple crimes targeting Asians and Chinese people worldwide, with victims including teenagers, international students, and women. Therefore, these groups need to be more vigilant abroad to ensure their personal safety. For details, please see the content of this month's newsletter.

News Commentary


CBC News, a Canadian public broadcaster, recently interviewed four Asian individuals on the harms of the "model minority" stereotype


(1) Incident Summary

CBC News, a Canadian public broadcaster, recently interviewed four Asian individuals on the harms of the "model minority" stereotype, two of whom were Chinese Canadians who shared their personal views on this label.


" The term "model minority" refers to a racial or religious population group whose members are perceived to have a higher socioeconomic status. This term is closely associated with American culture and is commonly used in North American contexts.


In American society, Asians, especially Chinese Americans, are often labeled as the "model minority group." In this widely recognized public image, Chinese Americans are seen as hardworking, ambitious, law-abiding, politically indifferent, enduring social injustice without protest, and never "causing trouble." Chinese families place particular emphasis on their children's education, with a clear and singular path to "success"—study hard, get into a prestigious university, and become respected, high-income professionals such as doctors, lawyers, engineers, or Wall Street financiers.


The "model minority" is a controversial concept that portrays Asians as intelligent, hardworking, quiet, obedient, analytical, and high-achieving. While it appears complimentary, it effectively separates Asians from other minority groups. This narrative is often used to contrast Asians with African Americans and Latinos, portraying Asians as law-abiding and hardworking citizens, while implying that Latinos and African Americans are often associated with high crime rates and reliance on welfare.



(2) Commentary Perspectives

1. A seemingly complimentary label actually perpetuates discrimination against Asians

Statistical data is often used to support the model minority concept, such as higher representation in advanced degrees and white-collar professions. A common misconception is that the affected groups feel proud of the "model minority" label. In reality, this stereotype is harmful because it pits different minority groups against each other, suggesting that "non-model" minorities have failed to achieve what the model minority has due to their own shortcomings.


Some statistics indicate that 99% of racism against Black people comes from white people, while attacks on Asians come not only from whites but also from Black and Latin American groups. This concept has been criticized by media outlets such as National Public Radio (NPR). Some Asian Americans point out that while the label may seem flattering, it only serves to reinforce societal discrimination against Asian Americans.


2. The "model minority" myth is fundamentally rooted in white supremacy, and the harm it causes is deeply hidden

Kimberley Wong, interviewed by CBC News, stated that the essence behind the "model minority" myth is white supremacy. "The function of the model minority narrative is to make groups who pose less threat to white people... become useful tools for white supremacists," said Ms. Wong. She believes that by "fighting for fairness for those who should be and will never be able to fit in," the racial discrimination embedded in the "model minority" myth can be dismantled.


Zoe Si, a woman from Vancouver, told CBC: "The harm the model minority label brings is very hidden, because on the surface, you appear to be a happy, successful person." Zoe herself is a typical example. She excelled academically as a child and believed these stereotypes were part of her Chinese identity. "I didn't realize it was a problem that people only saw me as a high-achieving Chinese student and ignored all my other aspects," said Zoe. When she left school and began working as a litigation lawyer, she felt her abilities were consistently underestimated and faced repeated obstacles. The narrow societal standards and expectations imposed on her as an Asian led her into a cycle of "proving herself," only to discover later that these stereotypes were completely disconnected from what society truly demands of talent.


3. Second-generation Chinese immigrants are breaking free from the constraints of stereotypes and actively participating in social development and political activities as leaders

Statistics show that Chinese American students on average perform far better on the SAT than African American, Hispanic, and other minority groups, and significantly outperform even white students, who rank second. However, in Ivy League university admissions, Chinese American students face widespread systemic discrimination. Admissions officers hold a perception that Chinese students excel academically and in exams but rarely engage in public affairs and lack strong social skills, making them less ideal candidates for elite schools that emphasize "serving society." This is undoubtedly a form of ethnic discrimination based on stereotypes, yet the persistence of this image reveals an awkward truth—Chinese Americans have long been a group that, either actively or passively, remains voiceless and invisible in American society.


John Chiang, former California State Treasurer and gubernatorial candidate in 2018, expressed helplessly: "Typically, people don't see Asians as leaders, but as competent individuals. In the collective consciousness, Asians are not seen as political leaders."


Another important fact is that Asians have become the fastest-growing ethnic group in the United States. In New York City, the Asian population has increased by over 345,000 since 2010, accounting for 15.6% of the city's population. Among them, Asian residents in the Long Island area have grown the fastest, increasing fivefold over the past decade and now making up 34% of the local community. This surge in Asian population is reshaping the socioeconomic landscape of local communities and emerging as a potential political force. After decades of difficult integration, second-generation Chinese immigrants have come of age. They have shed their parents' fear of politics and are no longer content to be professional elites, showing increasing interest and participation in political life. Numerous young Chinese American politicians have already entered the U.S. political arena and are being embraced and integrated into mainstream political circles.

A 16-year-old Asian teenager was stabbed to death at a bus stop in Australia by a gang of high school students


According to Australian media reports, a 17-year-old boy was arrested by Victoria police on suspicion of stabbing 16-year-old Pasawm Lyhym. Police revealed that two other suspects of the same age have been identified but remain at large. Witnesses, including other students and commuters, said the incident occurred shortly before 3:45 p.m. on May 19, when Lyhym, an 11th grader at Staughton College, was stabbed in the neck at a bus stop in Sunshine. Police confirmed through surveillance footage from the area that the gang that attacked Pasawm was armed with knives, though the specific cause of the altercation remains unclear.


Lyhym died at the scene despite medical efforts, while a 16-year-old boy from Sunshine North sustained minor injuries and was hospitalized afterward. Mawn, Pasawm's brother, was at work when he was informed that his younger brother had been stabbed, and he immediately tried but failed to contact Pasawm. Mawn could not understand why Pasawm was attacked: "He was a kind person, and still so young." Pasawm enjoyed making him laugh, loved music, dance, karate, and football, and dreamed of becoming a carpenter. He hopes the perpetrator will apologize to him and his family. The Victoria Matu community also issued a statement expressing heartfelt condolences to Lyhym's family.

A 25-year-old Chinese man found dead in a luxury apartment in Paris, France, with signs of binding and beating on his body


According to reports, on the afternoon of May 16 local time, a 25-year-old Chinese man was found dead in an apartment in the 16th arrondissement of Paris by his landlord.


The entire apartment was thoroughly searched. According to sources: "The body was found at home, hands and feet bound to the bed, mouth sealed with tape." In addition, there were marks of strangulation and physical assault on the body. Preliminary investigations indicated that the victim was seen alive on Monday. The Paris prosecutor's office confirmed, "Following the discovery of a dead body in an apartment in the 16th arrondissement today, the regional criminal division of the judicial police has launched an investigation into murder and aggravated robbery." His body was transferred to the Institute of Forensic Medicine in Paris for autopsy. The location is one of the most expensive residential areas in Paris, home to many luxury residences, hotels, and foreign embassies, drawing widespread public attention.

News


Sudden shooting at a bar in Badalona, Spain's Chinese-populated area; bar owner killed on the spot


On the evening of Thursday, May 25 local time, a shooting occurred at a bar in Badalona, resulting in the death of one man and serious injuries to a woman.


Witnesses said the shooter arrived on a motorcycle at the bar located on Avinguda Marquès de Montroig. The gunman entered the bar without removing his helmet, opened fire, then immediately fled the scene with the motorcycle rider. Besides the victim and his daughter-in-law, three or four other customers were present but managed to escape after the shooting. Shortly after the incident, police patrols, city guards, and ambulances quickly arrived at the scene. The victim's daughter-in-law, shot in the shoulder and leg, is currently out of life-threatening condition, while the victim was pronounced dead at the scene.


It is understood that the area near the crime scene is home to many Chinese warehouses and schools, and many Chinese residents live there. The incident has caused great anxiety among nearby residents.

Chinese woman attacked and robbed on the street in New York, dragged halfway into a vehicle and beaten


On the morning of May 16, a Chinese American woman in Brooklyn, New York State, was the victim of a violent robbery. As she walked near the entrance of an elementary school, a woman in a parked SUV gestured to the victim. When the victim approached, the female robber suddenly reached out and forcibly yanked the necklace she was wearing. The male driver then dragged half of the victim's body into the vehicle and beat her before snapping off her necklace and quickly driving away.


Currently, detectives from the 62nd Precinct are investigating the case, and the two suspects—a man and a woman—are being sought by police. The man is described as around 30 years old, and the woman around 50. Police suspect this criminal duo may have committed similar crimes before, and there may be more victims in the community.

Child marriage is declining, but at the current pace it will take 300 years to eliminate it completely


A newly released report shows that the number of child marriages globally is decreasing, but the pace remains too slow. UNICEF warns that the world is still far from achieving the goal of eliminating child marriage by 2030.


Claudia Cappa, the report's lead author, said: "The good news is that child marriage is declining around the world." Over the past decade, the proportion of child marriages has dropped from 23% to 19% of all marriages. However, more than 12 million girls under the age of 18 still get married every year. Therefore, if the situation does not change, it will take approximately 300 years to completely eliminate child marriage."


According to UN estimates, currently 640 million girls and women worldwide were married before the age of 18, and 12 million girls become child brides every year.


Cappa said: "The causes of child marriage vary across regions, but are often linked to poverty and lack of development opportunities for women." "Gender inequality, outdated social norms, weak law enforcement, and fear of premarital pregnancy also contribute to child marriage to some extent."

Turkish President Erdoğan fuels culture war with rhetoric opposing LGBT and promoting "conservative family values"


At campaign rallies across Turkey, President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan has seized opportunities to attack LGBTQ+ people. At a rally near the Black Sea, he said: "We oppose LGBT. Family is sacred to us; strong families mean a strong nation."


At another rally earlier this month, Erdoğan accused every party in the Turkish opposition alliance of being linked to LGBT.


Erdoğan also withdrew Turkey from the Council of Europe's Istanbul Convention, which aims to combat violence against women. While promoting conservative family values, he has attacked organizations defending women's and queer rights.


For many Turkish women and LGBTQ+ people, the upcoming election represents a stark choice. They say Erdoğan's re-election could further intensify a culture war. Erdoğan and his supporters have already taken numerous actions to fuel this war, authorizing institutions to target anyone perceived as different, and turning a blind eye to nationwide violence against women, gay, and transgender people.

The UN Committee on the Elimination of Discrimination against Women issues concluding observations on eight countries at its 85th session


The United Nations Committee on the Elimination of Discrimination against Women held its 85th session in Geneva from May 8 to 26, during which it issued concluding observations on the implementation of the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women by China (including Hong Kong and Macao Special Administrative Regions), Germany, Iceland, São Tomé and Príncipe, Slovakia, Spain, Timor-Leste, and Venezuela. In addition to recognizing positive progress made by these countries in fulfilling their obligations, the Committee also raised major concerns and made corresponding recommendations.


In its concluding observations, the Committee acknowledged China's progress in legislative reforms since 2014 and efforts to improve institutional and policy frameworks to promote gender equality.


At the same time, the Committee pointed out that women's representation in political and public life in China remains insufficient, especially in terms of equal representation in decision-making positions; it also expressed concern about excessive restrictions on the registration of non-governmental organizations in China, calling for ensuring that women human rights defenders are not intimidated, harassed, or retaliated against for their work; regarding Germany's persistent 18% gender pay gap, the Committee recommended measures to narrow the income disparity; it also recommended that Germany regulate the dissemination of artistic content that promotes gender-based violence; the Committee noted that Spain lacks an intersectional approach in implementing gender-equality laws, and gender-based violence remains widespread.

New York Chinese community supports first Chinese American candidate for Nassau County legislator


The New York Chinese General Chamber of Commerce and the New York Shandong Association hosted a meet-and-greet and fundraising event in Great Neck, Long Island, to support Wei-Hua Yan's campaign for Nassau County legislator. Yan said that Asian Americans make up 12% of Nassau County's population and are growing every year. While Asians have made significant contributions to Nassau County's development across various fields, their political representation remains inadequate. That is why he is running to represent Asian interests, to speak up for Asians, and to fight for greater political participation and voice. At the same time, Yan hopes to inspire more Asian Americans to actively run for office at all levels of local government and organizations to serve the community. He also pledged to work toward building a safer and more comfortable community, reducing tax burdens for residents and businesses, attracting more high-tech enterprises to boost the local economy, and protecting natural resources.

U.S. Railroad Workers Center commemorates the struggle history of Chinese laborers

On the 154th anniversary of the completion of the first transcontinental railroad in North America, the Chinese Railroad Workers History Center in San Francisco's Chinatown officially opened to the public for the first time on the 10th, honoring the struggles of Chinese laborers and recognizing their immense historical contributions to America's political and economic development. Lieutenant Governor Eleni Kounalakis stated in her speech that the construction of the transcontinental railroad from west to east was particularly difficult, especially in California and the mountainous regions of Nevada. "It took the courage, strength, sweat, and even lives of Chinese immigrants to complete this most challenging section," said Kounalakis. "The contributions of Chinese workers will forever be treasured in American history." Deputy Consul General Pan Qingjiang noted that according to a recent national survey, three-quarters of Chinese Americans experienced racial discrimination within the past 12 months. He expressed hope that the Chinese Railroad Workers History Center would raise public awareness, strengthen mutual understanding between China and the United States, combat racism and hate crimes, and benefit the peoples of both nations.

Another Hollywood Chinese American actress honored with a star on the Walk of Fame

On May 30, Steve Nissen, president of the Hollywood Chamber of Commerce, celebrated Chinese American actress Ming-Na Wen as she received her star on the Hollywood Walk of Fame. Ming-Na Wen is the 2,757th star to be awarded and the sixth Chinese or Chinese American star after Anna May Wong, Bruce Lee, Jackie Chan, Lucy Liu, and James Hong. She sees this honor not only as a personal achievement but also as a shared honor for the entire Asian American community: "I am so thrilled and deeply feel a sense of mission—I will continue striving for this honor."


After 30 years of dedication in Hollywood, Ming-Na Wen has delivered numerous outstanding roles and works, shaping many iconic Chinese American images. In 1993, she made her big-screen debut in *The Joy Luck Club* as June Woo. In 1998, she won the Annie Award for Outstanding Individual Achievement for Voice Acting in an Animated Feature Production for her voice role in the animated film *Mulan*. Her portrayal of Dr. Jing-Mei Chen in *ER* earned her a nomination for Best Television Actress at the Asian Excellence Awards. Her deeply memorable role as Agent Melinda May in *Agents of S.H.I.E.L.D.* led to a 2014 People's Choice Award nomination for Favorite Actress in a New Drama Series. In 2019, Ming-Na Wen received the Disney Legends Award, an honor given to only 12 performers, with only one other Chinese American actor receiving it before her.
