Introduction

On October 6, 2022, the Nobel Prize in Literature was awarded to 82-year-old French female writer Annie Ernaux. She is not only a living memory of past struggles but also an inspiration for today's social activists—on issues of gender, social class, and equality. Fighting for social justice and resisting injustices against women are not only two central threads in her writing, but also the main lines of her life. She believes in literature, believing that words possess immense power, as she said in her Nobel lecture: "I want to engrave in literature the voice of a woman and of someone who has crossed social classes, because literature will always be a place of freedom."


The original text comes from the French editions of Marie Claire and Les Inrockuptibles. The following article was translated by the French team of Avoice's language support team, hoping to allow more people to reflect, through dialogue with Annie, on the difficulties women face today, the relationship between literature and reality, and questions of belonging in the social world.


Since 1974, the 2022 Nobel laureate in literature has been building a powerful body of work that intertwines intimacy, social justice, and women's rights—resonating today across borders and generations.

Annie Ernaux agreed to meet with French literary critic Nelly Kaprièlian and Italian novelist Simonetta Greggio, who wished to deliver a letter from a male friend. Through this small note, the friend invited Annie to reflect on her journey and sharply and accurately articulated the issues women face today.


A Letter to Annie Ernaux


"Dear friend, when you told me you were going to meet Annie Ernaux, I thought of her again, of what she means to me. Between the ages of 15 and 17, I devoured French literature from the 18th and 19th centuries, Russian, Spanish, Latin American, British classics, detective novels by Jean-Patrick Manchette, European and American drama, poetry, and even the Nouveau Roman, which always bored me. But Duras's work was truly exceptional. In short, I read a lot, even becoming quite familiar with 20th-century French literature.

 

I probably read one book a day back then. When I finished Annie Ernaux's La Place, I had just discovered Patrick Modiano. She was the first person who made me feel, when talking about women, as if she were talking about me. Her work is extremely sincere, precisely described, stripped of any unnecessary decoration or embellishment, striking directly at my heart. I saw her as another version of myself.. I had already read works by some female writers—Simone de Beauvoir, Virginia Woolf, Sylvia Plath, who fascinated me, Sarah Bernhardt, and I also liked Marceline Desbordes-Valmore. I even read stories about prostitution or drug addiction, but I had never before so authentically placed myself in women's shoes, truly felt what they feel.

 

It was then that I began to form deep friendships with women. I believe Ernaux helped me understand women better. Since then, I have never stopped reading her work, because I always find in it people I recognize, speaking to me in a shared language, resonating with me. Last night I thought about it. I wanted to tell you this before heading to work this morning. Forever supporting you, loving you, Daniel."

 

P.S.: Finally, I'd like to add that she (Ernaux) also helped me understand my own identity as a man.


  Words  

Annie Ernaux: These words deeply moved me; they moved me even more because they were written by a man. It was precisely Passion simple, a first-person narrative, that won me readers—male readers as numerous as female ones. But for other books, such as L'événement, which recounts the female bodily experience of secret abortion, male readers needed more time to understand.

 

The men who read these books are deeply shaken. I remember a Canadian reader once told me: "I read your book. I was born in the same year you had your abortion. It terrified me, because I felt I might not have been born due to that abortion." I told him there are many fetuses in the world that never get born, and that is perfectly natural.

 

He was frightened by these words because men still know nothing about what abortion truly entails; they are completely ignorant of the process. Even today, as women begin to speak more about themselves—about menstruation, menopause—there remain many silences, many taboos surrounding women. Abortion is one of them. But since 2000, I believe the situation has gradually begun to improve.



"We often have freedom of speech, especially in literature.

But for women, it helps very little."


I really like the generation of women who were 20 years old in 2000 and are now turning 40. Over these years, I've sensed a shift in their thinking and in their attitude toward men. Their desire for equality has grown stronger. They are questioning male norms. I mean even small things—like always paying their own share when invited to a restaurant, or going out freely without explanation or interruption. Just like those women who signed the petition—you remember?


[A January 9, 2018 column in Le Monde reported that a hundred women gathered, using an anti-feminist strategy to deliberately mock the physical and psychological harm men inflict on women. Their slogan was: "We defend the freedom to pester, which is essential to sexual freedom." —Original Editor's Note]

Simonneta Greggio: You have contributed greatly to women's freedom, and this is not just in terms of speech.

 

Annie Ernaux: I don't like the term "freedom of speech," because if we only have free speech, we won't get far. Our speech is often free, especially in literature, but it changes nothing for women.

 

Simonneta Greggio:  But you have participated in these discussions, your words have taken a stand on these issues—your writing is an act. When you write, do you have an active, hopeful desire to change things?

 

Annie Ernaux: The fact that girls could not have the same rights as boys cast a shadow over my early life. They were oppressed by patriarchal rules, a patriarchy that permeates society. When I was in my twenties, to make a long story short, the only options were marriage or abortion.

 

In 1974, I published my first novel, Les Armoires vides (The Empty Chests). Since then, I have continuously expanded in two dimensions: social issues and feminism. Fighting for social justice and resisting injustice against women—these are the two threads of my writing. More difficult still, they are also the two threads of my life. But I can tell you, for my life, these two threads are even more complicated.

 

[Annie laughs. Some laughter expresses apology; Annie's laugh is not one of them. Her lively, free, and mischievous laughter reflects exactly who she is. This laugh, in a way, foreshadows and supports what she is about to say; it provokes, allowing the audience to feel a unique irony.]


Nelly Kaprièlien: In the final pages of Les Années (The Years), you said you regarded this book as a tool of resistance. Who are you fighting for, and what are you resisting?

 

Annie Ernaux: Resisting social inequality and defending women's rights—these two things are connected for me. Overall, it's about opposing injustice. This relates to my political views, which don't often change. The world we first inhabit during our upbringing decisively shapes the literary path we choose. Bourdieu once said: literature is the intersection of a habitus and a literary field. I find his words extremely accurate. For example, my habitus is popular, feminist. When I was 20 and writing, I only connected with the literary field; I hadn't yet grasped my own habitus. That was in 1960, when I was influenced by the Nouveau Roman movement. From then on, when I wrote, my awareness—my awareness of my position and my origins—was entirely confined within that literary framework. By the 1970s, women had gained a voice. This was crucial, because I gained a voice—I spoke about abortion, and about things I wanted to discuss but that had left no trace in literature: social fracture. I was born into the lower social class but received higher education. At first, I wrote in an angry tone, because my resistance was real. I will never give up resistance, but I no longer write in the same tone. This change began with La Place, a book in which I stopped writing about myself and began writing about my father. I no longer use an angry tone, because anger quickly breeds populism, miserabilism, and complaint.




"Once, this fierce struggle lasted for a time.

I was doubly unfortunate:

as a woman, I was wretched; as a traitor to my class, I was even more suffering."


Men

Simonneta Greggio : Since the publication of your novel *The Years*, you have finally become widely known.

 

Annie Ernaux : You mean, recognized?

 

Simonneta Greggio: Yes, recognized. In 2020, you were shortlisted for the Booker Prize (an international literary award presented in the UK). In 1984, you won the Prix Renaudot (a major French literary prize) for *A Man's Place*. Aside from these two awards, the public knows very little about the other honors you have received. At the time, some media outlets described you in derogatory terms. I always assumed that authors like Modiano or Le Clézio would never face any issues with recognition.

 

Annie Ernaux: I am a woman. Yet we must admit, women are recognized far less than men in society, in every field. Literature, and every other domain, is dominated by men.

 

Whether it's literary critics, jury members, editors, or top executives, men make up a significantly higher proportion. These men really are big talkers... You know, I've been verbally attacked before. Even great writers like Colette and Beauvoir couldn't escape sexist insults. In the 1990s, I endured a lot (of abuse), especially regarding *Passion Simple*, *Shame*, and *Journal du dehors*.

 

Simonneta Greggio: What did they criticize you for?

 

Annie Ernaux: It's complicated. I never made any concessions to this world that looked down on me. Not a single one. I even refused some awards. Texts like *A Man's Place* had a huge impact, and although many considered it popular literature from the 1930s, I ultimately won the Prix Renaudot with it—something that made many people deeply resentful.




"I have experienced social contempt and gender-based discrimination."


When I published *Passion Simple*, they attacked me relentlessly. I was called "Madame Ovary." A flood of mocking comments poured in, calling me a union writer who wrote books for typists.

 

I was subjected to social contempt—and gender-based discrimination. When *The Years* came out, things seemed to improve, but it was only an illusion; the mockery quickly returned. *Look at the Lights, My Love* made the audience curious about me again as a female writer: What is she up to now, trying to write literature in supermarkets? Telling stories about strangers she's never even met? But soon, I was once again dubbed the "Mother of the RER."


*Photo of Annie Ernaux by Charlotte Abramow for *Marie Claire* magazine*


Women

Simonneta Greggio: These criticisms didn't anger you—they gave you even more energy.

 

Annie Ernaux: Indeed, I wasn't angry. I tried to stay rational and analyze these things. I continued to live well, and I resist: I resist racism, sexism, and all forms of injustice. Resistance—that's a word I love. I cannot stay silent. On one of my notebooks, I wrote this sentence: "I will write to give voice to my race."

 

Simonneta Greggio: What race do you mean?

 

Annie Ernaux: This phrase comes from Rimbaud, who said: "I will always belong to the inferior race." I appropriated this term. "Race," in fact, refers to my social origin.

 

Simonneta Greggio: Do you really believe writing can bring about change, can shake anything at all?

 

Annie Ernaux: Some films have been adapted from my works, and my books are used as research material in universities. When people read *La femme gelée* (*The Frozen Woman*), their perspectives are shaken, even though it's a book from 40 years ago. It tells the story of a couple energized by ideals of gender equality, yet constrained by traditional social norms. So, I believe writing can change something. Words have immense power—sometimes, they can even destroy.

 

Nelly Kaprièlien: What do you think about the American #MeToo movement against sexual harassment?

 

Annie Ernaux: It revived the feminism of the 1970s. In the 1990s, feminism experienced a complete regression. Of course, there have been abuses that deviate from the movement's original purpose, but it's strange—we always demand that the oppressed be perfect. Just like in the Yellow Vests movement, the slightest misstep—admittedly, they did make such mistakes—immediately turns into: "They're all anti-Semites," or "They're all Islamophobic."




"Today, resistance comes from those who suffer profound injustice, from those who have no future.

I believe this will continue. And I will keep supporting these people."


Things

Is there anything you'd like to say to men now?

Women achieving equal status with men is entirely beneficial to men, with no downside.

 

And to women?

Never settle.

 

What will happen between men and women?

Between men and women, struggle is life, and life is struggle.

 

What dreams of yours have already come true?

In the past, dreams didn't exist.

 

Is there, within all this, a kind of hope? What is it?

I call it the glimmer before dawn.

 

Annie, what is freedom to you today?

What I'll say might make you unhappy.

 

We're not here for that.

I only need to write. There's no need for me to answer all these questions.
