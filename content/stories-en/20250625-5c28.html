On June 3, 2025, the "Listening to the Silent: Jingjing's Twenty-Year Journey in Domestic Violence Interpreting" panel discussion, hosted by the Asian Women Development Plan International (AWDPI) Australia National Office, was successfully held in Melbourne. The event was moderated by Cindy Gui Jiaze, Cindy Gui Jiaze, founding initiator and head of AWDPI Australia, featuring an in-depth dialogue with Jingjing Lipski, an outstanding interpreter with 21 years of experience in the anti-domestic violence field, who has served community organizations and the justice system at all levels and is the only Chinese recipient of the Liberty Victoria Voltaire Awards. Jingjing Lipski The event attracted over 60 online and offline participants, focusing together on the role of Asian women interpreters as critical bridges and exploring pathways for domestic violence survivors to seek help and self-rescue.




The role of an interpreter goes far beyond language conversion: they are witnesses to violent incidents, communicators across cultural divides, and interpreters of laws and regulations. The panel delved into Jingjing's motivations for entering the interpreting field, her professional balance between "neutrality" and "empathy," her profound understanding of the definition and implications of "domestic violence," and the challenges of 'invisibility' faced by Asian women in Australian society. Her work not only concerns survivors' access to safety and healing but also outlines a future direction for Asian women's strength and professional development.



|Challenges, Value, and Inner Resilience in the Interpreting Journey|


Jingjing's 21-year career in domestic violence interpreting began with a search for life meaning and the awakening of her innate talents. Despite facing challenges with dyscalculia, she demonstrated exceptional aptitude in language. Even with a stable life, she still felt "a lack of passion that could ignite her soul." This desire for change found a powerful response when she encountered an Interpreting & Translation course—"my passion was instantly ignited." At that moment, she clearly found her life's purpose: to fulfill the mission of "being willing to serve as a bridge for others to communicate." This mission, combining her linguistic talent with a deep desire to help others, became the driving force behind her commitment to the highly challenging field of domestic violence survivor services—a commitment she has upheld to this day.


However, the work of a domestic violence interpreter is far from simple language transfer. Jingjing expressed its core value and the accompanying significant challenges: they are key bridges in handling traumatic content, interpreting culturally sensitive concepts, and maintaining communication when parties are emotionally overwhelmed. This requires interpreters to make a delicate balance between the professional principle of "faithfully conveying information" and empathetically transmitting the "speaker's expressions and tone." Jingjing noted: "Empathy is an essential quality in interpreting, but professional competence is equally important. No matter how intense the parties' views or emotions, we must not let them affect our professional attitude." This calm professionalism is especially crucial in the face of humanity's darkest moments—whether interpreting for defendants in major criminal cases (murder, rape, drug trafficking) or conveying to a mother the news of her child's death in a medical setting. The interpreter's role is "always only that of an interpreter," requiring restraint of personal emotions and strict adherence to professional boundaries. When survivors remain silent, Jingjing's professional boundaries are equally clear: "We are not responsible for what they say, how much they are willing to say, or how it may affect the progress of a case—we are only responsible for accurately conveying the words they do speak." If the person chooses not to speak, the only thing she can do is faithfully convey that silence.


Long-term exposure to high demands for empathy and extreme emotional environments poses a serious threat to interpreters' mental health. Jingjing mentioned that in her work, she "often faces the darkest aspects of human nature," and this sustained exposure can easily lead to "vicarious trauma." When sharing how to build psychological resilience and protect inner stability, she outlined her coping strategies. Based on her invaluable experience, we summarize the following four self-care methods, hoping to provide practical psychological protection pathways for all workers in trauma support, social services, and related fields.


Self-care

■  Self-awareness and education are the starting point of protection.:  "Be aware of how you emotionally respond in different situations and have sufficient understanding of your psychological reactions—this requires self-education." Proactively learn about trauma response mechanisms. 

■  Daily habits are the foundation of resilience.: Nourish the mind and body through "reading, exercise, and socializing," actively replenish psychological energy, build daily stress buffers, and actively maintain psychological flexibility.

■External support is a professional safeguard.: When necessary, "communicate with professionals to complete a process of self-reflection," receive effective psychological counseling, and never bear the burden alone. 

■  Clear role definition is the source of strength.:  "Whenever anything happens, you need a decompression process to protect yourself." The core lies in a clear understanding of one's role: "Understand that our mission is not to save anyone, but to build a bridge for communication. Only with inner stability can we walk further on this path." 


This clear awareness of mission and emphasis on self-protection form the inner support that enables her to continue on this difficult journey.


|Australia's Definition of Domestic Violence and Multidimensional Forms of Abuse|


During the panel, Jingjing Lipski shared with the audience the broad and culturally transformative definition of domestic violence in Australia. According to Australia's Family Violence Protection Act, domestic violence is not limited to physical conflict but encompasses "any behavior that is violent, threatening, or controlling, or intended to make someone feel fear or unsafe," a definition that manifests in five specific dimensions:


■  physical violence (e.g., hitting, shoving),

■  psychological/emotional abuse (mental manipulation, degradation, humiliation),

■  verbal violence (public shaming, threats, intimidation),

■  economic control (restricting employment, depriving financial autonomy),

■  and sexual violence (marital rape, forced sexual acts).

Jingjing noted that many Asian women regard "a husband publicly scolding his wife for ordering a large coffee" or "prohibiting her from seeing friends in the name of cultural tradition" as normal—even many survivors say they "are used to it"—yet these are precisely the behaviors explicitly defined and prohibited as domestic violence under Australian law! Cultural constraints often trap survivors in a crisis of agency, asking "What would I do without him?"—while the awakening of younger generations is driving a break in silence. When we remove violence from the label of "private family matters," every act of speaking out infuses power into rewriting survivors' fates.


Jingjing also shared four deeply moving real-life cases that revealed the complex nature of domestic violence:


Swipe up for more

Case One: The Cycle of Violence in an Immigrant Family and Institutional Intervention

A migrant couple's conflicts escalated due to language and work stress. The husband, following old habits, assaulted his wife. After she reported it, police immediately activated a "Family Violence Intervention Order" (requiring the abuser to leave the home). But the husband blatantly violated the order, threatening the entire family with a knife. The child grabbed the knife and accidentally caused the mother's finger to fracture. More tragically, in her subsequent relationship, the woman was sexually assaulted by a new partner. These fragments made the audience realize that the prevalence of domestic violence is far higher than imagined.

Case Two: Hidden Violence Behind the Facade of High Education and Legal Equality

A university professor subjected his Chinese Australian wife to years of sexual violence and threatened her with deportation. With friends' support, she bravely reported the abuse, and the perpetrator was ultimately punished by law. In a similar case, a company CEO attempted to use large sums of money to avoid conviction on the grounds that "marital rape doesn't exist," but was unanimously found guilty and imprisoned by the jury. Jingjing noted: "Without these women's courageous voices, such violent acts would certainly continue."

Case Three: Behavioral Correction Breaking Intergenerational Transmission

A Chinese Australian woman reported her husband for replicating the violent patterns from his family of origin. When he snatched her phone to prevent her from calling for help, she fought to escape and sought refuge at a neighbor's home. The court issued a restraining order and mandated the man to attend anger management programs. Encouragingly, after deep reflection, the man significantly improved the marital relationship. Appropriate judicial intervention is a crucial step in breaking the intergenerational transmission of violence.

Case Four: Intimate Relationship ≠ Ownership

After a university student couple broke up, the boyfriend, driven by jealousy, assaulted his ex-girlfriend in public and forcibly dragged her into his car. She tried to escape at a gas station but was pulled back by her hair—caught entirely on surveillance. In court, the boyfriend questioned: "She cheated, so what if I hit her?" The judge immediately rebuked: "She has the freedom to be with whomever she chooses, but your act of violence is a crime."




Collective Action Builds Strength, Together Building a Violence-Free Society


Jingjing Lipski's 21 years of invaluable experience reflect the multidimensional challenges of Australia's anti-domestic violence efforts: from the broad legal definition of domestic violence (covering physical, psychological, economic, sexual, and other forms) to the cultural dilemmas faced by Asian communities (such as pressure to "endure in silence" and experiences of "invisibility"), and the need for coordination among judicial, medical, and community services.


The cruelty of domestic violence lies in its universality and concealment—it exists across different social strata, including immigrant families, highly educated groups, and young couples, while language barriers and cultural differences often leave survivors isolated and helpless. The power to resist comes from sustained action. Countless frontline workers like Jingjing—interpreters, social workers, legal professionals, and community organizations—use their expertise and perseverance to convey the voices of the voiceless and build lifelines; their work is a crucial link in dismantling chains of oppression.
