Introduction:

Many single women have had the desire to buy a house and live together with female friends. To some extent, this is not only about having a place to shelter from wind and rain, but also about psychologically constructing a space exclusively for women, aiming to achieve safety and comfort. Yet upon stepping into the reality filled with "patriarchal vibes," we can't help but ask: In what way should an ideal "female utopia" exist in order to avoid the endless cycle of self-justification?


1

A refuge beyond the family?


The novella *Ark* explores precisely this kind of doubt. The story follows the lives of three independent middle-aged women after they buy a house together. Former classmates in their youth, they choose to live together in midlife. As they spend time together, they discover that the beautiful life they once imagined remains only in fantasy, while the practical issues they face continue to cause anxiety and distress. Even when they collectively devise solutions, these only ease the situation temporarily, without fundamentally changing their reality.


They face various problems and confusions. Even mutual understanding among sisters and owning their own home cannot shield them from inner self-doubt. They realize that as long as they wish to survive well in this society, they will be constrained, bound, and even judged by the moral standards prescribed by patriarchy. Even within their own space, their female identity does not permit them to express intense emotions freely—they are only allowed to suppress their desires.



But doing so is undoubtedly difficult. The challenges facing modern women go beyond workplace pressures and discrimination. When it comes to family and career, women are often the ones expected to make compromises and concessions. In the story, one woman enters marriage and then leaves it; another is entangled in her marriage due to material interests; yet another is labeled selfish simply for prioritizing care for her family of origin... They want to escape such relationships, yet find themselves struggling and repenting throughout their lives in exile. For example, Liu Quan wants to lash out and cry for no reason; Liang Qian constantly worries about her father; Cao Jinghua faces worsening back pain and the looming reality that she may no longer be able to care for herself in the future.


Maternal penalty is especially prominent in this novella. Cao Jinghua terminates her pregnancy against her husband's wishes due to financial difficulties; Liu Quan divorces and lacks the conditions to raise a child; Liang Qian is fully absorbed in her work and lacks sufficient time. It seems they have done everything society deems unfit for a mother. They choose themselves over their children, yet end up equally destitute. They support and affirm each other, yet also feel utterly defeated when no one has the energy to do housework. This is both the fragmentation caused by switching between identity and self-agency, and patriarchy's mockery of professional women.


2

The omnipresent patriarchal system


Although the three women strive to build a relatively ideal female space, just as the novel's title suggests, this so-called "ark" is not a utopia. It flows entirely within the broader social environment. Thus, we also see the external gaze upon this group of women, and the amplified criticism directed at women living alone.


Neighbors gossip about them, even commenting on their pet cat; Liu Quan's boss lusts after her, making her work life uneasy; Bai Fushan can freely enter their home simply because Liang Qian, who bought the house, is his wife in the midst of a divorce. He keeps Liang Qian tied to him for the sake of his own future, while Liang Qian refuses to divorce Bai Fushan to save face for her father and his comrades... Even acts that appear to be women helping women, such as Liang Qian securing a high-level position for Liu Quan, ultimately rely on connections through her father.



The seemingly trivial and taken-for-granted aspects of daily life are all deeply influenced by patriarchy. Within the space, there is questioning and struggle over identity and self-determination; outside the space, there is judgment and evaluation by mainstream values. Under such conditions, how could women not doubt themselves and feel anxious?


3

The elusive sense of security


Although the novella was published in the early 1980s, it remains meaningful and thought-provoking today. Now, feminism is widespread, and all-female communities online are everywhere. We can't help but reflect: Is it enough to simply establish all-female spaces on a physical level to achieve safety? Historical experience tells us, if a person lacks independent and confident self-awareness, they will inevitably become a plaything of power. Even if one becomes a sole female ruler in an all-female space, it is difficult to escape the shackles of power—ultimately, it may just become another form of PUA, using the moral codes of that power to exploit oneself and others.


Ultimately, the lack of female security stems from deep-seated societal misogyny. From childhood, women are taught that "safety comes from having a man," rather than from cultivating inner and physical strength. In contrast, men's behaviors—regardless of quality—are imbued with an incurable predatory nature, reinforced by the environment. Faced with such disadvantages, we seem to constantly rationalize our choices, sinking deeper into the cycle of self-justification. Rather than endlessly proving ourselves, what we need more is to build new confidence and goals to dismantle the barriers of outdated beliefs. At the same time, continuous learning and growth will also enhance our sense of self-worth. We must believe that besides the trope of hero rescuing beauty, there are other possible endings.



Creating all-female spaces is not the ultimate solution; what truly matters is creating a supportive and accepting environment for women's independent existence. The novella ends with hope placed on the next generation of men, but rather than that, we might as well place our hope in women themselves. After all, before the environment changes, it is women's courage and self-coherence that are key to solving the problem. Only through this can we advocate and speak out with greater confidence. Although within the patriarchal power structure, those of us who hope for all-female spaces cannot easily avoid self-justification, and persistence is especially difficult and prone to setbacks, the good news is that we have already begun to choose, try, and move forward.
