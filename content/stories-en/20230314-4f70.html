 Introduction:

"She's my wife—why can't I hit her!"

"……My husband wants to kill me. I fled with my child and don't know where else to go……"

These are the words of a domestic violence survivor, and also a real case intervened by the New York Garden of Hope Women and Children's Service Center.

The New York Garden of Hope Women and Children's Service Center, (hereinafter referred to as "Garden of Hope"), was established in 2004 through joint efforts by the Taiwan-based Garden of Hope Foundation and the Chinese women's community in New York.

Over the past eighteen years, Garden of Hope has primarily served victims of domestic violence, child abuse, sexual assault, human trafficking, and anti-Asian violence. Guided by the spirit of compassion central to nonprofit organizations, Garden of Hope is committed to providing care and services to those in need. Through its work, Garden of Hope strives to help survivors restore and rebuild their physical and mental well-being.

In July 2011, Garden of Hope established the first shelter offering Chinese-language services—"Garden of Hope (Garden of Hope Shelter)." To date, it has provided safe refuge to hundreds of women and children who became homeless due to domestic violence or abuse. Garden of Hope offers victims a range of free services, including crisis intervention, counseling, shelter, support groups, legal education, case management, and programs for children and youth. Over the past decade, more than 2,000 women and children have benefited from these services. Meanwhile, Garden of Hope continues to expand its influence within the community, helping victims escape violence and achieve full physical and psychological recovery. By instilling hope into the lives of victimized women, the organization helps rebuild their self-esteem and independence, empowering them to actively confront illegal acts such as domestic violence, sexual assault, human trafficking, and anti-Asian violence.

Avoice is honored to invite Ms. Yuanfen Liu, Executive Director of Garden of Hope, to discuss the current state and future of protection for Chinese American women's rights in the United States.


"After the pandemic, reports of domestic violence tripled."


According to Ms. Liu, since the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic in early 2020 in the United States, the total number of people served by Garden of Hope has remained largely unchanged, but cases of domestic violence have increased threefold compared to the same period before the pandemic—an alarming figure that evokes deep concern. Conflicts within each household vary, but the prolonged impact of the pandemic has caused internal and external factors to converge, resulting in more severe harm to victims.

In the early stages of the pandemic, family members living abroad often quarreled over how to distribute and use government financial aid; children attending online classes at home, both spouses working remotely, and fear of the pandemic's trajectory—all negative effects of the pandemic—caused tensions to erupt easily. Many individuals who previously required professional psychological treatment and regular medication were unable to receive timely care due to service disruptions, triggering outbreaks of family conflict.

During the pandemic, the efficiency of legal services significantly declined. Ms. Liu explained that although perpetrators in domestic violence cases are eventually arrested, state laws differ in the duration of protection orders for victims. If the police do not promptly arrest the perpetrator, victims cannot obtain protection orders and continue to live in fear and helplessness. Meanwhile, due to the pandemic, courts have not fully reopened, and online courts are mostly used for urgent cases, causing many cases to face long waiting periods. Some waiting periods lasting up to a year have intensified family conflicts and negative emotions.


"Family harmony brings prosperity in all things"—the culture of endurance among Chinese women


A report jointly released in March 2021 by the World Health Organization and UN Women found that one in three women worldwide will experience physical or sexual violence from an intimate partner or sexual violence from a non-partner during their lifetime. Garden of Hope's investigations reveal that the Chinese community is no exception. In cases assisted by Garden of Hope, many perpetrators view domestic violence merely as a private family matter, believing that harm to an individual should not be exposed outside the family and requires no external intervention. No matter how severe the violence becomes, they believe it can be resolved within the "closed" space of the "family." Perpetrators are not unaware of societal norms or even laws against domestic violence, yet they allow themselves to use violence against their partners as casually as someone might litter on the street. Under the guise of "family," victims' individual rights are stripped away. As a result, perpetrators act without fear, and domestic violence escalates.

Ms. Liu Yuanfen noted that due to traditional beliefs such as "family shame should not be publicized," "family harmony brings prosperity in all things," and "don't call the police unless it's a major incident," many Chinese women deliberately conceal their abuse even when experiencing domestic violence, meaning the actual number of domestic violence incidents is far higher than the number reported.

New York police handle at least 4,000 domestic violence cases annually, yet many women in Chinese communities, due to language barriers, have no access to reporting channels. Currently, New York has over a thousand shelter beds, but only a dozen offer Chinese-language support services, and shelters specifically serving Chinese women are extremely rare.

Chinese women often have low awareness of domestic violence and only recognize its existence after intervention by third-party protection agencies. Abused women rarely seek help proactively unless the situation becomes irreversible—such as when their lives are threatened or the violence has caused serious physical injury. Ms. Liu emphasized that each case is different; staying in the family does not mean these women do not want to leave or accept abuse, but rather that leaving represents an even greater risk for them. If they truly decide to leave with their children, figuring out how to survive becomes a major challenge. In reality, most mothers hope to gain custody of their children, but the reality is that many mothers are forced to give up custody due to lack of financial ability to raise children independently.


Gender economic inequality increases the burden on women


In New York, it is extremely difficult for single mothers to raise children alone. The pandemic has reduced women's opportunities to earn equal income, further widening the gender equality gap. According to 2021 data, the U.S. female labor force participation rate has dropped below 56%, with at least 2.5 million women unemployed. Many mothers experiencing domestic violence have lost their income, and even fathers responsible for child support have lost their jobs. After falling into economic crisis, women have no place to live and can only temporarily stay with their children in emergency shelters provided by the Red Cross, while continuing to endure threats and intimidation from the abuser.

School and daycare closures or early dismissals mean families with children require someone to provide care, and women are more likely than men to voluntarily sacrifice their jobs and take on greater caregiving responsibilities. Many women have given up their careers for their families, and since childcare workers are predominantly female, the suspension of childcare services has also forced them into temporary unemployment.

For single mothers, unless they can work from home or have flexible schedules, there is no one at home to care for children dismissed early from school. If they hire daycare or babysitters, their meager income cannot afford the cost, placing them in a dilemma where they cannot choose between work and parenting.

Garden of Hope helps women find employment, opening up a new world for these single mothers. The organization helps them find relatively simple jobs with flexible schedules, allowing mothers to balance childcare and work more easily. It also covers their tuition and certification exam fees, helping them restore and rebuild their lives. Many women who have received help from Garden of Hope also support each other, and this kind, empowering spirit and practice of women helping women should indeed be encouraged and promoted. .


Proactively using legal means to protect rights: a long and arduous journey


Regarding the legal aid provided by Garden of Hope, Ms. Liu stated that a lack of legal knowledge may cause victims to expend more effort in vain, or even achieve the opposite of their intended outcome.

For example, 70% of victims who call Garden of Hope hope the organization can help dismiss the case, giving their abusive husband another chance without affecting his green card application. However, U.S. law stipulates that victims can only serve as witnesses and cannot drop the case. Being a witness is highly stressful for some women, and given the complexity of legal procedures, many victims already suffering psychological trauma become overwhelmed and choose to continue barely surviving in violent environments, clinging to the idea that "enduring hardship brings temporary peace."

Regarding child custody, mainstream American culture holds that "if a mother cannot protect her child, she is not qualified to have custody." After a court issues a protection order to a victim, if the victim still allows the abuser to have contact with the child and this is discovered by a caseworker from the Administration for Children's Services (ACS), the victim will be deemed incapable of protecting the child, and the child will be taken away.

The attitudes of prosecutors and judges are also crucial. If a prosecutor believes a case is not worth pursuing, they typically will not proceed. New York has five boroughs, and judges in each borough have different attitudes—some are more empathetic toward new immigrants, while others lack understanding and may ask, "You were beaten like this—why did you still stay at home?" and suggest the victim see a psychologist. These cultural differences significantly impact trial outcomes.

Every year, the number of domestic violence cases in the New York area reaches as high as 270,000, with at least 5,000 involving Chinese women. Most victims require assistance related to criminal courts and compensation for criminal cases. The women who come to GRC for help are generally from relatively vulnerable groups. Due to language barriers and other challenges, GRC directly assists them in reporting incidents to the police, applying for protection orders, and finding safe shelters.

GRC's volunteer legal team provides victims with various solutions to choose from, and GRC staff accompany victims throughout the process. For example, during litigation, if the perpetrator has financial resources, lawyers often request a mental health evaluation of the victim, and GRC staff also appear in court to provide support.

Surprisingly, although domestic violence exists across all social classes and backgrounds, many of the perpetrators in cases handled by GRC are themselves highly educated or well-versed in the law. When perpetrators knowingly break the law and show no remorse, family relationships continue to fall into recurring behavioral patterns, unable to escape the quagmire. 

During the interview, Ms. Liu emphasized more than once that although some individual cases are difficult to explain or intervene in legally, and laws and protection orders vary from state to state, the most important solution when facing domestic violence is to immediately seek legal consultation. Ms. Liu advises that victims with legal needs must proactively learn about local U.S. laws and available resources, understand what legal rights they have to protect themselves, and be fully informed. At the same time, to assist victims in more regions across the United States, GRC is actively collaborating with prosecutorial and investigative agencies in various states.


Healing from trauma is a long journey.


In 2021 alone, nearly 900 individuals received assistance from GRC. GRC hopes to provide a safe environment so that victims can receive timely help after experiencing trauma. Among traumatic experiences, psychological wounds are often the most difficult to heal. Ms. Liu shared that some survivors, after suffering severe psychological trauma, need to go through the process repeatedly—up to seven times—before they can truly resolve to leave the violent environment.

Recovering from trauma is a long journey. This healing process involves not only confronting fear of past violence but also dealing with anger and attachment toward the former partner, stubborn feelings of injustice, self-doubt, parenting attitudes, views on love, and more. Each challenge is like a small peak among the many mountains to be climbed on this journey of self-discovery, requiring extensive time and strong perseverance to overcome. Escaping the cycle of self-doubt is not easy. Every survivor who emerges from hardship is courageous, and these advances are not only due to GRC's support and guidance but also represent a profound act of self-redemption by the survivors themselves.

They recover from physical and emotional symptoms, regain a sense of independent identity, reclaim control over their lives, gain knowledge about abusive intimate relationships, engage in self-reflection, forgive themselves and the abuser, and prepare for future intimate relationships, learning to embrace the present and future in a healthy way.

A survivor who received help shared: "I now have more hope for my future life. The divorce case is resolved, my immigration status is settled, and my child is healthy. Everything is moving in a positive direction. It is GRC led me out of darkness and helped me see the light. I felt understood, accepted, and loved, surrounded by light within. "



Conclusion

Jointly safeguarding overseas Chinese women

As stated in GRC's promotional video,

"We believe everyone has the right to live a life of dignity, freedom, and hope. In corners beyond our daily lives, many women are living in silent despair, but we always believe in the existence of love and justice, and hope to help these women step out of darkness and see the light."

"We hope to unite our communities to oppose violence and racism. Most importantly, we want every person we serve to know: we believe you can make a difference. Join us to jointly develop mental health committee social services for Chinese immigrant communities."

The redemption from domestic violence is a complex social and psychological issue. This dialogue with the New York GRC Women and Children Care Center has deeply inspired us. Avoice also looks forward to strengthening multifaceted cooperation with the New York GRC Women and Children Care Center in the future, working together to break the vicious cycle of abuse and violence and jointly safeguarding the physical and mental safety of overseas Chinese women.
