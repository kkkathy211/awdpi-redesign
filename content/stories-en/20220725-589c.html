Since 2015, progress toward gender equality has stalled, and now COVID-19 has dealt a heavy blow to women. Consider this—


In 2015, 193 United Nations member states came together to commit to achieving 17 Sustainable Development Goals. Goal 5 focuses on gender equality, setting an ambitious target to achieve gender equality and empower women worldwide by 2030. Five years later, significant gender gaps persist globally, and evidence shows that the COVID-19 pandemic has clearly contributed to setbacks in gender equality.


Ensuring women's roles in the workplace and society is critical for economic recovery in the era of COVID-19. Will women fall further behind? As world leaders gather at the United Nations General Assembly to assess progress and envision recovery—coinciding with the 25th anniversary of the Fourth World Conference on Women and the Beijing Declaration—everyone should understand gender equality.



1


Closing global gender gaps would boost global GDP.


Gender inequality is not only an urgent moral and social issue but also a serious economic challenge. A 2015 report by the McKinsey Global Institute (MGI), *The Power of Parity: How Advancing Gender Equality Could Add $12 Trillion to Global Growth*, explored the economic potential of closing global gender gaps.


Five years ago, women accounted for 50% of the global working-age population but generated only 37% of global GDP. The study found that if countries advanced gender equality to match the best-performing nation in their region, global GDP could increase by $12 trillion by 2025. This amount is equivalent to the combined GDP of Japan, Germany, and the United Kingdom, and roughly double the GDP growth that female workers could contribute globally between 2014 and 2025. Both advanced and developing economies would see substantial gains; GDP in every region could increase by at least 8%. In a scenario where women match men's participation, additional GDP could reach $28 trillion, or 26% of annual global GDP by 2025—roughly equivalent to the combined GDP of the United States and China. As noted in point 6, the COVID-19 pandemic has introduced new urgency and risks to the economic benefits of gender equality.



2


Since 2015, progress in gender equality has been minimal;

significant gaps remain.


Even before the arrival of the COVID-19 crisis, global progress on gender gaps had stagnated since 2015. Based on MGI's analysis of 125 countries, gender equality in society and the workplace are interconnected. While for most countries, absolute scores on social equality tend to be higher than those on work equality, almost no country achieves high equality on social indicators while lagging in employment and labor markets. This suggests that both issues must be addressed simultaneously.


We aggregated 15 indicators into a Gender Parity Score (GPS), ranging from zero (no gender equality) to one (full gender equality). Over the past five years, gender gaps have persisted across all regions (Chart 1). On social gender equality, the overall score in 2019 was 0.67, up from 0.66 in 2015, with similar trends across regions. The Middle East and North Africa region saw the largest improvement in gender equality, rising from a global GPS of 0.47 in 2015 to 0.50 in 2019. However, since 2015, some regions have experienced setbacks in either workplace gender equality or social gender equality.



Not everything should be viewed through a pessimistic lens—there are bright spots even in the darkness. Maternal mortality rates are declining worldwide, and literacy rates and secondary school enrollment are rising in many countries; in the workplace, most countries are also making slow but steady progress toward parity. Since 2015, McKinsey has partnered with LeanIn.Org to study gender diversity in North American companies. The research found that today, 87% of North American companies report that gender diversity is a top priority, up from 74% in 2015.


3


Over the past two decades, while women in advanced economies have made significant progress as workers, consumers, and savers, they have also faced rising costs and insecurity.


Over the past two decades, much of this progress has been offset. Between 2000 and 2018, women filled two-thirds of the 45 million jobs created across 22 OECD countries, but many of these were part-time, less secure positions with lower wages and fewer benefits. During this period, women's part-time employment increased by 2.3 percentage points, while full-time employment rose by only 0.7 percentage points. As savers, women also face challenging prospects. One study found that while the median net wealth of women is higher overall than two decades ago, a significant gender gap remains. In Europe, the median net wealth of women is 62% that of men.



4


Women continue to face the "double shift" at home.


Beyond the workplace, women also face inequality at home. Worldwide, women perform three times as much unpaid care work (translator's note: referring to housework) as men. Like millions of women globally, millions of Chinese women live a reality where they go to work and then return home to do the majority of household chores. On average, they work nearly nine hours a day, with only about half of that time compensated. Compared to men, Chinese women work almost a full day longer per week on average; in countries like India, it is nearly ten times as much. This phenomenon is not limited to developing economies; in advanced economies, women also work a double shift. For example, in the United States, women do nearly twice as much housework as men; even though 43% of these women are the primary breadwinners in their households, 43% still bear all or most of the household labor, compared to only 12% of men.



5


Women face growing challenges from increasing automation.


Rising automation increases the challenges women face in the workplace. MGI research finds that the proportion of women whose jobs are at risk of being replaced by machines is roughly the same as for men: in the next decade, as many as one in four women may need to change occupations. By 2030, globally, between 40 million and 160 million women may need to transition across occupations.


Women face unique challenges because long-standing barriers make it harder for them to adapt to future jobs. Both women and men need to develop skills, flexibility for labor market transitions, and technical knowledge required by automated systems. Unfortunately, women face long-standing (and widespread) structural and social barriers that may leave them with little time to learn new skills or search for jobs, as they spend more time on household work than men—and may also face financial constraints, lack professional networks, and lack sponsors. In addition, compared to men, women have less access to digital technologies and lower participation in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) fields. If women make these transitions, they can find more productive and better-paid jobs; if not, they may face widening wage gaps or even exit the labor force entirely.



6


Women's challenges are now greater because they are experiencing the economic impacts of COVID-19.


Although early evidence suggests that COVID-19 is more fatal for men—male death rates in countries such as China, Italy, and South Korea are nearly twice those of women—women have borne the brunt of the economic fallout. Regarding employment, our research found that women's jobs were 1.8 times more vulnerable than men's during the crisis. As of May 2020, women made up 39% of global employment but accounted for 54% of total job losses—partly because women are overrepresented in industries hit hardest by COVID-19 (Figure 3); another factor is that with childcare centers and schools closed, more domestic responsibilities such as childcare have fallen to women. For example, according to one study, in the United States, women's time spent on household work increased by 1.5 to 2 hours.


The pandemic may also have consequences beyond economics for women—reports suggest that intimate partner violence against women may have increased during lockdowns. If no action is taken to address the gender setbacks caused by COVID-19, global GDP growth by 2030 could be $1 trillion lower. If increased childcare burdens, slower economic recovery, reduced spending on services such as education, and women being forced to permanently leave the labor force are factored in, the impact on growth could be even greater. However, if action is taken to advance gender equality, global GDP in 2030 could be $12 trillion higher; this represents a potential of $13 trillion, meaning a $1 trillion shortfall in global GDP by 2030.

7


We can make progress on some gender equality issues,

but not others.


Even if policymakers and businesses manage to recover from the COVID-19 crisis, there is no guarantee that economic recovery will help revive gender equality.


In MGI's 2015 parity report, gender action would face the greatest resistance where inequality is most concentrated. Whether women live in advanced or emerging economies, gender inequality remains severe: constrained economic potential (including women's participation in leadership and employment), time spent on household work, lack of legal rights and political representation, and violence against women. Today, globally, for every 100 men in leadership roles, there are only 37 women; one-third of women worldwide—including in developed countries such as the United States—have experienced violence from an intimate partner at some point in their lives. Economic growth alone is not enough to guarantee progress; in contrast, in some regions, economic growth can help advance gender equality. In areas facing low labor force participation in quality jobs (in South Asia, the Middle East and North Africa, and sub-Saharan Africa), poor maternal and reproductive health (in sub-Saharan Africa), unequal education (in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa), financial and digital exclusion (in South Asia, the Middle East and North Africa, and sub-Saharan Africa), and girl child vulnerability (in China and South Asia), economic growth can contribute to improvement and advancement. For example, increases in per capita GDP in Africa can provide more healthcare services, thereby reducing maternal mortality; as countries' living standards rise, girls' education levels also improve.


8

Gender and racial diversity set companies apart


Promoting gender equality is not only an opportunity for nations but also benefits companies. A 2020 McKinsey study analyzing data from over 1,000 large companies across 15 countries found that companies in the top quartile for gender diversity were 25% more likely to have above-average profitability than the average; companies in the top 25% for ethnic and cultural diversity were 36% more likely to outperform in profitability. Among companies that perform best in both profitability and diversity, there are more women in frontline roles than in executive teams.


Given the current pandemic, as companies focus on economic recovery, inclusivity and diversity may face significant risks of being deprioritized—a mistake that could harm performance and hinder long-term efforts.



9


There are leaks in women's corporate advancement pipeline


Over the years, research by LeanIn.Org has found that companies in North America have made some progress in advancing women. The 2019 Women in the Workplace report found that 48% of entry-level employees were women, up from 45% in 2015. Women made up 21% of senior management, up from 17% in 2015. However, as these figures show, women remain underrepresented at every level of organizations, with the biggest barrier for women on the corporate ladder occurring at the first step up to manager—a "broken rung." For every 100 men hired or promoted to manager, only 72 women are, and only 58 Black women. If the differences in promotion rates over five years are added up, this amounts to one million fewer women in leadership roles.


Women of color are especially underrepresented in the North American workforce, and their decline is the steepest. According to our 2019 Women in the Workplace study, while 18% of entry-level positions in North America are held by women of color, they make up only 4% of senior management. These outcomes reflect the daily experiences of women of color in the workforce—56% of Black women say they have equal growth opportunities as their peers, compared to 69% of white women; women of color also experience more workplace "microaggressions." For example, 40% of Black women and 30% of Asian women say they need to prove their competence more than others, compared to 28% of white women and 14% of white men.



Similar trends exist around the world. For example, across the Asia-Pacific region, only one woman holds a leadership position for every four men; in some East Asian countries, only 12 to 20 women hold leadership roles for every 100 men.


10


All stakeholders need to work together,

to sustain and accelerate progress toward gender equality


Considering the era of automation and the impact of COVID-19, women face new challenges on top of existing ones. The only way to renew momentum for Sustainable Development Goal 5 is for stakeholders to work together to comprehensively address the complex issue of gender inequality. As governments develop plans and companies restart strategies, more data is now needed to ensure transparency.


Governments can use laws to drive change across broader areas. For example, they can eliminate legal barriers that restrict women's work (such as regulations banning women from night shifts) and enforce laws protecting women from violence. Policy options are wide-ranging, from financial support for women such as cash transfers, to tax regulations, childcare programs, and incorporating gender considerations into the construction and design of public infrastructure. Non-governmental organizations can also play an important role, for instance, by shaping attitudes and social norms. Many governments and NGOs have established effective partnerships with companies to rapidly scale new solutions.


Companies can take action in multiple ways, starting with their own employees, to attract, retain, and promote women. Leaders need to advocate for gender diversity, ensure fair hiring and promotion practices, and foster a culture of inclusion and respect. In the era of COVID-19, many companies are exploring family-friendly policies, including flexible and part-time work, to support workers facing increased childcare responsibilities, and rethinking performance evaluations and promotions. Companies can use this moment to design and implement policies and practices that can sustainably support women in the long term.


Individuals also need to contribute, from advocating for themselves to helping others through sponsorship and mentorship. At work, one can actively support talented women and speak up when witnessing unconscious bias or microaggressions; in personal life, one can examine one's own biases, whether conscious or unconscious; if one has a family, one can avoid gender-limiting children; if one invests, one can support companies that advance gender equality in ways aligned with one's values.


Five years after the launch of the Sustainable Development Goals, progress on gender equality has been at best minimal, and now faces new challenges. With only 10 years left to achieve Goal 5, we need to accelerate further. Achieving equality for half the world's population is a global imperative, but complex global competition and the pandemic may undermine progress.



Creating more opportunities for women and the next generation is both an aspiration and a very realistic goal that can drive the global economy and contribute to a fairer society—we need to achieve it together.
