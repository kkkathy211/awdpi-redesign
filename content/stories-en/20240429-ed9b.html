Abstract:


According to UN Women, even before the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, one in three women globally had experienced physical or sexual violence, mostly by an intimate partner [1]. Since the pandemic began, domestic violence has sharply increased, becoming one of the "hidden costs" of COVID-19. A medical journal in the United States reported that domestic violence cases worldwide rose by 25%–33% in 2020 [2]. Winnie experienced her partner's first physical assault during the pandemic lockdown.


Looking back at the entire incident, Winnie appears to fit the definition of what we call a "perfect victim": highly educated, financially independent, never having confronted her partner directly, and never using extreme language to attack him. Yet her story reminds us once again: Violence does not require a reason. Anyone can become a victim.



1

Invisible harm: domestic violence you may not understand


What is domestic violence? Most people recognize the most obvious form—physical assault. But often, physical abuse is accompanied by a series of harder-to-identify, more varied forms of abuse. These violent behaviors work together to firmly establish a pattern of intimidation and control by the abuser within the relationship.



Winnie and her partner both lived in the United States. Before marriage, Winnie clearly told her boyfriend that household chores should be fairly shared, all decisions must be made jointly, and she would manage all household finances herself. He agreed to all these terms and appeared gentle and considerate.


During the COVID-19 pandemic, Winnie was pursuing her PhD and became pregnant. In early pregnancy, her partner still cared for her as he had during their dating period. However, signs of violence began to emerge in the middle of her pregnancy. As a PhD student with almost no income—whose research project had also been delayed due to the pandemic—Winnie received no support or encouragement from her husband. Instead, he mocked and belittled her at home, repeatedly questioning, "When will you finally graduate?"


Unable to bear the atmosphere at home, Winnie chose to drive to campus every day while pregnant to conduct research, eating all her meals there and minimizing contact with her husband. But verbal abuse escalated. One day, when she asked her husband to move the car from the garage, he suddenly exploded, accusing Winnie of failing to manage her own affairs and irrationally lashing out emotionally.


In domestic violence, abusers often use multiple forms of violence to create and reinforce their abusive system. The forms of abuse by Winnie's husband further evolved after they decided to move to the western United States.


2

Lonely in a foreign land: escalating violence and courageous help-seeking


Shortly after the baby was born, Winnie's husband had to relocate from the East Coast to the West Coast for work. Winnie was initially hesitant—her PhD program was on the East Coast, and all her life experiences and social connections were there. Aside from the immense difficulty of moving across much of the United States with an infant in a short time, Winnie would have to rebuild her life from scratch while facing academic pressure. After much struggle, she still moved to Seattle with her husband and mother-in-law.


Thus, Winnie moved to an unfamiliar city. Although this decision was not entirely forced by her husband, the move silently created another condition for the escalation of domestic violence: Winnie became physically isolated from friends, family, and work.



After the move, her husband's verbal abuse became increasingly overt—escalating from frequent use of profanity to furious outbursts over minor issues. Eventually, when the child was under one year old, the verbal abuse turned into physical violence.


A few months after moving to the western U.S., one night the baby suddenly cried. Normally, her husband was responsible for nighttime care, but no matter how Winnie tried, she couldn't wake him. Considering the baby might be hungry from missing a solid food meal during the day, she breastfed him. At that moment, with no warning, her husband suddenly exploded, accusing her of not being allowed to feed the baby, and slapped her twice. "I was hit so hard I had slight tinnitus. I went to the bathroom to calm down for a while, and realized things couldn't go on like this—I had to keep evidence." She turned on her phone's recording function and confronted her husband about why he hit her; he admitted it. At that moment, Winnie didn't realize this recording would later prove crucial—during the court hearing, her husband denied ever committing domestic violence against her.


Winnie first decided to call the police after her husband suddenly flew into a rage and threw something that hit her. Facing the officers, her husband tried to dismiss it as a "family dispute." At the time, Winnie had no understanding of the seriousness of domestic violence: "I was still quite soft-hearted, thinking giving him a warning would be enough."


Her leniency, however, did not lead to any change from him. When their child was 13 months old, her husband forced her into sexual intercourse, causing her to become pregnant again. The unexpected arrival of a second child pushed Winnie's mental and physical health to a breaking point, and became the turning point for her decision to seek help. She began communicating more with the outside world, joined a local Chinese mothers' group, and gradually rebuilt basic social connections.



After contacting the Avoice Overseas Chinese Women Protection Project online, staff provided her with psychological counseling, and a local Chinese community offered her free legal consultation. All these help-seeking efforts were done secretly during daytime walks with her baby. After counseling, Winnie decided to quickly proceed with an abortion, apply for a domestic violence protection order, and file for divorce.


3

You are not alone: legal protection and social support


Many people may wonder: how exactly is a domestic violence protection order enforced? After Winnie completed the paperwork at court, the judge issued her a 14-day temporary protection order, valid until the court hearing 14 days later. During this period, her husband was evicted from their home and prohibited from contacting her within 1,000 feet. Police also explained: if her husband wanted to retrieve belongings, he must be accompanied by an officer; child visitation must occur at a designated police station with a third party present for handover.


The protection order not only prohibits physical contact but also remote harassment via messages—any violation would escalate the case from civil to criminal, and Winnie's husband could face imprisonment.



With the temporary protection order in place, Winnie contacted a lawyer within a week to handle both the domestic violence and divorce cases. Initially, her husband kept avoiding service and refused to accept legal documents, until the court enforced it. In fact, the court deems the husband to have received the documents on the day they are mailed; no response within 30 days means acceptance of all divorce terms.


It is now the second year of the protection order's enforcement. Winnie's divorce case is still ongoing. Her lawyer told her they will eventually apply to the court for a permanent protection order.


Reflecting on her past experiences, Winnie considers herself lucky to have recognized the signs of domestic violence early and preserved evidence in time. Although the shadow of domestic violence has not disappeared, and she still lacks confidence in men and intimate relationships, she has adjusted her mindset. As Winnie said: "I am not a victim—I am a survivor."



"Knowing how domestic violence is defined—this first step of awakening is the most critical." Winnie has now completed her PhD, found a job, and returned to a normal life. She has also become a volunteer for Avoice in the United States, bravely sharing her story.


Through this period of fighting for herself and rebuilding her life, Winnie wants to tell more partners who may be suffering similarly:


There are five types of family abuse: emotional, psychological, financial or economic, physical, and sexual abuse [3]. As written on the UN website: "No one deserves to be abused. The abuse is not your fault. You are not alone."


Image source: official UN website


How can we help domestic violence survivors? First, provide a safe space where they can speak and be heard. When survivors share their stories, they have already taken the first step toward breaking the cycle of abuse. Believe their stories, because survivors are often the only ones who see the abuser's dark side.


Some survivors may not realize their situation is abnormal, because they have no other reference for healthy relationships and have gradually become accustomed to the cycle of violence. We need to tell survivors that violence and abuse are not part of a healthy relationship—but do not make decisions for them. We cannot "save" survivors. Abused individuals must make their own final decisions about whether to leave, when to leave, and when to seek help.


Do not say: "Why doesn't she just leave?"


Please say: "We hear you. We believe you. We stand with you."
