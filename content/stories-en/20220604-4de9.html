Introduction


In Vietnam, violence against women is highly prevalent. To date, a significant portion of women across the country continue to face substantial risks of intimate partner violence. Violence against women has severely affected women's personal health, safety, dignity, as well as their potential to change social status and economic productivity, with an estimated economic cost equivalent to 1.8% of the country's gross domestic product. This article presents and analyzes the history and current situation of domestic violence in Vietnam, hoping to draw greater attention and support for corresponding solutions, and anticipating that the challenges in combating domestic violence will be improved in the near future. 1.8% This article presents and analyzes the history and current situation of domestic violence in Vietnam, hoping to draw greater attention and support for corresponding solutions, and anticipating that the challenges in combating domestic violence will be improved in the near future.

Current Situation and Historical Origins of Domestic Violence Against Women in Vietnam


Problem Overview

1. Overview

Domestic violence against women has become a major issue in Vietnam. In 2010, the General Statistics Office of Viet Nam conducted a survey on domestic violence, interviewing approximately 4,838 women aged between 18 and 60. The study revealed that more than half (54%) of Vietnamese women had experienced physical, psychological, and sexual domestic violence, and 87% of those who experienced violence had never sought help from outside sources; about 26% of the interviewed women had been seriously injured due to domestic violence, with 60% of them injured multiple times (Viet Nam GSO et al., 2010). Worse still, even years later, the situation of domestic violence against Vietnamese women has shown no improvement and has even worsened. According to a 2019 report, nearly 62.9% of women nationwide had experienced at least one form of violence from their husbands or other intimate partners (including physical, sexual, economic, and psychological violence), with 31.6% having experienced such violence within the past year (UNFPA, 2019). Moreover, the severity of domestic violence shows significant regional disparities, with urban areas having significantly lower rates of violence than rural areas (UNFPA, 2020).


Violence against women has severely affected women's personal health, safety, dignity, as well as their potential to change social status and economic productivity. Long-term exposure to domestic violence significantly increases the likelihood of mental illness among abused women, and children who witness violence at home are more likely to exhibit behavioral problems such as bedwetting, aggressive behaviors, or excessive quietness and withdrawal. It is estimated that violence against women in Vietnam results in economic losses equivalent to 1.8% of the country's gross domestic product.


2. Impact of the Pandemic

The "shadow pandemic"[1] caused by the global spread of COVID-19 has significantly undermined anti-violence movements against women worldwide, and Vietnam is no exception. To contain the spread of the virus, tens of millions of residents across Vietnam were forced into prolonged home isolation, which undoubtedly increased the time women experiencing intimate partner violence spent with their abusers, while simultaneously blocking or hindering their ability to seek external help. Additionally, the pandemic's negative impact on the national economy significantly reduced or even eliminated the incomes of many women and their husbands, thereby triggering or intensifying family conflicts.


From June to September 2020, the Rosa-Luxemburg-Stiftung, the Institute for Social Development Studies, and the Hanoi School of Public Health jointly conducted research on the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on domestic violence against women in Hanoi. The study found that 99% of couples experienced marital or family conflicts during the pandemic, with 87.8% of women reporting psychological abuse and 59% reporting physical abuse. The project interviewed 303 women who had previously experienced violence from their husbands, of whom 84% reported increased frequency of controlling behaviors, 91% experienced more frequent psychological violence than usual, and 93% said physical violence occurred more often (Rosa-Luxemburg-Stiftung, Regional Office, 2020).


[1] Since the global outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic, due to the implementation of epidemic prevention measures such as home isolation, the time family members and intimate partners spend together has increased, significantly raising the likelihood of women experiencing violence from their partners. Societal attention to and protection for women suffering from violent acts have correspondingly weakened, making it more difficult for victimized women to receive help. Research by UN Women confirms that violence against women increased exponentially worldwide after the COVID-19 outbreak, a phenomenon termed "The shadow pandemic."



Historical Origins


Domestic violence—whether the violence itself, public officials' understanding of domestic violence, or women's "ignorance" about how to protect themselves—is essentially a manifestation of gender discrimination in Vietnamese society. Scholars primarily attribute the causes of domestic violence against Vietnamese women to the following aspects:


First is the intergenerational transmission of domestic violence, meaning children who have experienced or witnessed domestic violence are more likely to use similar violent behaviors toward their partners when they grow up (Vung & Krantz, 2009);


second, the likelihood of domestic violence is negatively correlated with the educational level and income of both spouses, and husbands with multiple partners are more likely to abuse their wives (Vung et al., 2008);


at the same time, an increasing number of Vietnamese women are entering the labor market, earning income, and participating in politics, which many men perceive as a challenge to their masculinity, leading them to resort to violence against their partners (Ha Song Vu et al., 2014).


Due to historical reasons, Vietnamese women have had many opportunities to work. During the 20-year Vietnam War (1955–1975), eligible men were conscripted into military service, leaving a large labor gap that forced the government to mobilize women to work in agriculture and industry (William S. Turley, 1972). Later, the Đổi mới (Renovation) policy launched in 1986 accelerated Vietnam's economic development and created more job opportunities for women. However, Vietnamese culture has been influenced by the "yin-yang theory" (Viet Nam GSO et al., 2010; Thi Minh Le et al., 2019)—where men, representing "yang," are seen as aggressive, while women, representing "yin," are seen as calm. This theory's interpretation of so-called "innate gender differences" not only somewhat legitimizes husbands' violent behaviors but also encourages men to use violence against their wives to maintain their masculinity.


Finally, traditional Vietnamese gender and family norms are also important causes of domestic violence (Viet Nam GSO et al., 2010). In traditional Vietnamese family gender norms, men are responsible for providing for the family and have the authority to discipline their wives, while wives are expected to focus on domestic duties—performing housework, bearing and raising children, and caring for other family members (Rydstrøm, 2006). This traditional framework places wives in a structurally disadvantaged position, making them more vulnerable to violence from their husbands.


In addition to the above reasons, when scholars examine this phenomenon, they often link gender discrimination to Vietnam's historical influence from Confucian culture.


Since the 2nd century BC, Confucian culture has been spreading in Vietnam (Fan Yehong, 2015), and to this day, Vietnam's politics, society, and culture remain deeply influenced by Confucianism (Bourke, 2001; Bui Thi Thanh Mai et al., 2004; Ghuman, 2005), forming one of the roots of gender devaluation and discrimination in Vietnam. From a gender perspective, Confucian culture is a male-centered gender system (Fu Hongquan, 2017), that is, patriarchy. Li Yinhe argues that patriarchy can be used interchangeably with paternalism, as synonyms (Li Yinhe, 2021).


The traditional patriarchal logic of Confucian culture centers on the principle of "the father as the guiding principle for the son." Jia Zhenyong (2014) argues that the Classic of Filial Piety and Dong Zhongshu's Luxuriant Dew of the Spring and Autumn Annals from the early Han dynasty were key texts in institutionalizing paternal authority into "theorized, conceptualized, standardized, and enforced" norms. During the Song dynasty, Zhu Xi and others defined the "Three Bonds" (ruler-minister, father-son, husband-wife) in the form of "X as the guiding principle for Y," making the concept of "the ruler as the guiding principle for the minister, the father as the guiding principle for the son, the husband as the guiding principle for the wife" widely known (Fang Zhaohui, 2020). The development and spread of Confucian culture, along with its endorsement by successive rulers, provided strong legal, administrative, and cultural support for the principle of "the father as the guiding principle for the son" (Jia Zhenyong, 2014).


As a result, patriarchy has diminished women's value in three aspects: "patriarchal, patrilocal, and patrilineal" (Li Dongli, 2000). Lineage, genealogy, inheritance of property, and parental authority are determined through the paternal line; after marriage, women leave their natal families to live with their husband's family, residing according to patrilineal kinship; patriarchal society disregards the value of women's domestic and reproductive labor; paternal heads of households hold power over property distribution; only males can carry on the family line, and so on. Patriarchy and gender discrimination are mutually reinforcing—men in patriarchal systems gain power through the devaluation, discrimination, and exploitation of women; and those who benefit must intensify the exploitation of women to further consolidate their privileges.



Challenges in Domestic Violence Support for Women in Vietnam


The journey to eliminate violence against women is long and arduous; the formulation and implementation of national strategies, the realization of change, and the fulfillment of visions cannot be achieved overnight. In general, Vietnam still faces the following challenges in combating domestic violence:


Victims themselves:

Low rates of seeking help due to entrenched beliefs


Studies indicate that even when women experiencing domestic violence seek help from local officials, the abuse is often treated as a "private family matter," and thus domestic violence cannot be effectively prevented. Moreover, very few women are aware of policies and laws related to domestic violence (Viet Nam GSO et al., 2010)—despite the fact that the Vietnamese government enacted the Law on Prevention and Control of Domestic Violence in 2007, which officially came into effect in 2008 (Ha Song Vu et al., 2014).


Despite the alarmingly high prevalence of victimization, violence against women remains largely hidden in Vietnam. Among women who had experienced violence from intimate partners such as husbands, 90.4% of victims did not attempt to seek any help, and only a small minority of 4.8% reported the incident to the police. Behind this widespread silence are traditional beliefs held by women themselves, as well as societal norms that blame victims while excusing perpetrators. Due to widespread biased social and cultural norms, many victims of domestic violence do not perceive gender-based violence as a crime. The majority of women who have experienced violence in intimate relationships do not speak out or seek assistance. 35.9% of women believe that husbands hold the status of "head of the household" and should take action to demonstrate and defend their position; 27% of women believe a good wife should obey her husband's decisions even if she disagrees; and more than half of women (51.8%) believe a husband may beat his wife for one or more specific reasons (including but not limited to: disloyalty to the husband, failing to properly care for children, or not completing household chores). Beyond society's general indifference toward domestic violence, some women also remain silent due to feelings of shame associated with being abused, choosing to endure the abuse in order to preserve family harmony, protect their own or their parents' reputation, or safeguard their children's upbringing. (UN Women Viet Nam Country Office, 2020)



Government:

Intervention and assistance for specific victims remains inefficient.


Models of intervention and support for victims of violence are currently very small in scale and fragmented, implemented by various stakeholders (national, local, and international NGOs, community organizations) without an established coordination mechanism. For individual cases, government intervention and assistance remain inefficient and limited, with unclear division of responsibilities across government levels and poor cooperation and coordination. In Vietnam, domestic violence falls under the responsibility of the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism, while gender-based violence cases are managed by the Ministry of Labour, Invalids and Social Affairs. The participation and contribution of all relevant sectors (culture, police, justice, health, education) in preventing and controlling violence are weak and ill-defined. There appears to be little coordination or collaboration among ministries to address these issues in a coherent and comprehensive manner (UNICEF, 2020). Mediation teams, community workers, and local government bodies that are meant to provide direct assistance to victims often fail to respond promptly and effectively due to lack of motivation and capacity. The number of social work centers that can provide medical care, psychological counseling, and legal advice to victimized women is far from sufficient.


At the community and urban levels, there are no hotlines for women to contact when experiencing sexual harassment or violence. Typically, they choose to report directly to the police, local authorities, or local organizations (such as the Women's Union). Family Affairs Committees and Child Protection Committees respond to domestic violence cases as needed, but these committees tend to prioritize mediation as a solution, only referring cases to other service providers or authorities when they deem the violence to be very severe (UNICEF, 2020). Police response to cases of violence against women is also relatively slow, and mediation is often the default first option. Police typically only intervene when the perpetrator continues to use violence after mediation has failed. Women also find it difficult to access legal aid and have direct contact with judicial officials such as police and prosecutors. There is no specialized mechanism within the justice system to assist women experiencing domestic violence in accessing and participating in legal procedures. (UN Women Viet Nam Country Office, 2020)


Furthermore, many women hold distrust toward the support services available in their communities. Among women who have suffered violence, nearly two-thirds of victims cannot receive timely assistance from relevant authorities. (UN Women Viet Nam Country Office, 2020).


Health system:

Slow response to updates in relevant laws and policies.


After experiencing domestic violence, women may, for various reasons, choose not to pursue legal action or seek third-party intervention from civil organizations or social workers. However, when facing serious injuries, they are still likely to actively seek treatment through the health system. Ideally, if healthcare workers could pay attention to women suspected of experiencing domestic violence and facilitate intervention by social organizations or even the police, it would undoubtedly provide strong support to help silent victims escape the shadows.


Unfortunately, more than a decade has passed since the enactment of the Domestic Violence Prevention and Control Law (DVPC), yet there has still been no significant positive response or change within the health system. Decentralization has granted the health system a high degree of autonomy, allowing local authorities to independently decide on the budgeting and allocation of nationally devolved health funds. In the absence of a unified, dedicated budget line for DVPC implementation within the health system, local health systems' financial investment largely depends on the preferences and priorities of local managers, who often prioritize spending on disease treatment and environmental improvements.


There are few specialized temporary shelters at the central and community levels. Most hospitals themselves do not have dedicated psychologists or social workers. For victims showing depressive symptoms, only sedatives are provided to offer temporary relief and short-term alleviation of suffering, which does not help resolve their long-term problems. After treating victims of domestic violence, hospitals often face difficulties in following up on the victims' subsequent situations due to a lack of horizontal cooperation with the police or local government. Victims are typically treated as ordinary patients, with hopes placed on social organizations to provide further targeted assistance. (Le, T. M., Morley, C., Hill, P. S., Bui, Q. T., & Dunne, M. P., 2019)

Social services:

Funding shortages lead to poor quality and insufficient coverage.


Vietnam's National Strategy on Gender-Based Violence (2011–2020), the Programme on Prevention of and Response to Gender-Based Violence (2016–2020), and the National Strategy on Gender Equality (2021–2030 phase) all mention the need to provide social services for victims of domestic violence, including emergency temporary shelters, safety protection, care support, and counseling. In practice, with funding primarily sourced from the national budget, local ministries and departments, and with support from international organizations such as the United Nations, Vietnam has indeed established temporary community shelters, legal aid centers, and other social service units in central general hospitals and communities, aiming to provide women with legal aid, psychological counseling, and emergency assistance. By 2018, the Vietnam Women's Union had established over 500 social service points across the country capable of providing such support (UN Women, 2018).


However, to date, the coverage of these social services reaches only a portion of victims. According to a UN Women survey, only 41.5% of victims are aware of the existence of these social services and know specific information such as hotline numbers and exact locations of institutions, while only 25.4% of victims actually used hotline services to seek social support and assistance. Most victims are unaware of the specific services available. In addition to insufficient promotion and low visibility, the quality of some social services also falls short of expected standards, such as lacking basic infrastructure including medical supplies, tableware, and bedding. Most social workers have not received adequate professional training and are unable to address sensitive issues such as victims' psychological needs (UN Women, 2018).



Domestic violence response policies and institutions in Vietnam


Vietnam has given significant attention to combating violence against women and supporting victims of domestic violence, signing or participating in numerous international conventions aimed at eliminating violence against women and incorporating many of their provisions into its domestic legal framework. In addition to regularly formulating phase-specific national strategies to address gender-based violence, the Vietnamese government is, to date, the only national government in the world that has used the World Health Organization's cross-culturally validated methodology to conduct two dedicated studies. It collaborated with the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA) in 2010 and again in 2019 to carry out national studies on violence against women, producing detailed reports that summarize progress and explore ways to continue effectively addressing the issue.


Domestic violence response policies and institutions in Vietnam

1. The Family Violence Law and the Law on Prevention and Control of Domestic Violence

The Vietnamese government is committed to promoting gender equality, women's empowerment, and ending violence against women through policy and legal reforms. Vietnam was one of the first countries to ratify the UN Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW) and is a signatory to many other international human rights treaties. Vietnam is currently working towards achieving Sustainable Development Goal 5: "Achieve gender equality and empower all women and girls." Gender equality is enshrined in the Constitution, the Law on Gender Equality, the Law on Domestic Violence Prevention and Control, and various sectoral laws. Preventing gender-based violence is a key objective of the National Strategy on Gender Equality (2011–2020). In 2014, the Vietnamese government revised the Law on Marriage and Family, emphasizing gender equality in household labor, protecting the rights of women and children, and preventing domestic violence. Between 2015 and 2016, the Law on Domestic Violence was reviewed, with recommendations for revision. The revision aimed to clarify the law's purpose as preventing all forms of domestic violence, rather than primarily preserving family unity, thereby placing the protection of women and children above family cohesion and breaking through the traditional limitation of "an upright official cannot resolve family disputes." The Law on Domestic Violence also clearly defines gender-based violence and aligns the definition of sexual violence with international standards. In addition to legal and policy protections, the Vietnamese government has established a formal system of domestic violence services and shelters (UNFPA, 2020).


A key feature of Vietnam's Law on Domestic Violence is the application of criminal offenses under the Penal Code to cases of domestic violence. The Penal Code defines several crimes related to domestic violence against women, and general offenses such as intentional injury or assault apply equally to violence involving strangers and violence occurring within families. As awareness has grown that existing civil and criminal procedures often fail to adequately address domestic violence, the government enacted a separate piece of legislation—the Law on the Prevention and Control of Domestic Violence. This law promotes support and protection for victims, whereas other criminal and administrative laws primarily focus on sanctioning perpetrators. It outlines principles and measures for the prevention and control of domestic violence, as well as the roles and responsibilities of institutions and organizations. Articles 1 and 2 provide a broad definition of domestic violence, including physical, sexual, psychological/emotional, and economic abuse. In some respects, this definition is even broader than the understanding of violence under international standards. For example, psychological or emotional abuse is generally limited to ongoing threats, degradation, or controlling behaviors, rather than merely causing mental stress. When defining these forms of violence, the law does not establish new criminal or administrative offenses for punishing perpetrators. Criminal and administrative sanctions remain defined in the Penal Code and the Ordinance on Handling Administrative Violations. Therefore, the definition of domestic violence in this law is primarily used to identify acts that entitle victims to special protection orders and other support services (UNODC, 2011). Vietnam's comprehensive legal and policy framework reflects an advanced understanding of women's rights protection and provides a solid foundation for preventing domestic violence.


2. The Law on Gender Equality

Cultural beliefs in gender, such as the idea of "men superior, women inferior," legitimize violence against women. When a culture harbors "attitudes supporting violence," women may normalize and rationalize the violence they experience. These attitudes do not directly cause violence, but they can create an atmosphere of acceptance that hinders women from seeking help. Gender inequality is the cultural breeding ground for domestic violence. If a community widely holds "attitudes supporting violence," it can affect the responses of leaders and service providers and make it harder for women to receive effective assistance when seeking refuge. Therefore, preventing domestic violence requires changing gender perceptions among men, women, and young people—including children—promoting gender equality, and raising public understanding and awareness of gender-based violence.


To promote gender equality and effectively prevent domestic violence, the Vietnamese government enacted the Law on Gender Equality. This law explains the meaning of "gender equality": "Gender equality indicates that men and women have equal status and roles; they should, under equal conditions and opportunities, demonstrate their abilities in social and family development and equally enjoy the fruits of development." At the same time, the Law on Gender Equality states that the goal of gender equality is "eliminating gender discrimination, creating equal opportunities for men and women in socio-economic development and human resource development, achieving substantive equality between men and women, and building and strengthening cooperation and mutual support between men and women in all areas of society and family" (Shi Hai Ci, 2015). During 2017–2018, UNFPA supported MOLISA in conducting an independent review of the Law on Gender Equality. The review recommended the need to define harmful practices that lead to gender inequality (e.g., sex-selective abortion, sexual harassment, early or forced marriage); treat such harmful practices as criminal offenses; and increase financial penalties for violations of gender equality (UNFPA, 2020). The Law on Gender Equality emphasizes not only equal opportunities and outcomes for both sexes in the public sphere but also equality within the private domain of the family. Extending the reach of the law into the household demonstrates the Vietnamese government's commitment to women's rights and gender equality.

Vietnamese society's efforts: future outlook


KontraS

(Indonesian Commission for the Disappeared and Victims of Violence)


Vietnamese society has demonstrated strong political will and a demand for data in addressing issues of violence prevention and women's empowerment.. The 2007 Law on Domestic Violence revealed a need for rigorous baseline data. Key implementing agencies such as the Ministry of Labor, Invalids and Social Affairs and the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism have requested data to assist them in preventing and responding to violence against women (Gender data, 2019).


On November 15, 2016, the National Committee for Advancement of Women, the Ministry of Labor, and local governments at all levels responsible for women's advancement jointly launched a one-month-long series of activities titled "Eliminating Violence Against Women and Girls," involving 55 provinces and cities, 7 ministries, and 18 UN-related organizations and NGOs, hosting a total of 790 related events and attracting nearly 200,000 participants.


In many parts of Vietnamese society, traditional gender stereotypes and perceptions of domestic violence remain dominant. Many government officials, state administrative agencies, and judicial institutions lack capacity in gender mainstreaming. Although other studies using survey data have deepened understanding of risk factors for violence against women, gaps in data and programming still exist. For example, UN Women points out a lack of assessment, research, and coordination among different ministries, departments, agencies, and NGOs.


The initial development of Vietnam's Law on the Prevention and Control of Domestic Violence was driven by both external and internal pressures, but its subsequent implementation within the health system has experienced persistent delays. Monitoring and evaluation mechanisms still need improvement, and the quality of training for workers must be enhanced. Research shows that in cases involving domestic violence, several challenges limit the effectiveness of police intervention, legal aid, and efforts to resolve and respond to domestic violence cases. Generally, there is a need to strengthen the capacity of relevant institutions to handle domestic violence cases with the necessary knowledge and skills. Police, mediation teams, and legal aid providers need training on the concept of domestic violence. Officials, mediation teams, and legal aid providers—especially local authorities such as local administrative units and People's Committees, who often serve as first responders—need training on the concepts, causes, and consequences of domestic violence so they can take these cases seriously and avoid victim-blaming.


Cooperation among institutions must be strengthened to facilitate case referrals, higher-level authorities must provide more guidance, and both authorities and the public must raise awareness and change attitudes toward domestic violence. To overcome these challenges, the Vietnamese government is intensifying communication and awareness-raising efforts, conducting training on gender equality and domestic violence laws, and consolidating the national database system on gender-based violence (a key commitment under the National Thematic Programme) as well as fulfilling key commitments of the National Thematic Programme (NTP) on the prevention of and response to gender-based violence (2016–2020).


In May 2016, the 14th National Assembly and People's Councils at all levels were elected for a term from 2016 to 2021, selecting a total of 494 National Assembly deputies, including 132 women, accounting for approximately 26.72%. The Ministry of Labour, Invalids and Social Affairs of Vietnam and the South Korean Ministry of Gender Equality and Family jointly held a closing conference for the "Support Program for Enhancing Vocational Skills of Vietnamese Women" in Quang Nam Province, Vietnam. Launched in 2011, the program aimed to establish a model for improving vocational training capabilities among Vietnamese women, with pilot implementation in Can Tho, Thai Binh, and Quang Nam provinces. Over its five years of implementation, hundreds of thousands of rural women in Vietnam have benefited. 


In December 2016, the Vietnam Women's Union, together with a Dutch development organization, held a seminar titled "Enhancing Women's Entrepreneurship and Agricultural Economic Management Capacity to Promote Gender Equality," attended by Politburo member and Deputy Prime Minister Vuong Dinh Hue. At the event, Nguyen Thi Thu Ha, Chairwoman of the Vietnam Women's Union, stated that the union had submitted the "Women's Entrepreneurship Support Project 2017–2027" to senior leadership, aiming to achieve a target where women account for 35% of every million entrepreneurs by 2020. In February 2017, a large-scale women's entrepreneurship briefing session was held in Hanoi.


Breaking gender stereotypes and norms, providing institutional safeguards and mental health support for women, and successfully empowering women economically will become the crucial foundation and primary goal for changing the current situation of domestic violence. It is believed that with increased attention and efforts from all sectors of society, the goals of protecting women's rights and achieving gender equality will be realized sooner.
