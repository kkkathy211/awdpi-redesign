On February 23, 2021, at 9:20 a.m. local time in the Netherlands, Leah posted to her social media feed. In five close-up shots, the reflections of riverbeds and tree branches overlapped on the water's surface, with moss, dead wood, and sprouting grass shoots forming a vivid contrast. The last photo, reflected on the glass door of a street-facing building, showed her own figure carrying large bags. She captioned the series: "Hope Hope."



Friends often assume Leah lives in perpetual springtime, as green is undoubtedly the most frequent color appearing in her social media posts—even now, during Amsterdam's winter. In reality, this winter has not been easy for Leah. She is fighting a difficult legal battle against someone who was once her closest companion, who now holds all the advantages and is contesting her for their shared assets and their daughter. Over the phone, Leah's demeanor makes it hard to associate her with this protracted, cross-border divorce case spanning mainland China, Hong Kong, and Taiwan. She embodies the image of an independent woman—clear career goals, broad vision, positive, talkative, quick-speaking, and sharp-minded. When discussing her divorce, she mostly sounds as if narrating someone else's story. Only when she speaks of her two daughters does her voice reveal sorrow, pause, and break with emotion, reminding us of her strongest yet most vulnerable identity in this legal battle: that of a mother to two children..


Beyond this, she is also the president of a transnational art association, founder of a women's museum, and director of an art gallery in the Netherlands. After her marriage broke down, she returned to her hometown and established the women's museum. Initially an outlet for self-healing, it later became her gateway to the world and a channel through which she transmits strength and energy.


She openly acknowledges her needs and struggles. For her, fighting the legal battle, founding the women's museum, and proactively speaking out through Avoice all point to the same goal—defending her rights and helping more women learn to protect their own rights. She said: " If someone could have told you about these things beforehand, like getting a preventive vaccine, even if it helps just one person, it serves as a warning or reference. That is the purpose of my speaking out. "


Below is Leah's story.


01 Love

Every woman or girl has countless fantasies and dreams about love, believing that with love, one has everything—including me in the past. Many women regard love as the most beautiful thing in the world, as the entirety of life. I want to say, it truly is one of the most beautiful things, but it is not the entirety of life.


My ex-husband and I met in the Netherlands in 2004. He proposed to me 100 times. After marriage, we lived in China for a period. Despite some cultural gaps between East and West, we still felt sweet and happy. Because he didn't understand Chinese language and culture and was not proactive in engaging with or integrating into society, I ended up handling most matters. Gradually, I gained control over our household finances. I never worried about money—he didn't care how much I spent. All the properties were under my name, so I never had any sense of crisis. This led me, in 2016, to overlook the red flag when I discovered he frequently called an Italian woman, thinking chatting might just be his way of unwinding.


In 2018, in Italy, I confirmed he was having an affair. He began using verbal and physical violence against me, then begged for my forgiveness after each incident. We returned to the Netherlands to see a psychologist, and I thought we had a chance to reconcile. But while I was trying to repair our relationship, he was plotting to leave me with nothing. The chance I saw for reconciliation became his opportunity to transfer all our assets. He apologized to me, pretended to promise he would break up with that woman, said he knew he was wrong and could never leave me, while secretly planning a much larger scheme. He froze my bank cards, canceled all my residency permits, took the children's passports, sold all our properties, and transferred all our assets. Then he handed me two suitcases, having packed all my clothes and personal belongings, and told me, "This is all that belongs to you."


Later, he even told me he had been planning this for four years. At that moment, I felt as if waking from a dream. After 16 years of marriage, was it really over like this? Could he treat a woman—someone who might not have shared every hardship with him, but who had weathered 16 years of storms alongside him—this way? At the time, I couldn't believe it was real.


02 Children

Our marriage is beyond repair. Now the main issues are property and the children.


I told my ex-husband, "I don't want money—just give me our two daughters." But he kept insisting that each parent take one child. I refused. I believe the children must live together. Why should two sisters have to bear the consequences of separation just because their parents' marriage failed? This is why I have never compromised and continue fighting the legal battle. I want to secure custody of both children, to care for them daily. Every summer, they could go live with their father. I don't want them separated. They've built a strong bond since childhood, and if one day we're no longer here, they'll still have each other as sisters.


Every unfaithful parent should reflect deeply. They fail to see the trauma their extramarital affairs inflict on children after the family breaks apart. My elder daughter has become extremely sensitive amid this painful conflict. My ex-husband used to frequently record audio and video at home as evidence, which has made her now strongly averse to being photographed or recorded. She is also more mature than her peers, asking me questions like, "Why did Mom and Dad separate?"


One day, my elder daughter suddenly told me she never wants to get married. She said, "If Mom hadn't married this person, none of these things would have happened." I felt heartbroken, feeling like a complete failure as a mother. Even if a marriage cannot be restored, spouses should never bring their emotions and resentments into the children's world—because the children are innocent. 


I told my elder daughter, "Yes, it was Mom's mistake. When I got married, I probably wasn't cautious enough—I was too young. Everyone makes some wrong choices. Maybe the marriage you see from Mom and Dad now seems bad, but I hope and believe your marriage will be beautiful. And of course, whether or not to marry is entirely your own choice. People's thoughts change at different stages. Marriage isn't a path every woman must take." 


I'm not saying this just because my marriage was imperfect. I believe everyone has the right to choose. Marriage or not—it's simply different lifestyles.


When I'm alone with the children, they always ask me, "How many more minutes until he (Dad) comes to pick me up? How many more minutes until I have to leave?" They clearly wish to stay longer with me, yet they still worry about the time to return. I feel deeply pained. I never imagined I wouldn't even have the power to decide where my own children live, that our time together would feel so rushed, that I'd have to send them back the moment the clock strikes. Amid the joy of reunion, I also face the heartbreak of impending separation. When they leave, they cry. When they cry, I cry too—and then they comfort me, saying, "Mom, don't cry. Go back home."


03 Self

When I first discovered his infidelity and domestic violence, I consulted a lawyer. If I had rationally and courageously filed for divorce then, I would still have had the houses, the children, and the residency permits—instead of being forced now to fight this divorce case across three countries. But my attachment to over a decade of relationship and my strong sense of pride prevented me from firmly pursuing divorce at the time. 


When most people discover betrayal, they are unwilling to accept the truth. But we must accept it, because this is a critical moment—when the other person may still feel guilt, and when you still hold more control over assets. Once he feels no emotion left and sees you as an obstacle, he will do everything to remove you. Ending up passive is extremely dangerous. 


Perhaps this advice is meant for the younger generation—or maybe even for my daughter. Never let yourself fall into a passive position. That is the most important point, because the cost is too high. This process has been dark, with no visible edge, seemingly endless—shuttling between three countries, an unceasing drain on my mind, emotions, spirit, finances, and time. Last December, I was again subjected to verbal threats and physical violence. This time, my immediate reaction was to call the police, because reporting is how I protect myself. I will no longer choose silence..


I want to use my experience to tell young women, life is full of changes, and life is unpredictable. You cannot demand others remain unchanged. When facing such changes, what matters most is your own strength. We must be mentally prepared—whether emotionally or financially—to have a sense of self-protection. Even if one day I truly end up with nothing, I still have the ability to support myself. 


Perhaps everyone will face a moment when money, experience, and personal relationships can no longer help you. Facing hardship by constantly complaining or blaming yourself is meaningless. Losing the love for life, ceasing to care for others, constantly sinking into suffering without being able to rise—this is true failure. 


I want to encourage women with similar experiences: no matter how life treats us, we must not imprison ourselves. We should face and change our current situation with a calm heart. Our souls and thoughts are things no one can take away. Even when such things happen, we must still preserve beauty within ourselves and maintain kindness and love toward others.


I believe those who harm others are themselves deeply suffering. Now my ex-husband feels completely unfamiliar to me—he dresses sloppily and appears to be in poor condition, entirely different from how he was when we were together. "The most familiar stranger"—this phrase perhaps best describes how I see him now. I feel sorrow for him.


04 Healing

Around 2016, I was running a guesthouse in the Netherlands when I met a woman from Romania who had come to visit her child. Her ex-husband had taken the child away, and she never expected that simply seeing her child would become so difficult. At the time, her story felt very distant to me. I never imagined that four years later, the same thing would happen to me.


Perhaps every woman sees stories in the media about domestic violence or financial fraud, but because we haven't experienced them firsthand, we always feel these things are far away. Now that this has happened to me, I feel a responsibility to share my experience with younger women. If I don't speak up now, perhaps in four more years, many others will continue to repeat these same experiences.


Currently, I am building a women's museum—a space to focus on women's own development. My museum isn't a traditional exhibition of artifacts; rather, it centers on women to express ideas. The most important thing is to make the museum dynamic, a place for continuous exchange of ideas. Thoughts are the most interesting and vital things.


I am an ethnic minority and am familiar with minority cultures, so I start from my own experience, linking together the three milestones of a minority woman's life—birth, marriage, and death—and then expanding infinitely to include customs, traditions, and humanistic care across different ethnicities and nations, creating a topic that women worldwide can discuss.


In my ethnic language, the word for "fate" means "woman." In Chinese, it refers to life and destiny. I am "fate" (woman), yet I don't believe in fate—I refuse to accept so-called "destiny." As a child, I lived in a small village, but I refused to grow old in one place, so I chose to leave and see the outside world, to experience different cultures. The breakdown of my marriage brought both gains and losses—gains and losses are like light and darkness. Without such intense pain, my museum would not exist. So I believe life holds infinite possibilities.


In addition, the women's museum offers psychological counseling support to help women awaken and grow. We provide different types of empowerment based on participants' professions: if she is an artist, she can set up exhibitions here; if she is an entrepreneur, she can give lectures or run business management classes to help economically disadvantaged women achieve financial independence.


The museum reflects my inner journey. I hope that by sharing my story and experiences, I can bring healing and vitality to others, helping them avoid unnecessary detours. For me personally, the museum has also given me the power to heal. My marriage was utterly broken, but through the museum, I found an outlet—learning to sort through my emotions, express my experiences and trauma, and ultimately discovering my own value.


I still have many ideas, such as applying to the government for funding to support the museum as a project, or applying for UN women's craft workshops. There may still be a long way to go before I realize my dream. But I believe that once I've started, I must persist—perhaps opportunity will come when I least expect it. I now have a small volunteer team, and I hope more people will join in the future. I also hope to collaborate with more organizations like Avoice, so that more female communities will pay attention to these women, giving suffering women greater voice, so that younger women may no longer repeat our experiences.




Perhaps the end of Leah's 16-year marriage shattered her once-happy and stable dream. But today, she has become her own dreamer, regaining control of her life, and hopes to share her experiences and feelings with more women in similar situations.


Her story may give more people the courage to speak out. Let more real stories be seen, and let women become sources of mutual support. These stories may vary widely, but one thing remains unchanged: behind these stories, the resilient strength of women is breaking through darkness and growing freely..
