Reflections and Suggestions on Domestic Violence

Domestic violence does not begin when one person grabs another's neck, but when one says, "I love you, you belong to me."


Domestic violence escalates from an initial slap or kick, a shove or push, to eventual outcomes of violent retaliation—"either you die or I die." Facing these experiences of domestic violence, the scenes are truly horrifying: women blinded by having their eyes gouged out; bound hand and foot, whipped with belts, mouths stuffed with foul socks; hands cut slice by slice; and many more forms of family violence we fail to recognize or even name—coldness, insults, degradation, sexual abuse, modern-day PUA, forbidding a partner from working. The psychological harm these inflict is obvious and extremely severe. Of course, more common forms of domestic violence involve severe beatings, leaving victims bruised and bloodied, covered in wounds; this reminds me of the drama *Don't Talk to Strangers* starring Feng Yuanzheng and Mei Ting.


It seems that lives affected by domestic violence have been cast into a fixed pattern and cycle. I believe this is not the outcome anyone desires. Repeatedly witnessing the process of domestic violence in counseling sessions, and seeing accounts of such experiences through various channels, my heart feels heavy. I constantly wonder: how can we change this situation? What can we do?


Therefore, today I would like to discuss the theme of "Living Out Inner Strength" from three aspects.


1

Facing the truth,

not only physical assault constitutes domestic violence!

Facing the truth—that not only physical assault constitutes domestic violence—is the first step toward living out inner strength. Only by facing it and becoming aware of it can we have the opportunity to address and escape domestic violence.


Domestic violence can be divided into four categories:

1) Physical violence: slapping, punching, pushing, kicking, dragging, burning, choking, or even using knives, guns, or other weapons;

2) Emotional violence, such as the coldness, insults, degradation, and PUA mentioned above;

3) Economic abuse: forbidding a partner from having disposable income;

4) Sexual violence: sexual abuse, forced sexual relations, etc.;


Moreover, it is not only women who suffer domestic violence—though they are more numerous—men also account for about 10%. Men who experience domestic violence are even less likely to receive help, due to the internal shame that makes them reluctant to seek support.


Clarifying these points is to call for greater public awareness of domestic violence. When we think "this isn't domestic violence" or "this cannot be spoken of," we are actually neglecting our own injuries and giving implicit permission for the abuse to continue! Therefore, from the very beginning, recognizing that one is experiencing domestic violence—recognizing that one is being harmed—allows one to bravely confront it, thus reducing the tolerance and harm caused by misunderstanding as early as possible. This suggestion is not only for survivors of domestic violence, but also for their family and friends. When we notice someone close to us suffering such experiences, being able to name it and further stand with the victim to find solutions can significantly reduce the harm to them. We see many victims, silenced by the abuser's violence, bearing everything alone. Often, the end result is using violence to end violence, bringing irreparable pain to themselves and their loved ones.


In fact, many people rationalize and make assumptions about initial experiences of assault, provocation, or sexual abuse: "He was drunk," "He was angry," "He must have had a bad day," "I didn't do well enough," "I provoked him," etc.


Recently, there was a TV drama called *The Lady's Law*, featuring a lawyer named Jiang Qiong. She is a lawyer specializing in marriage-related litigation and is experiencing domestic violence. When Chen Ran suggests she should divorce, she tells Chen Ran that divorce would leave her with nothing. At that moment, she feels the pressure of public opinion outweighs the harm she is currently suffering from domestic violence. She tells Chen Ran she must endure—it is her calculated choice. If even a lawyer can respond to domestic violence this way, what about others? In fact, she is using rationalization to avoid confronting certain painful realities. What Jiang Qiong faces is the prospect of losing everything—her years of hard work going up in smoke—which, to her, is worse than the domestic violence itself. In other words, she uses rationalization to reduce the likelihood of what she perceives as even worse outcomes. What could be worse than domestic violence? Perhaps facing even greater violence, retaliation, abandonment, public ridicule, feeling that family and friends won't believe her, bringing trouble to her parents or children, causing harm to them, or fearing she won't have money to live if she resists. There may also be lingering fantasies and hopes that her partner will change. All of these lead victims to choose tolerance and forbearance in the early stages of abuse, to avoid more serious consequences. Unaware that this repeated tolerance leads to even greater harm later on, it is crucial to recognize abuse early and take appropriate action, as this is the best way to help oneself and others escape the damage of domestic violence.



2

Pay attention to your own pain

Shift your focus onto yourself.

Research has found that most domestic violence survivors are people who only give and constantly give, rarely acknowledging their own existence. They seldom pay attention to their own needs, and self-support is even rarer. Anyone in the family seems more important than the harm they are currently enduring. We also see that each incident of domestic violence brings dual suffering—psychological and physical. Physical injuries may gradually heal with treatment, but the psychological and emotional pain lingers, deeply suppressed due to fear, causing longer-lasting internal damage. Studies show that besides direct physical harm, domestic violence causes chronic health problems through long-term stress.


Taking women as an example, compared to women who have not experienced abuse, survivors show higher levels of depression, anxiety, and fear, with significantly higher rates of emotional distress, suicidal thoughts, and suicide attempts.


After experiencing domestic violence, every survivor will try to prevent future harm—this is human instinct and necessary. In addition, one can seek help from women and children protection organizations, legal aid, or public welfare organizations to prevent greater harm through action. At the same time, I suggest that everyone also pay attention to their inner pain, shifting some focus onto their emotional wounds, helping the inner self become more resilient rather than rigidly tense and anxious. Only then can more energy be freed to find better ways to escape domestic violence. One must also find strength—or seek support—to heal the psychological trauma caused by domestic violence. This is a necessary and highly effective part of escaping the harm of domestic violence. Unhealed internal pressure is a major source driving victims toward violent retaliation or self-harm. Therefore, I recommend that individuals seek professional psychological counselors to assist in trauma healing. When we regain psychological resilience, our goal can shift from "how to avoid" to "how to leave more easily!"



3

Let's discuss

the softness and integration of the female gender.

We must not equate domestic violence simply with "one party losing emotional control." In fact, domestic violence is a pattern of control and being controlled. Research from around the world shows that domestic violence in heterosexual relationships results from one party's desire to exert power and control over the other.


Simply put: "You must belong to me!" Research on female victims shows they perceive abusive men as low in intimacy and caregiving, but high in anger, aggression, authority, and dominance. The United Nations, in its *Declaration on the Elimination of Violence against Women*, points out: Rigid gender roles are a key predictive factor in the intergenerational transmission of violence..


What is gender role rigidity? It is the habitual belief that "men should act like men, and women should act like women," placing one gender's role above the other in power and authority.


What is gender role rigidity? It is the habitual belief that "men should act like men, and women should act like women," placing one gender's role above the other in power and authority.


For example:

Which family doesn't have men beating women?

Women just need to be beaten into obedience...

Women should always cooperate with men when men want sex, and have no right to refuse.

Women should tolerate violence in order to maintain family harmony.

Men have the right to physically punish women for their "inappropriate" behaviors.

Violence and sexual acts (including rape) are seen as signs of masculinity.


These all reflect rigid gender beliefs, and many women, influenced by such beliefs, come to accept and even demand of themselves that they endure abuse. Women seem to have long been placed in a subordinate, weak role that depends on men—whether in shaping their future or in meeting their emotional needs and desires. Phrases like "marry a man, gain food and clothing" or "marry a chicken, follow the chicken; marry a dog, follow the dog" reflect this. Any attempt by women to resist or change this seems like an impossible fantasy. All of this reveals a singular, rigid state of mind. Therefore, moving toward integration is a path that must be taken. In this process, we come to see that weakness is not the same as softness, and fear is not the same as endurance. Only when we see these differences can we find more ways to help ourselves escape domestic violence.


I hope that through the above three points, everyone can learn to face reality, care for their own pain, and integrate the characteristics of female identity. You may feel it's not easy to achieve these things. When you are being beaten, fear and pain may drown out your inner strength, leading you to choose escape. The experience of being beaten is extremely painful, so it's often hard to look back, and you may not dare or want to. Yet deep inside, you long for someone to see you, to support you. When we realize that societal norms restrict and confine women's identities, the question becomes: do we submit to these constraints, or do we bravely challenge these limiting beliefs? This requires strength. Therefore, living out your own strength is a crucial psychological factor in addressing domestic violence.


I want to say to those experiencing domestic violence: you need to slowly pay attention to and support yourself in the small details of daily life. Even when it seems impossible, seek out ways to support yourself, allowing your suppressed and blocked strength to gradually coalesce and accumulate into the full, powerful force you once had, so you can protect yourself and help yourself heal from the trauma of domestic violence.


I want to say to those around the victims: don't ask them why they don't leave. Instead, discuss with them the situation they are currently experiencing; ask what specific help they need and what supportive resources you can offer to help them escape domestic violence; maintain regular contact to some extent, so they know you are someone they can reach out to when needed, a source of support, someone standing with them through this. If possible, you can also help create a safety plan centered on their needs and fears.


Ending violence is not a fight you face alone! Domestic violence is not a private issue, but a social one—it is part of a broader culture of systemic violence. While we seek ways to reduce domestic violence, it is equally important to truly center the survivor in how we help them. Using our care and attention to awaken their own self-care and self-worth is even more essential.




Perpetrator personality traits



Here, I will share some common personality traits of perpetrators in most cases of domestic violence.

They can be roughly divided into the following 15 points:

1

Insecurity and inner emptiness

The more insecure and internally empty a person is, the more they crave power, the more domineering they become, and the stronger their desire for control and possession. In contrast, the more confident, internally rich, and strong a person is, the more tolerant, accepting, and gentle they are; they understand compromise, care less about others' judgments, accept criticism and rejection, allow others to be themselves, and truly respect others.


2

Obsession (stubbornness, fixity)

A personality that clings rigidly to logic, gets stuck in narrow thinking, immerses itself in its own understanding, completely shuts out external voices, and sees itself as the holder of truth; extremely self-centered and emotionally detached.


3

"Should" thinking

Demanding and regulating others according to one's own set logic or standards, taking it for granted that others must comply, and naturally judging others by one's own standards.


4

Absolute thinking (black-and-white)

These people love moral judgment, are harsh on themselves and others, lack warmth, cannot tolerate flaws, differences, or mistakes, and especially cannot bear defiance—tending toward extremes.


5

Reformist mentality

Wanting to reshape their partner into their ideal type; otherwise, they will force a fit, or even prefer destruction over compromise.


6

Savior complex

Placing themselves in the role of savior, believing "I do this for your own good," "I'm saving you," "I've sacrificed so much for you"—so if you don't cooperate, don't appreciate it, or don't meet their expectations, it's because you're ungrateful or unworthy, and thus they have the right to be angry and to hurt you.


7

Victim mentality

Feeling that they are always the one giving, always trying hard, yet constantly betrayed, hurt, ignored, or abandoned—seeing themselves as deeply wounded and wronged, a pitiful victim. They believe all their failures are your fault, so hurting you is only natural.


8

Self-abuse as emotional blackmail

Specifically, sacrificing one's physical, emotional, or mental health by self-harm to make the other person feel guilty, thereby emotionally blackmailing them into staying, complying, or not leaving. Once successful, they transform into a completely different person—whatever extreme, degrading methods they used to please you, they will later use in equally extreme, despicable, and cruel ways to hurt you.


9

Antisocial personality

These individuals are extremely destructive, with very low moral and rule awareness, lack of empathy, and treat people as tools.


10

Impulsive personality

Including aggressive and explosive personalities. They show extreme emotional instability, lack impulse control, and are prone to violent or threatening behaviors—especially when criticized, when their dignity is threatened, when desires are unmet, or when they feel their authority is challenged or ignored.


11

Histrionic personality

For some, domestic violence is actually a form of "performance," intended to attract attention. The more attention they receive, the more excited and fulfilled they feel. These people are highly suggestible and dependent, self-indulgent, inconsiderate of others, extremely self-centered, and prone to extreme emotional outbursts.


12

Rebelliousness, hatred, and revenge mentality

Time flies. Before we know it, another busy year has passed—whether in decline or in passion. In the past year's work, under the leadership of company management, we strictly followed all work procedures, fulfilled our duties conscientiously, and kept our goals clear.


13

Infantile mentality, omnipotent narcissism

Feeling they are the center of the world, expecting their spouse to immediately and accurately fulfill their wishes; treating their partner as personal property, entitled to do as they please; and having never established proper boundaries, being used to immediate and endless gratification.


14

Bullying the weak while fearing the strong, tyrannical at home

Some people appear weak and unimportant outside the home, suffering long-term suppression and accumulating deep anger, which they then vent on family members—because family members are the lowest, even zero-cost, target.


15

"Heroic" or "self-sacrificing" type

These individuals are extremely strict with themselves, have a very strong sense of responsibility, tend to shoulder burdens for others, always consider others, and constantly strive to give and endure. They hope for reciprocation but receive none, even experiencing the grievance and anger of being let down, yet dare not express it. They feel angry, self-blaming, and repressed all at once, until a volcanic eruption occurs.


Final thoughts



Leaving domestic violence is not easy for many women deeply trapped in it, but preserving inner courage and living out your inner strength is the first step toward a new life. Never give up on yourself, never stop trying. Believe that one day, you will surely stand in the brightest place, becoming the best version of yourself!
