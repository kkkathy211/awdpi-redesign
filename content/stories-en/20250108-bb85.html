In recent years, there has been a growing number of news reports on violence against women and girls. Currently, data from at least 161 countries document the prevalence of intimate partner violence. If you would like to learn more about the specific data, please visit UN Women's research and data page

(https://www.unwomen.org/en/what-we-do/ending-violence-against-women/research-and-data) , to further understand the critical role data plays in UN Women's work to prevent and respond to violence against women and girls.



Technology-facilitated violence against women and girls


Due to the lack of a shared, clear definition, technology-facilitated violence lacks comparable data globally. However, existing evidence collected at national and regional levels demonstrates the high prevalence of this phenomenon.


In the European Union countries, as many as one in ten women may have experienced online harassment by age 15, including receiving unwanted explicit sexual emails or text messages, or facing inappropriate and unwanted sexual advances on social networking sites..


A regional study in Arab countries found that approximately 60%women internet users in the region had experienced online violence in the past year.


In 2021, in Uganda, about half of women (49%) reported having experienced online harassment at some point in their lives.


According to a 2016 survey by the Korea National Human Rights Commission, approximately 85%of women had received hate speech while online.


Climate change and violence against women and girls


Climate change and environmental degradation increase the risk of violence against women and girls. Displacement, resource scarcity, food insecurity, and disrupted services for survivors caused by climate change act as triggers.


After Hurricane Katrina in 2005, in Mississippi, United States, cases of rape against women displaced to trailer parks were 53.6 times the annual baseline..


In Ethiopia, an increasing number of girls are being sold into early marriage in exchange for livestock to help families cope with the effects of prolonged drought.


In Nepal, the number of human trafficking victims increased from about 3,000–5,000 per year in 1990 to approximately 12,000–20,000 per year after the 2015 earthquake..


Femicide


In 2022, approximately 48,800 women and girls worldwide were killed by their intimate partners or other family members. This means that every hour, more than five women or girls are killed by their own family members.


In all female homicide cases, 55%were committed by intimate partners or other family members, whereas in all male homicide cases, only 12%were committed by family members..


Violence against women and girls is widespread.


Globally, an estimated 736 million women—nearly one in three—have experienced physical or sexual violence by an intimate partner, sexual violence by a non-partner, or both, at least once in their lifetime (30% of women aged 15 and older). This figure does not include sexual harassment. Compared to women who have not experienced violence, those who have are at higher risk of depression, anxiety, unintended pregnancy, sexually transmitted infections, and HIV. In addition, they face numerous other health problems that may persist long after the violence has ended. Most violence against women is perpetrated by current or former husbands, partners, or intimate partners.


More than 640 million (26%) women aged 15 and older have experienced violence by an intimate partner. Among adolescent girls aged 15–19 who have been in a relationship, almost one in four (24%) have experienced physical or sexual violence by an intimate partner or husband. About 16% of young women aged 15–24 experienced violence in the past year.


The impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on violence against women and girls


 The pandemic has further intensified violence against women and girls (VAWG), while exposing and deepening deep-rooted structural inequalities,


reversing decades of progress made by women in labor market participation. The number of women living in extreme poverty has increased, and the burden of unpaid care and domestic work on women has grown heavier, all of which have increased the risk and driving factors of violence. Since the start of the pandemic in 2021, 45% of women said they or women they know have experienced some form of violence. 70% of women said they believe verbal or physical abuse by partners has become more common. Six in ten women believe sexual harassment in public spaces has become more severe.


Seeking help related to violence


After experiencing violence, fewer than 40% of women seek help.


According to data from some countries, most women who seek help turn to family or friends, while few seek assistance from formal institutions such as the police or health services. Among women who do seek help, fewer than 10% report the violence to the police. Laws on protecting women and girls from


violence At least


162 countries have enacted laws on preventing domestic violence, and 147 countries have laws addressing sexual harassment in the workplace. However, even when such laws exist, they do not always meet international standards and recommendations, nor are they necessarily implemented or enforced. In 2022,


the Republic of the Congo and Côte d'Ivoire adopted laws protecting women from all forms of domestic violence, while the Republic of the Congo, Indonesia, and Jamaica enacted laws against workplace sexual harassment, including criminal penalties and civil remedies. Risk factors influencing violence against women and girls


Between 2016 and 2019, a women's health survey was conducted in five CARICOM member states—Grenada, Guyana, Jamaica, Suriname, and Trinidad and Tobago—


and regional analysis revealed that among women aged 15–64 who have had a partner, if their male partner's attitudes and behaviors consistently reflect male dominance over women, gender inequality in the relationship becomes entrenched, and women in such relationships are more likely to experience partner violence. Controlling women's bodies, autonomy, and contact with others is also closely associated with increased risk of partner violence. The economic cost of violence against women and girls


Violence against women imposes significant losses on nations, societies, and victims. Costs include both direct and indirect, as well as tangible and intangible expenses.


For example, the wages of shelter staff represent a direct tangible cost, borne by victims, perpetrators, governments, and society as a whole. In


Vietnam, out-of-pocket expenses and income losses due to violence against women account for nearly 1.41%of the country's gross domestic product (GDP). More importantly, if productivity losses due to violence are factored in, women who have experienced violence earn 35% less than women who have not experienced abuse, representing another major loss to the national economy. Globally, 6% of women report having experienced sexual violence by a partner or non-partner.


However, given the stigma associated with sexual violence, Shame, Many victims are too ashamed to speak out, so the actual prevalence of non-partner sexual violence may be much higher.


Worldwide, approximately 15 million adolescent girls aged 15–19 have experienced forced sex. In many countries, adolescent girls are highly likely to experience forced sexual acts (forced intercourse or other sexual acts) by a current or former husband, partner, or boyfriend. According to data from 30 countries, only 1% of victims have sought professional help.


Trafficking of women


In 2020, globally, about 4 out of every 10 victims of human trafficking were adult women and 2 were girls. In cases of human trafficking for sexual exploitation, the majority (91%) of victims are women. Analysis based on court cases shows that female victims are three times more likely than males to suffer physical or extreme violence at the hands of traffickers.


Violence against girls


Over the past decade, the global rate of child marriage has declined, with the proportion of women aged 20–24 who were married before age 18 dropping from nearly one in four in 2010 to nearly one in five in 2022 (19%). However, the COVID-19 pandemic has challenged progress on women's rights, and in the next decade, up to 10 million girls may be at risk of child marriage.


Gender-based violence in schools is a major barrier to universal school education and the promotion of girls' right to education. Globally, one in three students aged 11–15 experienced bullying by peers at school at least once in the past month, with girls and boys facing similar likelihoods of being bullied.


Compared to boys, boys are more likely to experience physical bullying, while girls are more likely to suffer psychological bullying and are more frequently subjected to bodily humiliation.


Female genital mutilation


In the 31 countries where female genital mutilation is most concentrated, at least around 200 million women and girls aged 15–49 have undergone female genital mutilation.


In sub-Saharan Africa, one in every four women and girls has undergone female genital mutilation. However, the situation varies greatly between countries.


In some countries, the practice is widespread, with at least nine out of ten girls and women aged 15 to 49 having undergone genital cutting; whereas in Cameroon and Uganda, the proportion of girls and women who have undergone genital mutilation does not exceed 1%..


Violence against women in public life


According to a survey, approximately 82% of female parliamentarians across five regions reported experiencing some form of psychological violence during their tenure, including sexist or insulting remarks, gestures and images, threats, and mob harassment, among others. Women report that social media is a primary channel for such violence, with nearly half (44%) of women reporting that they or their families have received threats of death, rape, assault, or kidnapping. 65% Women have been subjected to sexist remarks, primarily from male colleagues in parliament.


A global survey shows that 73% of female journalists have experienced online violence. Twenty percent reported that they had been attacked or abused offline as a result of online violence. The most common reporting themes associated with increased attacks are gender (49%), followed by politics and elections (44%), and human rights and social policy (31%)..
