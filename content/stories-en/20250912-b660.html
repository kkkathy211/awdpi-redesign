This year marks the fifth anniversary of AWDPI (Asian Women Development Plan International) five years.


Since its founding in 2020, AWDPI has been committed to eliminating gender discrimination and sexual violence against Asian women overseas, building a transnational support and rescue system, and advancing community empowerment and advocacy. Over the past five years, an increasing number of volunteers have joined this warm and determined movement, using companionship and listening to light a glimmer of hope for survivors rebuilding their futures.


In the first two articles of this series, we saw how volunteers remain steadfast in their rescue work, continuously reflect on and reconstruct their own gender awareness, and cherish precious bonds of female solidarity.


This final piece of the series, we interviewed two frontline volunteers who directly interact with survivors of sexual violence. Through their frontline rescue experiences, they illustrate the practical challenges faced in "anti-violence" and "transnational rescue" work, and how they embrace these challenges, transforming the principles and slogans of "anti-violence" into tangible, on-the-ground impact.


Let us continue to draw closer to this group of companions who turn small glimmers of light into a blazing torch. companions.





Wang Leyi, External Liaison, Psychological Support Division

Volunteer for nearly one and a half years




"Finding their true needs through listening and communication."


In my role in external liaison, my core responsibility is to connect with visitors who come to AWDPI seeking help.. Once they are onboarded, we form a team to match them with suitable counselors and accompany them through psychological counseling and support services.


As an external liaison, I first need to understand the visitor's situation, then relay the information to counselors or team members in the psychological support division, helping both sides confirm time and method to ensure services proceed smoothly. Since counselors cannot directly join the visitor's work group, we liaisons serve as a bridge for information transfer, ensuring smooth communication.

Additionally, we participate in cross-regional support projects. For example, after the Moscow terrorist attack, we partnered with local organizations to provide training for volunteers on how to comfort and accompany traumatized students.

In our interactions with visitors, we also conduct a certain level of psychological crisis intervention, though this is not frequent. We typically first assess whether they are at risk of self-harm or suicide, and their potential future behaviors. As a transnational organization, AWDPI does not operate under the legal frameworks of every country and cannot assume responsibility for all cases. Therefore, if the risk is high, we prioritize contacting local professional institutions or referring them to appropriate support channels. During training, we also learn crisis intervention methods so that if a visitor experiences an emergency, we can immediately provide basic intervention and companionship.

Each visitor has different needs.

I once worked with a woman who remained very silent throughout the counseling process. Even when asked if she needed psychological counseling, she said no, so we accompanied her for a period and concluded the case upon closure. Another woman was very clear about what she wanted: she needed psychological counseling, not just emotional listening. Because she had previously worked in psychological support, she had a deep understanding of the counseling process and goals, and could clearly express in which areas she hoped to receive help or improvement. Some visitors have no specific needs for psychological support but make clear requests for legal aid. Visitors' conditions and needs vary greatly; the role of external liaison is precisely to identify their true needs through listening and communication.

Help-seekers have different needs and circumstances. Why do some women show strong resistance when facing violence, while others remain silent? The reasons are complex. Our contact with visitors is often brief and online, making it difficult to fully understand their living environments, but whether they have a supportive social network is crucial. For example, some women have no family abroad and lack friends, and this isolation can lead to despair and a loss of courage to fight back.

The other factor is psychological strength. I once worked with a woman who had not completed her education to the stage society generally recognizes, yet she demonstrated a strong sense of self-protection and willingness to resist. This strength stemmed from the self-awareness she developed during her upbringing and her understanding of women's rights and self-protection. In contrast, those who grew up in families that ignored women's voices often become accustomed to passive acceptance, not knowing they have the right to say "no," and thus have weaker resistance.

Domestic violence is not only physical harm, but also includes psychological oppression and degradation as forms of abuse.

One visitor shared her experience. She was striving to study and settle abroad, but in an intimate relationship, she long endured verbal violence from her partner. He never laid a hand on her, but constantly negated and undermined her sense of worth—her efforts were downplayed or even dismissed as "meaningless." At first, she didn't realize the destructiveness of these words, only feeling increasingly exhausted. Later, she realized this was long-term psychological violence. Her partner gained a sense of superiority through belittling and denial, and her self-esteem was gradually eroded, her emotions sinking lower. Her previously recovering mental state was once again derailed, and she fell into crisis.

Before working with AWDPI, I used to take for granted the question of "whether victims should immediately leave their abusers," believing that as long as domestic violence occurred, one must leave immediately, or else safety could not be ensured. But the reality is far more complex than I imagined. Often, victims are not "unwilling" to leave, but "unable" to leave—this is a forced compromise.

Leaving might mean losing their residency status, being unable to care for their children, or losing their job and property. The common online sentiment that "if you don't leave, you deserve it" is a serious misreading of reality. Victims may not know how to protect themselves through legal or social resources, and their physical condition may not allow direct resistance, so they have no choice but to continue living with the abuser.

The common "black-and-white" judgment overlooks the victim's real situation. True anti-violence work is not about making crude moral judgments, but about understanding, listening, and companionship.

I believe the most fundamental and long-term effective anti-violence approach is education and public awareness. Many people do not change their behavior not because they intentionally do harm, but because they simply don't realize their actions are hurting others. Some grow up in "son-preference" families and communities where they never learn to respect women. Without exposure to correct values and behavioral models, they cannot treat women as equals in real life. The primary task of education is to help more people clearly understand what correct values and behaviors are, to treat women as complete, equal human beings—this should be reflected in every communication, decision, and daily interaction.

Education should focus on at least two aspects: First, continuously dismantling the concept of son-preference and its harms. While public awareness is improving, deeply rooted gender biases still exist in some economically underdeveloped regions, requiring long-term, actionable updates in mindset and consciousness-raising. Second, clearly explaining the definition and scope of "domestic violence.". Public understanding often stops at "physical assault," or even just vague internet terms. In reality, victims can be of any gender. Forms of domestic violence include physical harm, verbal abuse, emotional manipulation, humiliation and degradation, and economic control. Only by clearly identifying which behaviors constitute violence can we recognize, stop, and correct them.

AWDPI has done excellent work overall in domestic violence rescue. Whenever a visitor seeks help, the team quickly forms a cross-departmental working group, and everyone actively and responsibly engages in support. From my observation, the team does its best to assist every visitor in most cases.

Of course, there is room for improvement. For example, I hope we can build a broader and stronger network so that visitors from different countries can more quickly connect with local social work organizations. But visitors are spread across the globe, and achieving full coverage is nearly impossible—this is just my personal ideal.

Additionally, I hope that within AWDPI's counseling framework, counselors could offer more free sessions. Many visitors have limited financial means. Although charging two or three hundred RMB per hour is already a significant contribution from counselors, visitors may still be unable to afford it. AWDPI cannot compel counselors to offer more free services, so this remains an idealistic suggestion.

Overall, I believe the AWDPI team is doing its best to provide optimal support under current conditions. How to maximize resources and create safer spaces for visitors within real-world constraints is a challenge we must face together in the future.





Gou Gou, Case Coordinator

Volunteer for nearly one and a half years




"We cannot immediately change the entire system,

but we can drive long-term change through accumulating individual cases."


The case coordination process begins when a help-seeker contacts our online assistant. The assistant is the primary contact when someone first reaches out to the organization, conducting an initial understanding of the help-seeker's needs while providing a safe space for them to speak.


When a case meets AWDPI's eligibility criteria—for example, the person is an overseas Chinese woman who has experienced violence against women—we sign a service agreement with the help-seeker. Then, the case coordinator conducts an initial assessment to determine what support resources—psychological, legal, or language empowerment—should be assigned to the client.

During the process of specific support groups providing services to clients, case coordinators accompany the client over the long term, monitor her condition, and conduct crisis intervention when necessary. Overall, the role of a case coordinator is more like a front desk—ensuring that the person seeking help can smoothly access appropriate services and receive continuous support and protection throughout the service process.

When I first started working in this field, I felt a strong sense of helplessness in the face of reality. When reading many social news stories, I often wondered: why is the world like this? It seemed that no matter what an individual did, they couldn't change the current situation. At that time, I hoped to find a "leverage point" through which I could make the world a little better by my efforts.

But after working in this role for a while, my mindset changed. The sense of helplessness didn't completely disappear, but I began to realize that many structural issues that cause helplessness—such as gender inequality—have persisted for thousands of years and are difficult to change in the short term. I gradually let go of the idea of fighting these large-scale problems directly, and instead shifted my focus to accompanying and helping specific individuals. I became more concerned with how to help these people live better in this world, rather than getting stuck on the problems themselves or worrying about future directions.

We cannot change the entire system immediately, but we can promote long-term change through the accumulation of individual cases.

In our work, the core is actually resource linkage. Many clients, due to language barriers and being in a state of high stress, often lack the mental capacity to think: "What resources can I access now? Who can help me?" At this point, we step in to tell them which organizations they can contact, what kind of assistance these organizations can provide, and offer translation and referral services, or accompany them through the entire help-seeking process. These concrete actions are exactly what we do every day.

At the same time, from the client's perspective, we also help them understand: falling into difficulty is not their fault, but the result of a large-scale structural problem. In social work terminology, this is called "externalizing the problem," which reduces self-blame, alleviates their tendency to over-reflect on the past, and empowers them psychologically.

To give a concrete example: There was once a client living in a European country who initially held optimistic expectations about the local legal system and anti-domestic violence resources. But when she tried to seek help, she found many resources were unavailable to her, and even some public authorities showed bias towards the perpetrator when handling her complaint. For a time, she attributed the problem to herself, thinking it might be due to insufficient language skills or her inability to make others understand her predicament.
Although we couldn't directly resolve the systemic barriers she faced, we helped her analyze the situation and understand that these difficulties were not her fault. As a woman in a foreign country, encountering prejudiced attitudes or institutional discrimination is a real and existing challenge. When the client realized this, her self-blame decreased, and she was able to face the difficulties more calmly, rather than putting excessive pressure on herself or stubbornly pushing through. This kind of companionship and understanding often stabilizes their psychological state more effectively than immediate intervention.

My primary profession is that of a lawyer. Comparing AWDPI's online case work with legal case intervention, you can see there are significant differences. Cases handled by lawyers often have already entered formal legal procedures— the client has reported the incident, the help-seeking process has started, the case has moved offline, and state agencies such as the police and courts have begun to intervene. In contrast, AWDPI clients are often still in the exploratory stage—they are unsure of what to do, possibly hesitating or testing the waters, with no clear plan for future actions.

Therefore, online support is more companion-based: we provide language support, emotional support, help clients clarify their thoughts, and map out feasible action paths. In comparison, lawyers handling real-world cases are more like the main drivers pushing the case forward..

In daily work, our roles are also more focused. A lawyer may need to communicate simultaneously with the client, the abuser, and external government agencies. In AWDPI, I mostly engage in deep connection with the client. Through this connection, we can continuously accompany her, help her analyze her difficulties, and provide psychological and informational support.

Interestingly, I found that the difference between real-life clients and those seeking help online is not as large as I once imagined. At first, I thought online clients, being overseas, usually had higher education backgrounds and stronger information-searching abilities, so their requests would be very clear; while offline clients might have lower education levels and average incomes, appearing more vulnerable. But in actual cases, regardless of their background, the resilience and ability to resist injustice shown by clients were remarkably similar.

They all push their cases forward in their own ways, and continue to process and endure the stress when facing various negative feedback during the process. Their external circumstances may differ, but their core is the same: all are doing their best to protect their rights and seek better support and protection..

My role as a volunteer at AWDPI has greatly influenced my perspective. In the past, I might have emphasized the value of action more, always thinking that taking action was the only sign of being proactive or making progress. But now, I've begun to understand and respect the client's pace—even when they choose not to act or choose to hesitate.

Past experience made me feel I understood the case process better than the client, but now I've learned to place myself in a more humble position. I cannot possibly understand her situation better than she does, nor can I fully grasp how painful the trauma she has suffered truly is. All I can do is accompany her, understand her, and respect her choices.

I can't say I completely accept all of the client's decisions with total calm—sometimes, when taking on a case, I still have certain expectations about the outcome. But at least in actual service and communication, I try my best to avoid making the client feel pressured or that I have ulterior motives. I use case service techniques and language to contain my own expectations, so they don't affect the client's feelings.

There is currently a certain degree of misunderstanding about domestic violence in society. In the comment sections of some online content, you often see very male-centered views, such as "She must have done something wrong to get beaten." Behind such views lies a bias that implicitly treats men as the subject and women as subordinate. Although our anti-domestic violence work, both domestically and internationally, has been ongoing for many years, and in actual service almost no one explicitly says "domestic violence is a private family matter, don't report it," invisible biases still exist, making me realize that many people in reality actually lack a gender perspective.

For example, when handling cases of women experiencing domestic violence, we pay attention to gender-specific challenges such as women's roles in the family, parenting responsibilities, and visa issues. But there are always people who say, "This isn't unique to women—men can also experience domestic violence."

This view may sound objectively reasonable at first, but in fact, it ignores gender differences. According to UN statistics, approximately 90% of domestic violence victims are women. Equalizing the issue actually obscures the specific vulnerability of women in domestic violence. This attitude of denying and downplaying gender factors reduces people's sensitivity to gender when handling cases.

Such statements, while seemingly neutral on the surface, are actually a means of silencing women under patriarchal narratives. They make it harder for women to be understood and supported when suffering violence, and cause society to overlook gender-specific issues that require targeted intervention. Applying a gender perspective to understand and support victims is not only a professional necessity but also a key to protecting their rights.
