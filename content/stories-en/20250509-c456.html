On April 30, 2025, the Asian Women Development Plan International (AWDPI) Australia National Office successfully hosted an interview event in Melbourne on the theme of Asian women's leadership. Annisa Zhang, the first Chinese Australian Deputy Mayor of Glen Eira City Council in Australia, Zhang Li (Annisa Li Zhang) , engaged in an in-depth dialogue with Cindy Gui Jiaze, founder and head of AWDPI Australia, attracting over 50 online and offline participants to jointly focus on Cindy Gui Jiaze the breakthroughs and growing influence of Asian women in the public sphere. The event opened with Annisa Zhang's personal journey, from her background as a first-generation Chinese immigrant to her path to becoming a city councillor, extending to the original intentions and reflections behind her book *In the Town Hall*. The interview delved into.



how Asian women can break stereotypes and integrate multiple identities within multicultural societies, and actively promote community development through participation in public affairs. The interactive session, both online and offline, sparked sincere exchanges around topics such as identity, Asian women's leadership, community governance, and multiculturalism, illustrating the unique value and immense potential of Asian women in diverse societies.. "Stepping Forward"



01


At the beginning of the event, Annisa Zhang noted that although gender parity (50:50) was achieved in the 2024 Glen Eira City Council elections, the representation of Asian communities across the city's councils remains significantly lower than their population share. When the identities of "Asian" and "woman" overlap, the representational challenges faced by this group become even more complex and profound:


They must not only break through the glass ceiling of gender, but also fight for their own space to speak out amid cultural differences, language barriers, and social stereotypes. She As a first-generation Chinese woman who immigrated to Australia in her thirties, Annisa candidly shared that she had also faced a.


"double bind"—the dual challenges of language barriers and cultural differences—during her political journey. She recalled, "Language was a huge challenge, and Eastern culture values subtlety and restraint. We didn't have debate or public speaking training back home, yet these are essential skills in politics." Her words resonated deeply with the audience, both online and offline, especially touching international students, new immigrants, and young professionals—those who quietly navigate identity in multicultural societies, a reality they know all too well. To overcome her language limitations, Annisa continues to listen to English morning broadcasts and read English newspapers daily, constantly refining her communication skills. Yet, as her political experience grew, she gradually realized:



"Perfect English isn't the core. What matters is whether I can use it to bring the unique perspectives of Asian communities—and their unspoken needs—into decision-making spaces." True voice isn't about accent or pronunciation, but about the courage to "step forward" and break the silence, creating possibilities for marginalized groups to be seen, understood, and represented. Her practice reminds us that in multicultural societies, the identity of Asian women should not be a barrier, but can become a unique channel for voice and change. You don't need to wait until you're perfect to participate, nor wait to be invited to speak. Every attempt to step forward opens space for more of "us," making our presence clearer, firmer, and indispensable. "Caring about politics




02


is caring about yourself."

"What does a city council actually do?" "What work does a councillor perform?" Facing many audience members who felt politics was distant, Annisa used a vivid and relatable analogy to make the abstract structure of city councils concrete. "If you imagine the city council as a company, councillors are like board members responsible for decision-making." From long-term city planning and budgeting to creating local laws affecting daily life—such as smoking bans, tree removal regulations, and pet management rules—she emphasized,


"Councillors are elected vote by vote by city residents, not appointed by the government." This explanation not only made the structure and decision-making processes of local government intuitive and accessible, but also sparked a personal realization among many: "I actually have the right and the opportunity to influence the community I live in." For many Asian women, politics often feels like "someone else's business." Due to language anxiety, cultural differences, or the "obedience" and "silence is golden" values instilled during upbringing, we often default to being observers in public affairs. Yet Annisa's words reminded 


us: You are here, you are living, and therefore you have the right to speak and the right to participate.: For us Asian women, engaging in public affairs isn't distant. Speaking up doesn't have to begin with standing on a stage or becoming a leader—it might quietly begin with carefully reading a questionnaire sent by the city government, or gathering the courage to write down one suggestion. It is these seemingly small actions that quietly redefine who gets to represent us, and gradually reshape our understanding of who we are. The courage to "stay in the room"



03


Reflecting on her two election campaigns, Annisa admitted, "Running for office is extremely draining." But what kept her going was an unavoidable sense of responsibility:


"If I don't run again, there will be no Chinese Australian representative on our city council." This determination allowed many audience members to feel the gentle yet resilient strength within her.  During the pandemic, a single phone call made her understand even more deeply


the meaning of "staying in the room." "Compared to being a popular councillor, I choose to be a responsible one." An English-speaking Chinese elderly person, suffering from neighborhood discrimination and in distress, called the only Chinese Australian councillor on the council—Annisa. "I comforted them in Chinese. They felt comforted and reassured. That's when I realized my presence as a Chinese Australian in the town hall is itself a form of power." The sound of fluent, familiar accents echoing in the town hall is in itself a remedy against fear. "Only by understanding and participating can we gain a voice; with a voice, ideals can become reality."


A comment from an online participant captured the deep resonance behind this story. For many Asian women living in Australia, the courage to "stay in the room" is not just persistence in the face of the external world, but also an inward affirmation— We belong here, and we deserve to be seen and heard. "If I don't write, no one else will"



04


" During the event, Annisa's book


*In the Town Hall*became a highlight. Written from the first-person perspective of a Chinese Australian female city councillor, the book documents her observations and experiences both inside and outside the town hall—from sharp debates at the council table to everyday community concerns. Between the lines flows the rhythm of Melbourne's seasons and the authentic warmth of immigrant life. She said writing the book was not only for documentation, but also because "it's interesting." To her, interesting things matter deeply, because they provoke thought, evoke emotional resonance, and even quietly drive change. When asked by Cindy how she balances multiple roles—councillor, writer, mother, daughter, and rugby team captain—Annisa admitted that "balance" itself is a false concept.


"I don't strive for perfection in every area. Different priorities are needed at different stages." Sometimes family takes precedence, sometimes career. In the long run, I actually come closer to the balance I want. Writing is not just recording words, but affirming one's own existence. 


Through her writing, the town hall no longer feels distant, participation no longer abstract, and the voices and identities of Asian women no longer marginal. You can be imperfect, but authentic; you don't need to control everything, but you can make active choices; you don't always need to be in the spotlight, but you always have the right to speak. Because,


"If I don't write, no one else will." If you're not present, the story will never be complete. " Conclusion

 


Seeing the future through dialogue


This conversation not only showcased the resilience of an immigrant woman breaking through barriers, but also ignited confidence among Asian women to engage in public affairs. Annisa's experiences and reflections remind us that


when we embrace our multiple identities with unique perspectives, limitations can be transformed into potential. Through sharing and listening, we see each other, and we also see broader possibilities.


The AWDPI Asian Women Development Plan International Australia National Office will continue to build authentic and powerful dialogue platforms, empowering more Asian women to write their own chapters in overseas societies. Text & Photos | Jiaze Gui & Rong Li, AWDPI Australia National Office
