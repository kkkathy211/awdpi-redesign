A woman's life is often quietly shaped by certain "subtle" moments—ones that feel exaggerated to speak up about, yet impossible to let go of.


During adolescence, elders tease you about your body, classmates joke about your bangs; dismissed as harmless banter, yet bordering on offense, and no one ever taught you how to push back.

When choosing college majors, doors are shut in your face under the guise of "what's best for you," leaving only narrow paths like teaching, nursing, or "female-friendly" jobs, as if to say: this is all you're fit for.

In the workplace, unacknowledged dirty work—buying coffee, ordering lunch, serving as background at dinners—disproportionately falls on women's shoulders.

Mothers who quit jobs to raise children manage over forty household tasks, enduring a "second shift" followed by an invisible "third shift" of emotional labor, only to be met with society's collective amnesia and silence.


These offenses share a common structure: They wear the cloak of goodwill, making it hard to speak out; they occur within power-imbalanced relationships, leaving no place to seek justice; they target your identity, not your ability, repeatedly pushing you away from possibilities.


Why, in the moment of being offended, is our first instinct not to fight back, but to question ourselves? Why are verbal degradations so hard to recognize and counter?


Today, AWDPI's official podcast "etVoice" officially launches. In our first episode, we are honored to welcome Associate Professor Pei Yuxin, PhD supervisor from the Department of Sociology and Social Work at Sun Yat-sen University, to discuss those offenses we once couldn't voice, and the rebuttals we deserved.


And the most direct thing she said was: Anything that makes you feel uncomfortable is a form of offense. The skill of pushing back is not being confrontational—it's self-protection.


The words we couldn't say back then—now it's still not too late.

 Pei Yuxin  Associate Professor and PhD supervisor in the Department of Sociology and Social Work at Sun Yat-sen University. A long-time researcher in women's studies and social work, she is the author of *Desiring the City: A Study of Shanghai Women Born in the 1970s* and leads the National Social Science Fund project "Research on Female Entrepreneurship and Marital Dilemmas under the Digital Economy." The name "etVoice" carries three meanings.


"et" references the traditional Chinese narrative of "men plow, women weave"—we want to confront this history, and grow new possibilities from it.


"et" also means weaving: our strength is interlaced thread by thread, each voice a strand.


"etVoice" sounds like "zhīyīn" (a soulmate)—we hope to hear your voice, and become your zhīyīn.





[About AWDPI]




etVoice is the official podcast of AWDPI.


AWDPI Asian Women Development Plan International (Asian Women Development Plan International) is an international nonprofit organization dedicated to eliminating discrimination and gender-based violence against Asian women living overseas, and promoting their empowerment and development, so that overseas Asian women can gain greater opportunities in social, economic, cultural, and educational fields, and transform from a marginalized group into a vital force for social progress.


We operate in multiple countries and regions across Europe, North America, and the Asia-Pacific, with National Offices established in the UK, Belgium, the USA, Thailand, and Australia. We have built a broad network of partnerships with various sectors, including United Nations system organizations, relevant government departments, international organizations, multinational corporations, and academic institutions.
