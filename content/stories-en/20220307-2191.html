Each year, International Women's Day releases a theme related to women. The 2022 theme was "Break the Bias" #BreaktheBias— those committed to eliminating bias, breaking stereotypes, and rejecting gender discrimination can strike the #BreaktheBias pose—arms crossed in front of the chest—to say "no" to all forms of bias.



So, how can we break bias?

Besides personal courage, support from the external environment is essential.


In the process of growing up, women, to varying degrees, encounter biases stemming from their gender—they face stereotypes in education, gender discrimination in the workplace, differential treatment in life due to age, appearance, and weight, and are often constrained by traditional notions like gender roles within the family. Even when a woman suffers domestic violence, as a victim, she still has to fight against various prejudices. Amidst these multiple layers of hardship, Chinese women living overseas also have to confront discrimination and unfair treatment arising from their own race, nationality, status, language, and culture.



 

      

Sally is a Chinese woman living in the Netherlands who filed for divorce due to her husband's domestic violence. During the divorce process, because of her limited Dutch language skills, Sally faced discrimination from her lawyer, who not only treated her poorly but also provided no substantive assistance. As a result, she had to hire another lawyer who could communicate in English, at a consultation fee of 250 euros per hour (approximately 1,980 RMB). Besides lawyers, officials such as judges and staff from child protection agencies—those who should uphold impartiality and reject bias—also treated Sally differently because of her race, skin color, and inability to communicate in Dutch.


On the other hand, although Sally won custody of her children, her ex-husband still had visitation rights. This meant that after the divorce, Sally still had to interact with him and endured text message harassment and verbal abuse from his family. They even slandered Sally in front of the children, calling her a "thief." For the sake of her children, Sally could not "confront him (her ex-husband) with a stronger attitude," and could only maintain a superficially acceptable situation.


Although these events occurred several years ago and Sally has gradually moved past the shadow, this experience made her deeply understand the importance of "finding companions, finding organizations." The Avoice Overseas Chinese Women Protection Project (Against Violence to Overseas Chinese Women Program, hereinafter referred to as Avoice) provided Sally with post-legal assistance and support. Sally also joined Avoice, using her experience and energy to help more overseas Chinese women who have suffered harm.


For overseas Chinese women, multiple challenges such as language and culture, unfamiliar environments, and foreign status make them more vulnerable to various forms of bias. Sally's case particularly highlights this—her immigrant or foreign background made her subject to discrimination; language barriers forced her to pay higher legal fees; and the unfamiliar environment led her to choose endurance over confrontation when facing conflict.


Even "motherhood"—society's long-standing expectations of mothers—in a sense constitutes a bias that trapped Sally. Her emotional attachment to her child, her constant worries, her "sacrifice" and endurance, became opportunities for the abuser to attack her.


In Sally's story, her good education and professional work were among the key factors enabling her to successfully assert her rights. Without income, Sally would have struggled to afford the lawyer's fees—she might have needed much more time to fight for her rights, or might not have been able to divorce at all, remaining trapped in the shadow of domestic violence. One of Sally's female friends is exactly such a case. After marrying a Dutch man, she became a full-time housewife. She suffered physical abuse from her husband but endured it for the sake of her child. Her husband took away her passport and imprisoned her at home, yet due to language barriers, she could not seek help; worse, because of financial dependence, she had to give up custody of her children.



For a long time, society's undervaluation of domestic labor and rigid expectations of the roles of wife and mother—such traditional ideas that women should be obedient, self-sacrificing for the family—have all created biases that trap victims of domestic violence in an inescapable net, making it difficult or impossible to flee. Fortunately, more and more organizations and institutions are beginning to notice the difficulties women face.


The original purpose of establishing Avoice was to help overseas Chinese women who suffer domestic violence—providing language support, legal support, psychological support, community support, and building a multilingual database of rescue networks across countries to help overseas Chinese women find appropriate local assistance organizations. In addition, Avoice listens to the experiences of domestic violence survivors, records their true feelings, and shares their self-rescue experiences and methods, ensuring victims' voices are not buried.


"Wen," who lives in Belgium, once suffered severe domestic violence, including being beaten by her partner while pregnant. In April and May 2020,



Wen got to know Avoice. In a mutual aid group for overseas Chinese in Belgium, she also "met many women and heard their stories… all kinds of experiences. It turns out that all these unfair treatments—you've experienced them, I've experienced them, she's experienced them too." In this space, women help each other, listen to each other, and heal each other. As mentioned earlier, fighting bias requires not only courage but also support from the external environment. The Avoice community has made many women realize:


they are a community of shared destiny. The experiences of Sally, Sally's friend, and Wen—the bias, discrimination, and unequal treatment they faced when seeking help—have already, are currently, or may in the future happen to every woman. Women are vulnerable when facing bias; yet at the same time, they are powerful, because on the path of "breaking bias," they are never alone.



 In January 2015, the Stanford sexual assault case shocked the United States. The victim, using the pseudonym "Emily," bravely spoke out against the unjust court ruling. Influenced by this case, California removed the presiding judge and reformed its sexual assault legislation. In 2019, "Emily" published the book *Know My Name*, no longer hiding behind a pseudonym, but declaring to everyone—she is Chanel Miller, a Chinese American girl, whose Chinese name is Zhang Xiaoxia.


"

Besides shedding her pseudonym, Miller also detailed in the book the assault itself, the long and complex evidence collection and legal proceedings, and every subtle psychological, emotional, and detailed change after the trauma. For survivors, repeatedly recalling how the harm occurred, analyzing emotional fluctuations, and examining inner changes undoubtedly cause immense psychological and physical strain. Yet Miller chose to do so anyway—her words carry unstoppable power, because


she is not merely recording her own story, but speaking for all "voiceless" survivors. As women, we share similar fates and constantly feel the omnipresent bias.


However, fortunately, we have role models like Chanel Miller, Eileen Gu, and Zhang Guimei—they have broken through the barriers of bias and brought courage and hope to all women. "Contribute your share of warmth, emit your bit of light—even like a firefly, you can shine in the darkness." When gathered, these fireflies can ultimately become a torch, illuminating all of humanity. Text|
