In the practice of combating gender-based violence and supporting Asian women, continuous learning and professional development are key to ensuring our team delivers high-quality, responsive services. From late 2024 to early 2025, the AWDPI Chicago, U.S. Office organized a three-part in-depth training series on domestic violence for AWDPI internal members. We extend our gratitude to all members of the Chicago, U.S. Office, and sincerely thank Wu Tiantian, Director of the U.S. Office, and Qu Jihan, Director of the Chicago, U.S. Office, for their strong support and dedicated collaboration in making this training possible.


This training series was made possible with generous support from Kan-Win, a local Chicago nonprofit dedicated to combating domestic violence. It was co-facilitated by Abbey Zhu (she/they), Kan-Win's Community Engagement Director, and Malien Tingpalpon (she/her), Kan-Win's Education and Outreach Coordinator. Aligned with the U.S. standard "40-Hour Domestic Violence Training," the series was open to team members and core volunteers, offering systematic learning and capacity-building on key issues in domestic violence advocacy.



Training Series Overview



1


•  The distinction and connection between gender-based violence (GBV) and domestic violence: Domestic violence encompasses not only physical harm but also verbal, psychological, economic control, and technology-facilitated abuse (such as cyberstalking and revenge pornography), among other forms.

•  The control mechanisms of domestic violence: Domestic violence is not caused by: genetics, mental illness, loss of control, alcohol or drug use, anger management issues, communication or relationship problems, poverty, the survivor's behavior, or the perpetrator's low self-esteem. Rather, domestic violence is a learned, intentional behavior—a manifestation of the perpetrator's desire to gain and maintain power and control over the survivor.

•  Forms of violence in adolescent and higher education contexts: Special emphasis was placed on the hidden yet destructive nature of "academic abuse" and "technology-facilitated abuse" among youth populations.


2


•  Language sensitivity and survivor support principles: Participants were encouraged to use recovery-oriented language such as "survivor" instead of "victim," and introduced to the LISTEN support model.

￮ L: Listen without judgement
￮ I: Inform them about their options and resources

￮ S: Show your support by being there for them

￮ T: Trust and believe what they're saying

 ￮  E: Empower them by respecting their decisions

 ￮ N: Never break confidentiality

• Structural oppression and intersectional understanding: By introducing concepts such as "racialized misogyny" and "state violence," the training guided participants to understand the systemic factors behind individual experiences.

￮ Racialized misogyny: Institutional and interpersonal hatred, prejudice, and oppression toward women, resulting from systems of oppression. It refers to the economic, political, gendered, and social oppression experienced by Black, Asian, Arab, Indigenous, and Latinx women, as well as other racialized groups.

• Principles of sexual consent (FRIES): Freely Given, Reversible, Informed, Enthusiastic, Specific.

  ￮ Why does "consent" become complicated—or even impossible—under systems of oppression? Within systemic oppression, the concept of consent is not always clear or truly voluntary. Oppressive systems grant more power to some individuals through differences in gender, race, immigration status, and economic position, while simultaneously stripping others of the ability to make choices or say "no." Structurally, oppression is systemic power and control; in personal relationships, gender-based violence is the manifestation of this power in specific interactions. Therefore, when individuals are in unequal power dynamics—such as dependence on visas, economic support, language access, legal status, or social resources—even apparent "consent" is often driven by lack of alternatives, fear, or survival needs. In such cases, so-called "consent" is distorted by structural oppression, making genuine, autonomous choice extremely difficult, if not impossible.


3


• Unique barriers faced by immigrant survivors: Dependency on the abuser for immigration status, citizenship/residency issues, language barriers (in accessing domestic violence services, counseling, shelters, or case management), fear of ICE or police, lack of access to public benefits, misunderstanding or failure to recognize domestic violence, anti-immigrant legislation, hate, and public prejudice, feelings of shame, guilt, and social pressure, identity struggles and intergenerational trauma, difficulty navigating the legal system

• Limitations of the criminal justice system: Fear of deportation or arrest by police

• Legal relief measures:

  ￮  Order of Protection: Available to victims of violence within a family or intimate relationship. Provides remedies such as no-contact orders, property protection, and financial support.

￮ Civil No Contact Order (CNCO): Available to survivors of sexual assault who do not have a specific relationship with the perpetrator.

  ￮ Stalking No Contact Order (SNCO): Available to survivors of stalking, requiring proof of at least two incidents.

 ￮ Firearm Restraining Order (FRO): Temporarily removes firearms and ammunition from the perpetrator; must be requested by a family member or law enforcement agency.

• Immigration-related legal relief

￮ VAWA (Violence Against Women Act): Allows spouses, parents, or children abused by U.S. citizens or permanent residents to self-petition for immigration status. Requires proof of a bona fide marriage; approval rate is low (30%), and processing takes 2–3 years.

￮ U Visa: For crime victims who cooperate with law enforcement investigations. Limited to 10,000 per year, grants work authorization and eligibility for a green card.

 ￮ T Visa: For victims of human trafficking who report to law enforcement. Limited to 5,000 per year, with an approval rate of 23%.

• Safety planning

 ￮ Risk assessment: Using danger assessment tools to identify patterns of violence.

￮ Key questions:

▪ Who can help in an emergency?

 ▪ What are safe locations (e.g., a friend's home, public spaces)?

 ▪ How to escape or intervene (e.g., packing an emergency bag, contacting a shelter)?

▪ Phased planning: Immediate, short-term, and long-term action plans.


This training series closely followed the core framework of the U.S. "40-Hour Domestic Violence Training," a foundational and advanced course offered by many professional service agencies for new advocates and service providers. Through systematic learning, AWDPI team members deepened their understanding of the mechanisms of domestic violence and acquired practical tools and response strategies for supporting survivors, significantly enhancing our professionalism and cultural sensitivity in community work.


We believe that only by building a team grounded in empathy, professional competence, and cultural understanding can AWDPI effectively fulfill its mission, providing truly accessible, tangible, and sustainable support to Asian women, especially survivors of domestic violence and gender discrimination.


We sincerely thank the Kan-Win team for their passionate commitment throughout this training series, and all participants for their dedicated engagement and active interaction. It is precisely the focus and resonance of every participant that transformed this series from mere knowledge transfer into a process of shared understanding and the beginning of collective action.


We also extend our gratitude once again to all members of the AWDPI Chicago, U.S. Office, and express our heartfelt appreciation to Wu Tiantian, Director of the U.S. Office, and Qu Jihan, Director of the Chicago, U.S. Office, for their strong support and collaborative efforts in this training.


Although the training has come to an end, our learning will not stop. Moving forward, AWDPI will continue to launch more thematic trainings and dialogue spaces for both internal members and the public, and we look forward to having more partners join us in walking side by side on the path toward promoting love without violence.
