Abstract

On May 25, AWDPI joined AAAAH CULTURE, Accent Sisters, and a group of nonprofit organizations and artists to co-host Tap Tap Fest, a spring cultural festival in New York's Chinatown. In the afternoon, we set up a booth in Columbus Park, engaging face-to-face with the public. Our orange-and-white booth under the sun drew the attention of many young people concerned about mental health, legal rights, and women's rights, who paused to listen, share, and connect.


This was not just an exhibition, but an invitation—to draw closer, to listen to the voices of Asian women. In the evening, we watched the film *Chinatown Cha-Cha* together, and in the aunts' dance steps, we saw the authentic exchanges and gentle strength present in every street encounter.

We look forward to meeting you again at the next street corner.


[In Chinatown, we set up a booth]


5 May 25, New York's Chinatown, 2 p.m.

Sunlight poured down between the tall buildings, falling onto the red brick pavement at the street corner. At the intersection of Mulberry and Baxter streets, crowds gradually gathered. The wind fluttered festival banners and gently lifted the corners of our flyers on the table.


This is Tap Tap Fest the scene of a spring cultural festival uniquely belonging to Chinatown. AAAAH CULTURE, together with Accent Sisters and a group of nonprofit organizations and artists, invited people to stroll through a design market, enjoy live performances, grab some snacks, and take retro-yet-trendy street photos with friends—where urban vibrancy met everyday ease. The entire neighborhood seemed to have cracked open a seam for spring, letting light and people pour in together.


And we—AWDPI—were here too, claiming our own small space. We set up an orange-and-white booth. A few coffee cups printed with "WOMEN" were stacked into a small tower; canvas bags lay open like canvases, glowing softly in the sunlight. Orange-red ribbons swayed gently in the wind. From not far away came the sound of live music—voices, drumbeats, and the scent of street food weaving through the air, lively yet relaxed.


Amid the bustling crowd, we immersed ourselves in the spirit of spring, while also hoping to scatter warmth and kindness into the breeze. In the past, our voices were mostly found in text, on screens. But this time, we stepped into a Chinatown street corner, placing our booth in the sunlight, turning every introduction into an invitation—to draw more people closer, to hear one another, to get involved.


[Every act of approaching is a response]


That afternoon, many people paused at our booth.

A cool-looking psychology student from NYU, carrying a black-pink backpack, walked over. After hearing our introduction, she nodded and said she had also participated in some mental healing-related activities. In a calm yet firm tone, she said: "Many people just need a place where they can speak up."

In her eyes, we saw a quiet but powerful certainty. Later, she returned and chatted with us at length, expressing her hope to collaborate with us in the future.

A law student with a camera slung over her shoulder took several photos in front of our booth. She listened attentively, took notes, and asked seriously how our events were organized. We had a great conversation and eventually exchanged contact information. "I've been looking for opportunities to do something meaningful," she said, "what you're doing seems to be exactly what I've been searching for."


Another woman who does feminist stand-up comedy brought several friends after learning about our organization. We watched her introduce AWDPI to others while picking up our flyers and reading them carefully. At that moment, though not standing behind a microphone, her sharing was still powerful. We believe she was helping more people hear our message in her own way.


Throughout the afternoon, people kept coming and going. Women from all walks of life—some stayed for deep conversations, some quietly took notes, some stood beside us with their friends. They spoke differently, stayed for different lengths of time. But they all paused. They all came closer.

In that warm, sun-drenched afternoon, our table—displaying coffee cups and canvas bags—slowly transformed under the sunlight. It was no longer just a promotional booth for an organization, but a small platform:


A place where people set aside their identities and backgrounds, placing their thoughts and hopes under the sun, like handing in a note of trust.

Sometimes, it was us doing the talking; more often, it was us listening—listening to their hopes for society, their desire for change, their longing for gentler, stronger connections among one another.


That entire afternoon, the wind fluttered the corners of canvas bags, light fell on paper pages. Some conversations were left unfinished, but hearts had already drawn near—some moments of closeness were fleeting, yet they left a lasting impression in our hearts.

[When night fell, the light remained]


At the end of the event, we watched together the documentary*Women's World: Chinatown Cha-Cha*.

The film tells the story of the aunts in Chinatown—how they dance, live, gather together, and collectively resist loneliness. In the interplay of light and shadow, we saw them slowly turn on familiar streets, swaying to the rhythm—not performing for anyone, but stretching freely for themselves.


At that moment, standing among the crowd, we suddenly felt a bit dazed. Because the scenes in the film mirrored the people we had met during the day—some paused for just a few seconds, some sat down for long conversations; some quietly filled out questionnaires, others simply patted our shoulders and said, "You're great."


When the aunts on screen smiled, danced, and gathered together, we suddenly realized that these moments of closeness, these conversations, these small connections happening on the streets today, were also a kind of present-day "cha-cha."

We once thought we came to set up a booth, but later realized—we came to receive stories. Perhaps we even caught a few words that had never been spoken before, but were finally said.
