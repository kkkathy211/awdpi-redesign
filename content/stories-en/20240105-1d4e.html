01


In the second half of life,

I came to the United States to work as a home care worker.




Five years ago, 50-year-old Wendy came from China to the United States, beginning her career as a home care worker while also accompanying her child, who was studying at a U.S. university.


"My son and I can take care of each other," said Wendy. "I can also earn an income through my own labor."



Wendy now works in a Chinese household, where her main daily responsibility is caring for elderly individuals with limited mobility and difficulty in daily living. Each week, Wendy works four days at her employer's home, handling a wide range of detailed tasks: preparing breakfast at 7:30 a.m., cleaning—including mopping and vacuuming—from 8 a.m. to 11 a.m., and preparing both lunch and dinner. After dinner, she waits for her employer to finish washing up before cleaning the bathroom, not finishing her workday until 8 p.m.


From Monday to Thursday, in order to care for her employer who lives alone, Wendy must reside in the employer's home. "The boundary between work and rest becomes very blurred," Wendy said. "Private life can easily be consumed by work at any moment." This far exceeds the legal standard of a 7- to 8-hour workday.


Thursday is Wendy's busiest day, when she has no time at all for a lunch break. "I need to prepare lunch and dinner for the next three days on Thursdays."



Compared to home care workers employed by medical institutions, those like Wendy who work directly in private homes do not go through lengthy or strict hiring procedures, nor do they perform basic, highly repetitive auxiliary medical tasks. However, their jobs come with many soft requirements—such as cooking, laundry, and grocery shopping—making their role more akin to that of a full-time live-in housekeeper.


Yet, since most employers are elderly, comprehensive care from home care workers does not necessarily lead to extra bonuses. Wendy admitted: This is a long-term job that demands immense physical and mental energy.. "


Ms. Deng, a head nurse at a U.S. government hospital, said: "For new immigrants, home care work is a relatively accessible occupation." She noted that while nursing is a highly attractive profession in the United States, offering substantial income—potentially reaching middle-class levels—home care work, despite differing from nursing by only one character in Chinese, is vastly different in terms of income and benefits..


In contrast, home care work has no educational requirements and no age restrictions. For many new immigrants aged 40 to 60, it is a relatively accessible job that can support a family. New immigrants can choose to take and pass a home care worker certification exam before employment, or start working directly, performing basic, repetitive care tasks under the supervision of a nurse.



But for Wendy, job accessibility, career development, and supporting her family are only part of the equation. As a home care worker employed directly by a family and working far beyond the average hours, Wendy's life choice once again reflects her deep belief in "family."



Though exhausting, "I still want to care for the elderly this way. My mother passed away when she was only 60—this remains a lasting pain in my heart." Regretting that she was unable to care for her parents when they were alive, Wendy has since become especially attentive to elderly individuals who are lonely, frail, and often overlooked by younger generations.

 

"By this stage in life, elders have gone through countless trials. Their words and minds become more open and generous. Being with them, I gain so much wisdom about life," Wendy reflected. "This is the greatest reward I've received from this demanding job."



02


A flower of resilience, lifelong learning


Does no educational or professional background mean the job is easy?


On this point, Wendy emphasized particularly: Although home care work may seem undemanding, it actually requires very high overall competence from its workers. First, the physical demands are a major challenge. As Wendy described, the working hours and intensity for most home care workers far exceed the average. And because it is a service industry, "home care workers are expected to meet all the employer's needs as much as possible."


While people of any age or background can enter this field, Wendy has personally experienced how aging workers are gradually phased out by employers and the market. For Wendy, who is already past the age of forty, this is an unavoidable challenge.

 

Yet Wendy does not see this phasing out as inevitable. She calls on immigrant home care workers, especially older women, to leverage their age and life experience: "Asian women should enhance their life and service skills from multiple angles and plan their careers in advance."



Another major challenge is communication between home care workers and employers across language and cultural barriers. According to Ms. Deng, the majority of home care workers around her belong to ethnic minority groups. "80% are Filipino, with some Mexican and African American workers as well."


Since home care workers interact directly with employers and must communicate frequently, immigrant workers often need to spend significant time learning English and American culture. At the same time, employers, seeing themselves as the ones paying, may make all kinds of demands, expecting the worker to fulfill their wishes no matter what.


Throughout her five years as a home care worker, Wendy has often sensed a look of "inequality" in her employers' eyes. This sense of unease was especially strong when she first arrived, unfamiliar with American language and culture. Therefore, immigrant home care workers often easily feel socially isolated, bearing immense psychological pressure as a result..


However, Wendy believes that learning American culture and integrating into U.S. society are also ways to enhance one's skills and competitiveness. Wendy maintains a calm, optimistic, and upward-looking mindset. For example, she participates in Chinese church activities to learn English and local church culture. To her, the Chinese church acts as a transitional space, helping her integrate into American society while also offering support and fellowship from fellow church members.



03


Finding a sense of "home"


Wendy is like a resilient flower blooming in a foreign land.


Her career choice is not only about earning a living, but also a return of care and maternal love. In this seemingly simple profession, she grows with determination and a positive attitude, adapting and integrating into American society. Even when facing work pressure and cultural differences, Wendy maintains an optimistic and forward-looking mindset, gradually finding a sense of "home" abroad through continuous learning and hard work.


At the same time, Wendy contributes to community building, offering support as a home care worker to elderly individuals living alone and to other immigrant care workers.





Wendy's story is one about family, struggle, and dreams—a story that resonates with every person striving in a foreign land. Her story is like a small yet warm footnote, calling on the U.S. government to pay greater attention to the working and psychological conditions of immigrant home care workers, helping them better integrate into local society, cultivate diverse life and service skills, and enhance their competitiveness in the workplace.
