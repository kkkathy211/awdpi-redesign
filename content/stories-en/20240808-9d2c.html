Avoice Series Psychology Course Introduction:

Avoice's Self-Healing Series Course is designed for survivors who have experienced relationship violence, aiming to help survivors understand their own reactions and confusion, and actively identify internal and external resources. Through this course, we want to convey the following messages:

• It's not your fault 

• No matter what feelings or reactions you have, they are normal 

• You are not alone; you can receive support

• Your experience deserves to be understood, and deserves to be shared.


In the previous session, we introduced what trauma is and how to view it. When we go through moments that break our hearts, we may feel that ourselves and everything around us have lost their color. In this session, we will walk with you on the healing journey. When experiencing trauma, you may have a range of physical reactions and emotional feelings—you may feel sadness, anger, or even a loss of control over your body and mind. To protect yourself, you may have closed your heart or entered a state of constant alertness to ensure the harm won't affect you again. You may also have experienced symptoms of depression or anxiety.


Now, we hope to explore gentler ways of healing with you, helping you lower your guard and return to peace. The key to healing trauma lies in re-establishing a connection with yourself—building new links with your emotions, body, and thoughts—so as to regain a sense of control and re-experience safety. Paying attention to, recognizing, and understanding emotions—learning to relate better to your emotions—is the theme of this session.


This session lasts approximately 20 minutes, during which you will learn about:

• the importance of emotions

• becoming aware of emotions in the present moment

• how to accept emotions

• recognizing and understanding emotions


At the end of the session, we will guide you through a short exercise that relaxes the body to help relax emotions, hoping to assist you in better understanding how to practice self-healing by building connections with your body and emotions.



The Importance of Emotions


Let's begin with "The Importance of Emotions":


In life, the part of the brain responsible for decision-making and logical thinking actually makes up only 30% of the total brain volume, while the regions responsible for basic survival and emotions occupy a much larger area. The emotional brain begins growing from birth and, before we develop so-called "rationality," emotions serve as crucial guides for survival and responding to complex challenges. When an infant sees a caregiver's smile, they feel warmth and joy—these emotions signal that their current environment is safe. Emotions reflect human experiences of external events and things; they are both a response and a tool that help us feel and act, They tell us information about our current state, and also influence the choices we make.



When we experience trauma, the emotions we feel are also reminding us that we've gone through a terrible moment and that we want to protect ourselves. Therefore, increasing our understanding and connection with emotions plays a vital role in helping us understand our true selves. Next, we will discuss how to heal the self through emotional connection, focusing on three stages: becoming aware of emotions, accepting emotions, and understanding emotions.


"Stage One: Becoming Aware of Emotions"


Recognizing and becoming aware of what emotions you are experiencing in the moment an event occurs is a core ability of emotional intelligence and the foundation of self-understanding.


To briefly assess your level of "emotional awareness," try answering the following question.


Imagine when you are arguing with your partner, and they keep blaming you, saying it's entirely your fault—what would your reaction be? Which of the following situations is closest to your response?


1. Unable to describe your emotional experience, or only able to describe your physical sensations, for example: feeling a lump in your chest, headache

2. Only able to describe your emotional tendency or state, but struggle to use emotional vocabulary, for example: I feel okay / really bad

3. Able to describe a single, rigid emotional experience, for example: I feel very sad

4. Able to describe more complex, continuous emotional experiences, even experiencing opposing or clearly different emotions at the same time, for example: I feel very sad, but I can still sense hope

5. Able to simultaneously experience the emotional complexity of both yourself and the other person, able to distinguish subtle differences between emotions, and able to accurately describe your full emotional experience.


At which level is your emotional awareness?

In the midst of intense emotions, some people tend to skip emotional awareness altogether and rush straight into analyzing and solving problems—a pattern known as "emotional uncoupling." Emotional awareness can begin right here. After an event occurs, we can consciously stop attributing or processing emotions at the cognitive level, and instead try to perceive and see our emotions more clearly. For example, when we are emotionally hurt by someone in an intimate relationship, we may feel sadness and pain, and immediately start thinking: Why did they hurt me? Whose fault is it? How can I avoid conflict next time?


There's nothing wrong with these thoughts themselves, but endlessly trying to find solutions based on past experiences and seeking further explanations can lead us away from the emotions themselves, causing us to overlook our present feelings. Most of the time, the events that trigger emotions cannot be resolved immediately—just as when you're wrongly accused, you may not be able to clarify things right away; when we have conflicts with others, we can't instantly calm both parties' emotions or determine who is right or wrong. Ignoring the emotions themselves may leave us continuously troubled by emotions while trying to solve problems, overwhelmed by helplessness, and sometimes even prevent us from resolving the immediate issue. For instance, when we are suffering from trauma, trying to understand it too early may lead to re-traumatization and obsessive thinking. At such times, what we need to do is stop attributing emotions, stop overthinking, and return to the simplest question: " How am I feeling right now?" "


We can also better understand our emotional experiences by paying attention to our bodies. Emotions are often accompanied by a series of physical, behavioral, and psychological reactions. For example, when afraid, your heart rate increases, you may sweat or tremble; when tense, your body stiffens and blood pressure rises; when sad, your chest may feel tight and your breathing quickens. When we consciously notice how our bodies react in different situations, we gradually learn to detect signs of stress and comfort. The next time our body has a similar sensation, we can more quickly identify what emotion we are experiencing, and thus create distance from negative emotions. We can also soothe emotions by understanding and comforting bodily sensations—for example, during intense anxiety, deep breathing is an effective way to relieve anxiety. You can start by focusing on your breath, gradually deepening the breath and noticing how it changes. As your breathing slows, your anxiety also gradually eases. Focusing on the physical sensations of breathing also brings attention back to the present moment, preventing emotional escalation caused by overthinking. Only when we bring our attention back to ourselves and truly see our emotions can we move to the next step: accepting emotions.


"Stage Two: Accepting Emotions"


In some cases, we may find it difficult to immediately recognize our emotions, especially when we are in a state of tension or intense emotion. We may feel guilt or regret about the emergence of emotions. On the contrary, resolving these feelings simply requires first accepting the emotions. To make acceptance concrete, it can be simply summarized as: Say it out loud, face it.


[Say it out loud]


Try using a universal sentence structure to name your emotion to yourself: "It's normal for me to feel XX (an emotion)" or "It's normal for me to have XXX emotions." (For example: I was betrayed by my partner, and it's normal for me to feel angry or heartbroken.)—this is a way of using verbal cues to actively help yourself accept emotions. We aim to stop judging, resisting, or suppressing negative emotions, so understanding that all emotions are valid is the most basic way to achieve this.



[Face it]


Negative emotions never disappear through suppression; instead, they reappear later in the form of mental or physical illness. Coping strategies that involve avoidance may lead to depression or anxiety, and chronic emotional stress can also affect physical health by increasing stress hormones in the body. Below are several common ways to release emotions—


    First, record your emotions.


We can use an "emotion journal" to understand the causes and progression of our emotions. The method is simple: write in a journal format about what kind of situation triggered your emotion? How intense was the emotion? What physical reactions did you have? What feelings arose? When you felt this way, what thoughts came to mind? Each day, observe how your emotions changed. Like watching yourself from a third-person perspective, notice the characteristics of your emotions—do they follow a fixed pattern? Are there certain methods you commonly use that effectively help you regulate your emotions?

    Second, name your emotions.


Often, when emotions arise, we can only initially sense a vague and general feeling. For example, in the case described in the emotion journal, when your partner accuses you of not being family-oriented enough or not making efforts to avoid business trips, you might first feel something like "I'm completely falling apart" or "I'm so angry," a sensation that may be a general sense of anger. However, human emotions are actually layered, complex, and multifaceted.


After roughly experiencing this emotional sensation, we can try to refine and name the core emotions within it. Beneath the statement "I'm so angry," in addition to anger, we might also sense "disappointment" and "grievance" at our partner for not understanding the full context of the situation and not standing with us in facing the issue, a sense of "helplessness" about work being beyond our control, and "anxiety" about the risk to our job if we refuse to travel. Underneath the initial broad feeling of anger, there lie more nuanced emotions, and these emotions are linked to our own needs and expectations of important others. By naming emotions, we better understand the present moment and communicate our needs more effectively.



For those who are not accustomed to observing their emotions, emotional perception may be like a low-resolution monitor, capable only of recognizing coarse and imprecise feelings, which hinders our ability to further "name emotions." The ability to distinguish subtle emotions can actually be learned and practiced later in life. Sometimes, we simply don't use the language that expresses our feelings very often, so we can start by expanding our emotional vocabulary. For instance, in emotional lexicons, "shame" is defined as discomfort arising from recognizing one's flaws, wrongdoing, or failure to fulfill responsibilities; "embarrassment" is defined as uneasiness, shyness, or fear of ridicule due to timidity, unfamiliarity, or having done something wrong. These are emotions we might experience or recall when going through or remembering traumatic experiences. By becoming familiar with these nuanced emotional terms, enriching our language, and connecting them with life experiences, we can enhance the precision of emotional perception and more accurately express our feelings and needs to others.


In summary, during accepting emotions, through speaking up and facing it, understanding what situations trigger specific emotional responses and how they affect you, is also an important part of self-understanding.


"Stage Three: Cognitive Awareness of Emotions"


Through the first two stages, we come to understand that all emotions are valid, and seeing and accepting them is the best approach. At the same time, building emotional stability requires a third step: better coexisting with emotions through emotional awareness, thereby changing the way we view ourselves and the world, ultimately transforming our feelings and behaviors, and taking ownership of our self-healing journey.


Emotional stability is one of the signs of psychological maturity. Emotionally stable individuals possess a relatively mature worldview and stable self-awareness, along with familiarity and clarity about their own emotions, enabling them to effectively regulate and respond when facing emotions. Past experiences, especially traumatic ones, easily enter our subconscious and control our lives through emotions. Early life experiences also greatly influence how we perceive events and respond to them. For example, parenting and communication patterns during childhood directly affect our emotional response mechanisms, even causing us to repeatedly fall into the same emotional pain without flexible or spacious coping strategies.


On one hand, we need to understand and accept our emotions, consciously recognizing what is driving our current emotional reactions, interpreting the messages behind emotional signals and responding accordingly. For instance, anger is a signal that inner needs are unmet, sadness signals the loss of something important, and anxiety, depression, boredom, shame—these are all important signals, each serving as a bridge for communication between ourselves and our inner being.


On the other hand, when emotions become overwhelming, we can learn to practice STOP:

Stop and step back: pause;

Take a breath: breathe;

Observe: observe;

Put your value into play: act effectively based on your values.

Trying to pause creates some inner space and perspective, allowing you to decide what the most adaptive choice is, consider the consequences of your actions, align your behavior with your values, and treat both yourself and others with greater care.


You can try asking yourself open-ended questions: What is happening inside me right now? Observe your emotions or bodily sensations. Pay attention to what information they bring. When we connect with ourselves, we feel calmer and more confident in facing life. When you begin to notice habitual negative thoughts arising in your mind, don't resist them, judge them, or argue with them. Even if you experience a sense of inner emptiness that might feel frightening, try to accept this empty moment. It can bring a sense of inner peace, and in that stillness, you gain a sense of connection with your inner emotions and body.



The most authentic self is rooted within each person's body. Observe your emotions just as you would observe the voices in your mind. Sometimes, you need to sift through the top layers of voices to distinguish and hear your true voice. Accept the present emotion, be with it, and at the same time consciously manage the distance between you and it. You'll discover you can respond differently than before—this is the growth of a new self in the present moment. It tells us that we can be different from our past, and it is the strongest proof of your inner strength.


 This session of Avoice's course, centered on emotional healing, has guided you to understand why emotions are important, how to accept them, and how to better understand them cognitively. These are foundational steps that help us relate to our emotions more freely. Next, we will offer you a small exercise to relax your body and, in turn, relax your emotions.



The body is the container of emotions. We hope you will try more often in daily life to connect with your body, accept your body's current reactions, and accept your emotions as well. For more such exercises, please follow the upcoming sessions of Avoice's self-healing series.
