Migration is not an end point, but the beginning of another complex process of socialization.

In an era of increasingly frequent global population movements, migration is no longer merely geographical displacement—it is also the reshaping of identity and the struggle for recognition. From the moment one steps onto foreign soil, physical migration ends, but the question of "Who am I?" begins to grow complicated, especially when racial labels are attached—what challenges will individuals face in understanding themselves, their ethnic group, and their sense of belonging?


One

Lecture Introduction


This lecture will focus on two key themes:


· Post-migratory identity formation:


Migration is a continuous social process. After migration, individuals do not enter a neutral or equal social space, but instead step into a field of power deeply embedded with legal systems, racial discourses, colonial legacies, and social classification systems. Within this structure, migrants' sense of belonging and identity are continuously shaped by state discipline, cultural exclusion, and racialization mechanisms.


"Post-migration" refers not only to a temporal "after," but also to an ongoing dynamic process of negotiating identity, power, and belonging. Key features include: legal liminality (such as ambiguous or temporary residency status), systemic exclusion (such as non-recognition of educational and professional qualifications), and cultural marginalization (such as migrants being disciplined into becoming the "Other"). Under such structural constraints, can individuals sustain their original social roles and identities? How should they redefine themselves within a new social order? These are the core challenges that "post-migratory identity formation" must confront.

· Re-learning a racialized identity:


"Racialized identity" does not originate from individual self-identification, but is constructed, encoded, and continuously regulated by power mechanisms within specific historical and social structures. It is both an externally imposed social positioning and a dynamic process in which individuals constantly negotiate with, and even resist, structural forces.


In the context of migration, racialized identity often manifests in more complex ways. Migrants may undergo internal adjustments such as self-devaluation or excessive assimilation, or adopt strategic performances (such as downplaying accents or conforming to mainstream norms) in an attempt to "fit in." At the same time, an increasing number of individuals are taking political paths of identification—through self-naming, self-representation, and organized action—to reconstruct subjectivity and collective belonging.


This lecture will further explore: How do racial stereotypes profoundly influence migrants' self-perception? When "skin color," "accent," and "nationality" become tools for identity boundary-making, do migrants still have space to actively choose their own identities—or even subvert othering labels?

Guest Introduction | Dr. Sreenita Mukherjee

PhD Researcher at Queen Mary University of London

Specializing in critical research on postcolonial feminism, identity formation, and intersecting race-gender experiences

Dr. Mukherjee's research focuses on women's work in the architecture industry, particularly female immigrant architects living and working in the UK. Using postcolonial feminism as a theoretical framework, she critically examines the long-standing power structure in architecture dominated by white men, revealing how this structure shapes, excludes, and even reconstructs the professional identities of racial minority women.

Her research addresses several key questions:

  • What professional and personal challenges do racial minority women architects face when entering the UK architecture industry?

  • How does the geographical origin of architectural qualifications—for example, those obtained in former colonial countries—affect their professional legitimacy and social recognition in the UK?

  • In the face of structural racial and gender inequalities, how do these women reshape their professional identities and strive for stronger socioeconomic positions within the cracks?


Through in-depth analysis of these issues, Dr. Mukherjee's research offers not only theoretical insights, but also practical references for building more inclusive professional environments and policy interventions.
