Introduction


"Vietnamese brides" refer to Vietnamese women who migrate to other countries through arranged marriages, cross-border marriages, and bride-buying practices, using marriage as a means to improve their living conditions (Xue Ying, 2017). As a significant part of the Vietnamese female migrant population, Vietnamese brides constitute a large demographic group. The transnational migration of Vietnamese brides reflects not only the global trend of transnational marriages but also implies unequal economic relations between countries and gendered power dynamics. From the perspective of the legalization of the Vietnamese bride industry, this article analyzes the subject of Vietnamese brides and clarifies the background, causes, and roles played by various stakeholders in the emergence of the transnational marriage industry, aiming to present the real-life portraits and living conditions of the "Vietnamese brides" group.



Background and Current Situation


In Vietnam, Vietnamese brides are predominantly low-educated, low-income women from rural or border areas who are of childbearing age and urgently seek to improve their living conditions. Vietnamese brides in transnational marriages exhibit characteristics such as large numbers, significant growth, wide-ranging destinations, and strong purposefulness. Taking Vietnamese brides migrating to other East Asian countries as an example, according to data from Statistics Korea, in 2016, among 21,709 registered international marriages in South Korea, brides from Vietnam accounted for the highest proportion at 27.9%, showing a year-on-year increasing trend.


As a branch of foreign brides' marriages, which account for "4% of the global transnational marriage population," Vietnamese brides mainly flow from Vietnam to neighboring affluent Southeast Asian countries, oil-rich Middle Eastern and Far Eastern regions, South Korea, and Western Europe (Thornton, 1992). The causes of this phenomenon are complex, including the influence of interethnic marriage history, gender imbalance, economic disparities between countries, and the disadvantaged status of women (Liu Dandan, 2018). This article primarily focuses on the gender power relations and differences in national economic status behind this phenomenon and discusses accordingly. Patriarchal systems constitute the main structure of social relations in Vietnam, with male dominance at the core of gender power (Xue Ying, 2017). Within Vietnam's strict familial system, men hold natural, socially default priority rights in property inheritance, decision-making, and family lineage continuation. Most women, on the other hand, can only demonstrate their value through the process of bearing and raising children. Women's value becomes dependent on reproductive capacity—especially the ability to bear male offspring—leading to the devaluation of their self-worth. This deeply entrenched gender power relation solidifies the consciousness that women must be subordinate to men, turning it into a societal consensus in Vietnam. This not only exacerbates gender inequality but also continuously reinforces the image of the "ideal woman" as a Vietnamese bride, forming the primary logic through which Vietnamese women use marriage as a means of migration and survival. Differences in national economic status serve as the main pull factor attracting Vietnamese women to marry into destination countries. American scholar Klein, in *Measurement of National Power*, points out that national power relations are influenced by comprehensive national strength, leading citizens to tend to migrate from countries with less power to those with greater power. A comparison using several power indicators from Klein's equation of comprehensive national strength reveals that per capita GDP in major destination countries for Vietnamese brides—such as South Korea and the United States—is significantly higher than in Vietnam. Greater employment opportunities, better healthcare, and more comprehensive social security systems all contribute to the attractiveness for Vietnamese brides.


Although this study focuses on Vietnamese brides in legal transnational marriages, numerous issues persist under this framework: first, the lack of an environment in host countries that recognizes and accepts Vietnamese brides; second, insufficient access to children's education; third, challenges in maintaining family and marital relationships; and fourth, difficulties in obtaining nationality recognition and legal status. Transnational marriage migrants remain a vulnerable group in host countries, where differences in appearance, diet, and lifestyle easily create a clear distinction between "us" and "them" (Zheng Jin, 2019), reducing the group's sense of social participation and integration and increasing their marginalization. Meanwhile, families that marry Vietnamese brides are often themselves economically disadvantaged and socially marginalized, creating a "weak-weak" union that may reduce financial capacity for children's education and affect the next generation's human capital.


Regarding family and marital relationships, Vietnamese brides' marriages are often characterized by strong instrumentalism, known as "commodified marriages," with weak emotional bonds between spouses. Moreover, relative economic improvement does not necessarily correlate with the establishment of gender equality. Many Vietnamese brides continue to exist as commodities and remain vulnerable to domestic violence. In the process of gaining legal recognition, inconsistent nationality management regulations across countries force Vietnamese brides to overcome numerous obstacles (Weng Li & Yu Shanshan, 2017). Furthermore, due to regulatory blind spots in the household registration systems of many destination countries regarding this special group, the nationality and rights issues of Vietnamese brides are often not promptly and effectively addressed. Therefore, presenting the real-life portraits and living conditions of the "Vietnamese brides" group is of great significance.




Vietnamese Brides in Transnational Marriages


Since the end of the 20th century, due to Vietnam's weak economic position within the global economic system, the prosperity of cross-border economies, and increased cross-border mobility, "Vietnamese brides" have emerged accordingly (Huang, 2020). They marry abroad and are now found throughout Southeast Asia, Europe, and North America.


On one hand, transnational marriages involving Vietnamese brides are often referred to as "commodified marriages": marriage brokers treat them as commodities, profiting from their gendered identities, disadvantaged economic status, and foreign grooms' fantasies about Southeast Asian women (Gong Yijun, 2011). On the other hand, within this industrial chain, Vietnamese brides also improve their natal families' living conditions by participating in transnational marriages, gain residency in developed regions, and strive for greater possibilities of "upward mobility" (Li Mei-hsien, 2006). However, becoming a "Vietnamese bride" is no easy task: at marriage agencies, they must go through processes such as "raising brides," matchmaking, and "bride schools," during which they are trained to embody the image of "traditional feminine virtues" expected by foreigners (Tian Jingying & Wang Hongren, 2006; Gong Yijun, 2011).


After marrying abroad, they must overcome language and cultural barriers, strive to integrate into their new families and communities (Cahe & Hong, 2007), and actively respond to their subordinate and dependent status in marital relationships, reconstructing gender roles to claim more independent space and autonomy (Wang, 2007; Huang, 2017). At the same time, within Vietnam, "Vietnamese brides" are stigmatized as "vulgar," "bad girls," and "low-quality," and under the intersecting influences of Vietnam's elite-dominated social system, Confucian traditional thought, patriarchal structures, and negative media portrayals, they are even regarded as a "national shame" (Li Mei-hsien, 2006).. Vietnamese brides, who occupy a weak position in both national power relations and gender power relations, face exploitation and oppression from multiple sides, struggling within a vast transnational marriage industry to create development opportunities for themselves and their natal families.


In transnational marriages, Vietnamese brides become gendered commodities, exploited for profit by marriage brokers and their natal families through their gendered bodies, disadvantaged economic status, and aspirations for foreign marriage (Gong Yijun, 2011). Compared to female migrants in Japan's marriage brokerage market since the 1970s—who enjoyed autonomy in marriage choices and personal rights—Vietnamese women in transnational marriages do not receive equal treatment.


Taking Taiwan-Vietnam brokered marriages as an example, Vietnamese women are marketed primarily on the basis of "sexual purity" in Taiwan's marriage market. However, this "selling point" further deepens their disadvantaged status in Taiwanese society and strengthens the new family's control over them (Liaw & Huang, 2012; Wang, 2010). Taiwanese men can demand that Vietnamese women be exchanged through brokers like commodities, a practice widely accepted in Taiwanese society as "an important way for men to obtain women." This phenomenon reflects not only the class disparity between Taiwan and Vietnam in economic status but also the fraternal patriarchal system in Taiwanese society: Taiwanese men's sexual rights are publicly recognized, while Vietnamese women's subordinate status is tacitly accepted by the public (Gong Yijun, 2011; Pateman, 1999). Moreover, marriage agencies organize public, "demonstrative" marriage ceremonies that reinforce and deepen Taiwanese men's objectifying domination over Vietnamese women: "Taiwanese men can select suitable candidates from 100 to 300 Vietnamese women through matchmaking (like talent shows); they can even conduct 'physical examinations' on future Vietnamese brides to verify virginity... Vietnamese women are reduced to an undifferentiated 'category,' interchangeable and replaceable" (Gong Yijun, 2011; Simmel, 2001).


Whether in their families of origin in Vietnam or in new families in developed countries and regions, Vietnamese brides occupy a subordinate status. According to Nguyen and Tran's survey (2010), among the reasons provided by Vietnamese parents for marrying off their daughters to foreign husbands, "to help the family" accounted for 61.6%. Bélanger and Linh (2011) also noted that in some impoverished rural areas of Vietnam, many families have come to rely essentially on remittances from daughters already married to men in developed countries and regions to improve their economic and social standing. As a result, villagers even express a preference for having girls rather than boys. Under the influence of Confucian filial piety prevalent in Vietnamese society and through upbringing and discipline within their families of origin, most Vietnamese brides feel obligated to send money and goods earned in their new families back to their relatives, who often demand this and regard such remittances as their due (Liaw & Huang, 2012). Beyond the brides' parents, their siblings, and even nieces and nephews, hope to be supported or financially assisted by these immigrant brides, who are perceived as wealthy (Suzuki, 2005).


But have Vietnamese brides who successfully immigrated to developed countries and regions and entered new families truly achieved affluent and comfortable lives? The reality is otherwise. The economic disparity between the country of origin and the destination country, gender power imbalances, and familiarity with the destination country's culture and language significantly affect the living conditions, treatment, and status of migrants within transnational marriages, and Vietnamese women hold no advantage in this context. In their new families, Vietnamese brides strive to meet their foreign husbands' sexual and caregiving needs, using their "chaste" femininity and the perceived "traditional, submissive" gender image of women from Southeast Asian countries to resist discrimination from family members and gain acceptance—yet they constantly face the risk of their transnational marriages breaking down. (Liaw & Huang, 2012; Wang, 2010). Regarding the high risk of breakdown in international marriages between brides from low-income countries and grooms from high-income countries, Liaw and Huang summarize the following reasons: a) mismatched expectations between spouses, which easily lead to a "clash of dreams" (Thai, 2005); b) the harsh daily life and isolation experienced by foreign brides in rural and remote areas of the destination country, along with conflicts with cohabiting in-laws, are also high-risk factors for marital breakdown; c) the heavy burden of sending remittances to their families of origin becomes a serious difficulty in Vietnamese brides' daily lives within their new families and a source of conflict with their husbands (Suzuki, 2005).


Vietnamese women in transnational marriages exist at the boundary between Vietnamese society and foreign societies, struggling to gain national or ethnic recognition in either. On one hand, Vietnamese brides find it difficult to integrate into the culture, customs, and social networks of the destination country, and even their citizenship status and rights often fail to be fully realized due to inconsistent nationality regulations across countries (Weng Li & Yu Shanshan, 2017; Zheng Jin, 2019). Due to differences in diet, language, and appearance, the foreignness of Vietnamese women who marry into destination countries is highly visible, and the clear boundary between "us" and "them" makes it difficult for them to integrate into local ethnic communities (Zheng Jin, 2019). When there is a significant disparity in international status between Vietnam and developed destination countries, the label of "Vietnamese" makes these brides more vulnerable to prejudice and discrimination in the host society: "Women are often assigned the role of boundary markers in national construction; female migrants are especially likely to bear the stigmatizing labels imposed by the host society on their countries of origin"—Vietnamese brides are a typical case (Wang Yihan, 2012).


On the other hand, under Vietnam's mainstream elite culture, Vietnamese brides also face disdain and exclusion from their society of origin (Li Meixian, 2006). Moral condemnations and stigmatizing labels such as "greedy, low-level, bad girls, valuing money over love, a national shame" imposed on women who marry abroad push these Vietnamese women beyond the boundaries of their original society and ethnic group, severely challenging their national identity with Vietnam (Li Meixian, 2006; Zheng Jin, 2019). The Vietnamese government has also introduced a series of unfriendly laws and measures to restrict Vietnamese women of marriageable age from marrying abroad, resulting in Vietnamese women facing more hostile and stringent treatment during visa and marriage certification processes (Zheng Jin, 2019).




Who marries "Vietnamese brides"?


There are many reasons that facilitate transnational marriages. An increasing number of women from less developed countries and regions in Southeast Asia use transnational marriage as a means to migrate to more developed Asian countries, thereby improving their social status and lifting their families of origin out of poverty. Thus, to some extent, transnational marriage has become a migration strategy for economic gain and a source of family support (Chiu & Yeoh, 2021). In other words, one of the most critical factors is the economic imbalance between developed and developing countries and regions, which drives women from developing areas to seek opportunities to marry wealthier men in developed countries and regions. Yet beneath this surface logic lies the reality of increasingly unequal gender power structures. While "Vietnamese brides" hope to escape poverty through "transnational marriage," who exactly are the men becoming their husbands? 


Compared to women from other Southeast Asian countries, Vietnamese women have long been subject to being bought and sold as brides. This phenomenon emerged during the early colonial period from the 1870s to the 1940s (Luong, 2020). In the 19th and 20th centuries, the trafficking of Vietnamese women as brides occurred only within Vietnam. After Vietnam opened its economy to foreign investment, marriage migration gained momentum. In the 1990s, large numbers of Taiwanese businessmen came to work in Vietnam. They found that Vietnamese women, whose Confucian values resembled their own, could be suitable partners. When these businessmen returned to Taiwan, they—acting as opportunists—realized they could profit from arranging marriages. Since the mid-1990s, the number of Vietnamese women marrying foreign men from East and Southeast Asian countries has risen rapidly. Many Vietnamese women chose to live in viewing hotels around Ho Chi Minh City, waiting day after day for a future foreign husband to select them (Billo, 2012). According to data from Taiwan's Ministry of the Interior Household Registration Office in 2009, transnational marriages accounted for 7.36% of total marriage registrations that year. Among all foreign spouses, women from Southeast Asia made up over 60% (Y. H. Wang, 2010). Taiwan's traditional gender culture, which emphasizes male dominance in family life, is key to understanding these transnational marriages. Research has noted that Taiwanese men with lower education and economic status often lack competitiveness in the local marriage market and are more inclined to choose foreign wives. They typically hold patriarchal views, hoping to marry women with traditional virtues—such as being housewives who bear children and care for in-laws. Family systems and capitalism are the two institutional forces shaping their preference for "traditional" Vietnamese women (H. Wang, 2018). In relatively economically developed Asian countries and regions such as Singapore, South Korea, and Japan—common destinations for "Vietnamese brides"—grooms commonly face the dilemma of being unable to secure a place in their domestic marriage markets.


Men in these Asian countries and regions who marry "Vietnamese brides" also share clear motivational commonalities. Taking South Korea as an example, researchers have summarized four main reasons for Korean men's transnational marriages: first, domestic women have higher education and career levels, leaving less-advantaged men at a disadvantage in the local marriage market; second, cultural pressures in patrilineal Korean families expect daughters-in-law to care for in-laws, yet fewer Korean women are willing to take on this responsibility; third, marriage agencies and market environments promote foreign brides as more traditional and better suited for family life; fourth, official support and encouragement of transnational marriages (Naomi, 2001). One point that must be mentioned is that in developed East Asian countries like Singapore and South Korea, declining fertility rates over recent decades have led to shifting population structures, which in turn have created huge demands and gaps in childcare and eldercare. In Singapore, middle-class families meet these needs by hiring foreign domestic workers, while working-class men increasingly seek foreign brides as a more affordable way to ensure various forms of caregiving, including housework, caring for elderly parents, and raising the next generation (Yeoh et al., 2013).


In Western countries, the "mail-order bride" business has become a form of capitalist "venture investment" amid the wave of globalization (Starr & Adams, 2016, pp. 968–969). In an increasingly globalized and multicultural world, the U.S. government no longer needs immigrant women to sustain its national population. Yet the desire to find partners abroad is growing stronger, and mail-order bride services are becoming increasingly popular (Lee, 2022). "American grooms" are typically between 30 and 60 years old, 20 to 50 years older than their brides. The brides are mostly in their twenties, coming from Asian and Eastern European countries (Date4u, 2021).


Vietnamese brides do not always marry husbands of other ethnicities. An increasing number of immigrants and men of immigrant origin are seeking wives in their countries of origin, a phenomenon more common in Asian countries. Researchers examining 69 same-ethnicity international marriages found that a specific and typical marriage pattern involves highly educated Vietnamese women marrying overseas Vietnamese men in low-wage jobs (Hung, 2010). In this pattern, the traditional and widespread "marriage gradient"—where women "marry up" and men "marry down"—appears to be reversed. Highly educated Vietnamese women delay or avoid marriage with local men because they cannot find suitable partners to "marry up" to; more importantly, many men influenced by traditional Asian and Confucian gender, age, and class hierarchies view such women as unsuitable marriage partners.


Turning our attention back to the grooms, the men in the cases studied are ethnic Vietnamese men living in the United States. They belong to low-income groups and are paid by the hour; some of them migrated overseas after the Vietnam War and experienced significant downward mobility. In fact, as globalization rapidly opens up impersonal markets for capital, goods, and labor, it simultaneously opens up a highly personal market for emotions and marriage. However, it must be noted that unlike the two-way flow of capital and goods, the unbridgeable economic gap between the Western First World and third-world Vietnam prevents Vietnamese women from going abroad to find marriage partners, while men can easily return to Vietnam to find wives.




The operation of matchmaking agencies


As previously mentioned, "Vietnamese brides" carry East Asian men's imagination of the "ideal wife," symbolizing tradition, family orientation, diligence, and virtue. Then, how is this image constructed and realized? Here, we must mention the transnational marriage matchmaking agencies that connect grooms and brides.


With the increase in transnational marriages, matchmaking agencies have also flourished. These international marriage agencies facilitate marriages through the internet, newspaper advertisements, traditional mail correspondence, or by organizing "blind date" tours. In these matchmaking operations, brokers in the destination country recruit men, while brokers in the source country recruit women. They communicate with each other to provide channels for meetings and selection (Md Said et al., 2022).


Due to strict immigration policies and complex legal issues, marriage agencies have become crucial in organizing blind date tours, bridging communication gaps, arranging weddings, and handling paperwork (Lu, 2005). Taking cross-strait relations between Taiwan and Vietnam as an example, transnational marriages between Taiwan and Vietnam are closely linked to Taiwan's investments in Vietnam since 1994. Economic exchanges are a key factor that makes "Vietnamese brides" possible. Moreover, the operation of marriage agencies is an indispensable component. Among Southeast Asian countries, the Taiwan-Vietnam corridor has the largest cross-border migration industry, systematically developing a large-scale commercial transnational bride industry (Y. H. Wang, 2010). The emergence of these matchmaking agencies results from increased Taiwanese investment in Vietnam and the high demand for international marriages in both countries. However, these agencies, in turn, create demand for Vietnamese brides in Taiwan (Y. H. Wang, 2010). Taking South Korea as another example, "Vietnamese brides" who marry into Korea often perceive Korean men as economically stable, reliable, while Korean men who marry Vietnamese women typically view them as hardworking, capable, sincere, kind, and respectful toward elders (Cahe & Hong, 2007). Such a dual "imagination" cannot be achieved without the active role of matchmaking agencies.


As major destinations for Vietnamese brides, Taiwan and South Korea see their matchmaking agencies playing a significant role in marketing these brides. Vietnamese women are portrayed as symbols of youth, purity, gentleness, and beauty. Importantly, Vietnamese women resemble East Asians in appearance, meaning children born to Vietnamese brides largely resemble local Taiwanese or Korean children. On the Taiwan side, matchmaking agencies have established offices in Vietnam. Vietnamese agencies work with large-scale matchmakers ("da mei ren") in Vietnam, who then send smaller matchmakers ("xiao mei ren") into communities where prospective brides live. In both Taiwan and Vietnam, individual matchmakers also serve as intermediaries. They are often already-married Taiwanese-Vietnamese couples who leverage their networks to arrange marriages. From the Vietnamese perspective, it is observed that these matchmakers, both large and small, are often ethnic Chinese Vietnamese who can better navigate the linguistic and cultural contexts between Vietnam and China (Belanger & Wang, 2016).


Brides introduced by marriage agencies ultimately arrive in South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Malaysia, and even Europe, often controlled by networks of agents whose primary motivation is profit rather than the claimed goal of helping Vietnamese brides escape poverty or achieve upward mobility. Currently, the surge in Vietnamese marriage agencies is primarily market-driven. At the intersection of supply and demand, the market price for these women is approximately 5,000 US dollars. A Singapore-based agency, J&N Viet-Bride, advertises that men can travel to Vietnam, select a bride, and get married within a week. Once visa procedures are completed, their new wives can return home with them. Although marriages based on money are full of uncertainty, most Vietnamese women still hope for a good marital life. At the very least, the opportunity to send remittances back to their families is sufficient motivation to marry a stranger.


Like all advertisers, transnational marriage agencies must develop their own promotional strategies and rhetoric to attract clients if they wish to profit from "selling" brides. The most common approach is to portray these women from the Global South as "victims" waiting to be rescued, while emphasizing that they make better wives than local women (Lee, 2022). In reality, however, these matchmaking websites do not merely present brides as "traditional wives," but instead maximize their sexualized features for potential consumers, avoiding portrayals of economic desperation, lack of education, or "atheism" (Merriman, 2012, p.87). It is not uncommon for agencies to attract buyers by highlighting the sex appeal and physical appearance of mail-order brides. On a matchmaking website called "broomstickwed," the promotional slogan for Vietnamese brides is: "In addition to attractive physical features, they possess excellent personal qualities. They are very kind, calm, and lovely. In the eyes of Western men, they are like angels, often chosen as their wives." The accompanying image is a photograph of a woman dressed provocatively. In fact, most major mail-order bride websites require or encourage foreign women to list their body measurements in their profiles and upload "professionally taken photos in various outfits, highlighting different angles of their bodies."




Conclusion


The emergence of the "Vietnamese brides" phenomenon and the transnational marriage industry in Vietnam is complex, reflecting inequalities in economic status and gender power relations between nations. Within the global economic system and patriarchal structures, Vietnamese brides, as a vulnerable group, often become objects of exploitation, with little guarantee of equal treatment or basic rights. This article aims to portray the subjectivity of Vietnamese brides, analyze the causes and background of Vietnam's transnational marriage industry, and introduce the grooms from developed regions and the matchmaking agencies involved, in order to present a realistic picture of the lives and conditions of "Vietnamese brides" and increase social awareness. Protecting the rights of international female migrants and improving their living conditions is a long and arduous task, requiring not only systemic changes at the macro level but also attention and reflection from every member of society.
