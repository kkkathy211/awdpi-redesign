Are Asian women today still only recognizable through a limited set of predefined faces?


In recent years, Hollywood screens have featured an increasing number of Asian female faces.


They are no longer just background extras or side characters, but are beginning to become protagonists of their own stories: some save the world, some run businesses, some tell stories about family and growth, and some even step onto the Oscars stage. On the surface, it seems Asian women have finally achieved an unprecedented level of visibility.


Yet, whenever a new work emerges, discussions about "stereotypes" inevitably resurface.


Recently, *The Devil Wears Prada 2* faced criticism from many Asian audiences over the character Jin Chao, a Chinese American whose portrayal was seen as falling into familiar tropes such as the "Asian academic overachiever," the "nerd," and the "socially awkward" figure. This controversy once again reminds us: As Asian women increasingly occupy central spaces on Hollywood screens, why do these familiar labels remain so persistent?


In the film's setup, the Chinese American character serves as the assistant to the protagonist Andy, a Yale graduate with an outstanding GPA.


This may not be just a problem with *The Devil Wears Prada 2*, but rather a mirror reflecting Hollywood's century-long imagination of Asian women.


From "Dragon Lady" to "Lotus Blossom"

The Western imagination of Asian women


If we look back at Hollywood film history, we find that Asian women have long existed in ways shaped by imagination and external definition.


American scholar Edward Said, in *Orientalism*, pointed out that the West does not simply "discover" the East, but continuously constructs a version of the "Orient" that fits its own fantasies through cultural productions such as literature, art, and film. In this imagination, the East is both mysterious and backward, alluring yet dangerous. And Asian women have been one of the most important vessels of this Oriental fantasy.


In early 20th-century Hollywood, Asian women were almost rigidly confined to two starkly contrasting yet equally stereotypical character types.


The first is the infamous "Dragon Lady."


These women typically possess striking beauty, elegant and mysterious manners, but are cunning, seductive, and even dangerously lethal. This image originated from actress Anna May Wong's portrayal of Princess Ling Moy in the 1931 American film *Daughter of the Dragon*. From early cinema to later pop culture, such women often became the "exotic female" threatening the white male order, directly linked to the late 19th-century Western "Yellow Peril" ideology, casting East Asian women as both fascinating and frightening figures.







*Shanghai Express*

In the 1932 Hollywood film *Shanghai Express*, Anna May Wong plays a high-class courtesan who befriends the female lead on a train and helps her seduce and assassinate a rebel leader.


In contrast, the other image is its complete opposite—the "Lotus Blossom," also known as the "China Doll."


The "Lotus Blossom" represents submission. She is gentle, patient, obedient, self-sacrificing for family and love, with almost no desires or agency of her own. She rarely expresses anger, seldom has a career or life choices, and exists primarily as a supporting figure for male growth, rescue, or emotional fulfillment.


Feminist film theorist Laura Mulvey's "Male Gaze" theory also helps explain this phenomenon. In classical Hollywood narratives, female characters often exist first and foremost as objects of visual consumption, and Asian women become further racialized objects of this gaze. A quintessential example is Chinese American actress Anna May Wong.


As the first Chinese American star to gain international prominence in Hollywood, she possessed exceptional acting talent and immense popularity, yet struggled to break free from the role limitations imposed by her ethnicity. She played countless "Oriental women," but rarely had the chance to portray an ordinary modern woman; she was the mysterious dancer, the spy, the mistress, but almost never the romantic lead, and certainly never an average American citizen.


From "being represented" to "self-expression"

What happens after Asian women become protagonists


By the 1980s and 1990s, Asian women finally began to tell their own stories. The 1993 film *The Joy Luck Club* marked a pivotal turning point in Asian American cinematic history.


Still from *The Joy Luck Club*


This was Hollywood's first time centering its narrative on four Chinese American immigrant mothers and their daughters, allowing Asian women to become the true storytellers. The film explores mother-daughter relationships, immigration experiences, cultural identity, and intergenerational conflict, bringing Chinese American family experiences—previously absent from mainstream view—into the international spotlight. For many Asian audiences growing up in North America, *The Joy Luck Club* meant seeing people like themselves and hearing their own familiar languages in a movie theater for the first time.


However, cultural studies scholar Stuart Hall noted that representation involves continuous selection, construction, and meaning-making, rather than a simple reflection of reality. When a particular narrative dominates public cultural space for a long time, it risks transforming from "one story" into "the truth," eventually becoming a label for the entire group.


Because there were almost no other Asian-themed films entering the mainstream market at the time, *The Joy Luck Club* became, for a long period, nearly the sole window through which American society understood Chinese families. Though the film drew from specific historical, cultural, and immigrant experiences, it was often misinterpreted as a universal model of Chinese family life.


When "excellence" becomes a new template

How the "model minority" reshapes Asian women



In the 21st century, as more and more Asian creators enter Hollywood, the on-screen images of Asian women have begun to grow richer than ever before.


*Crazy Rich Asians* (2018) became the first major Hollywood studio film in twenty-five years to feature an all-Asian cast; *The Farewell* delicately and subtly redefined cross-cultural family experiences; *Turning Red* intertwined adolescence, mother-daughter dynamics, and Chinese American coming-of-age; and *Everything Everywhere All at Once* shattered genre boundaries, casting an ordinary Chinese American immigrant mother as a hero who traverses multiple universes to save the world—earning Michelle Yeoh the first Best Actress Oscar for an Asian performer. Together, these works have transformed the past reality in which Asian women could only play supporting roles. They now have lives far broader than the "Dragon Lady" or "Lotus Blossom" archetypes.


Yet, people are increasingly realizing that while old stereotypes are fading, new ones are quietly emerging.


The concept of the "model minority" first appeared in 1960s American society, used to describe the perceived group traits of Asian immigrants as "hardworking, disciplined, education-focused, and successful through individual effort." On the surface, this may seem like praise, but in reality, it compresses the complex and diverse Asian population into a single personality template, using "success" as the sole measure of Asian worth.


For Asian women, this template often results in similar characterizations: they are high-achieving, graduates of elite universities, professionally competent, rational, restrained, disciplined, emotionally inexpressive, socially awkward, and lacking in humor. They rarely fail, rarely act impulsively, and are always striving, always proving they deserve recognition.


In the recent popular American series *The Morning Show*, Greta Lee's portrayal of an Asian executive embodies a classic "model minority" figure: extremely rational, cold, and wholly absorbed in technology and business—a "high-efficiency work machine." She becomes a "diversity token" who disrupts the traditional white executive hierarchy, yet lacks genuine emotional depth or agency.


This shift also reflects a broader turn in contemporary media culture. As neoliberalism emphasizes individual effort, self-management, and performance-based competition, film and television increasingly favor narratives of "success through hard work." Asian women are often cast as the ideal embodiments of these values. However, such storytelling not only obscures racial discrimination and gender inequality and other structural issues, but also overlooks the real and diverse life experiences of Asian women.


The Chinese American character Jin Chao in *The Devil Wears Prada 2* is a purely functional role, existing only to convey a specific identity to the audience. Viewers need not know her backstory—just a few details—glasses, an elite university, high academic credentials, cautious and restrained demeanor—are enough to instantly categorize her. In contrast to Andy's resistance and compromise, Emily's sharpness and ambition, or Miranda's coldness and complexity, Jin Chao has no desires, no inner conflicts, no growth arc, and no subjectivity of her own.


More importantly, this stereotype operates under a seemingly positive value system. Precisely because of this, such characters are often harder to identify and more difficult to challenge, as they are cloaked in the guise of "praise."


Therefore, the controversy over *The Devil Wears Prada 2* truly reminds us not merely that Hollywood has once again created an "Asian overachiever," but whether Asian women today are still only recognizable within a limited set of faces.



When a character appears, can we first see her as a specific individual rather than immediately identifying which racial template she fits into? Perhaps this is the real standard by which we can measure whether true diversity in media has been achieved.
