Introduction:

As a gender group comprising half of the world's population, women are still defined by the international community as a "gender minority."


Like the LGBTQ community, under moral norms that advocate inclusion and understanding, women continue to be unable to escape systemic gender discrimination and hidden exploitation. Even more alarming is that much of this harm has become so normalized that we accept it as routine—even deceived by its hypocritical appearance into treating it as a tool for self-restraint.


From schools to workplaces, from pregnancy to illness, from public transportation systems to housing construction, from clothing design to street cleaning, from the top-down implementation of public policies to every detail of social life, operational rules established by male designers, engineers, leaders, and decision-makers unconsciously cater to male needs.. Due to widespread gender data gaps, women—who perform approximately 75% of the world's unpaid care work—are often marginalized, their contributions "invisible," and their labor equally unseen.


Image source: Douban Reading


Therefore, as a researcher and scholar long dedicated to advocating for women and marginalized groups, British writer Caroline compiled and documented a wealth of detailed gender data, powerfully revealing contemporary women's struggles through Invisible Women to the public. Some may have been indifferent, looked on coldly, or sneered dismissively, but the solid evidence and indictments presented here are sufficient to awaken their inner questioning of gender equality: Are we really equal enough yet?


If equality has not yet arrived, we must continue to fight for it. Let more cries for help be heard, let more vanished figures be seen. As the quote on the book's opening page states: Let us be unyielding women, and continue to be "difficult" people.

Data Evidence: Women's Silence, Absence, and Marginalization


Simone de Beauvoir said, "To represent the world as world is the activity of men; they depict the world from their own perspective and confuse their viewpoint with absolute truth."


Image source: Freepik


In the book Invisible Women, feminist writer Caroline conducted an in-depth study of the widespread data inequality between men and women. Through extensive research into quantifying women's experiences in families, workplaces, and communities, she revealed gender gaps across various fields, arguing that excluding women from big data only wastes more time, money, and even costs lives. The book visually presents numerous examples of how women are routinely overlooked in daily life, and could even be described as a catalog of persistent gender inequality in society.


Women being paid less for equal work, doing more unpaid labor at home, waiting in longer toilet queues, and being overwhelmingly the victims of various forms of violence—these are no longer surprising facts. But this book quantifies the suffering women endure with numbers, framing imbalances in percentages, offering a more concrete starting point for understanding and combating them.


In an interview, author Caroline pointed out that her motivation for writing the book came from the accidental discovery that symptoms of heart attacks in women differ from those in men—a fact she had never known, or been taught. Women may not experience chest pain or left-arm pain; when they have a heart attack, they might not even realize they are having one. It's not only women themselves—doctors also lack sufficient awareness. Women experiencing heart attacks are 50% more likely to be misdiagnosed, and since 1984, women in the United States and the United Kingdom have been significantly more likely than men to die from heart attacks.


Like this example, the book contains many other data points and cases worth pondering repeatedly: one-third of women worldwide cannot access safe toilets; urban planning and transportation decisions fail to consider the risk of violence against women; it was not until 2011 that U.S. car manufacturers began using female-model crash test dummies. We may already be accustomed to women lacking prominent representative roles in culture, media, politics, and film, but the book points out that this "marginalization" extends into every aspect of life, into fields closely tied to daily living—such as clinical medicine and food science—where even minor oversights can create serious safety hazards.


Image source: Freepik


Caroline notes that globally, women perform as much as 75% of unpaid work, spending 3 to 6 hours daily on such tasks, while men spend only an average of 30 minutes to 2 hours. This phenomenon is not improving; men's share of unpaid work remains "astonishingly stable." Although the gender gap in unpaid labor is influenced by geopolitical, socio-political, and class factors, with very few exceptions, women's total working hours exceed those of men, and this extra workload subtly affects women's physical and mental health, career development, and wealth accumulation.


Taking household labor division as an example, due to women's reproductive roles and established gender role divisions, women are traditionally seen as responsible for raising children, caring for husbands, and managing the household, while husbands are expected to work outside the home. Beck and Beck (1990) argue that the gender conflict is a product of industrial society, rooted in the separation between workplaces and homes, creating fundamental differences in men's and women's daily realities. Women's domestic labor is often unpaid and not recognized as having economic value, so they do not receive fair compensation (even if husbands give part of their wages to their wives, this money is typically used for household expenses and child-rearing, not as recognition of individual labor value). These tasks are highly repetitive and mechanical, offering no advancement for personal career development. The differences in the nature of work between genders are so great that, especially for homemakers dependent on unpaid labor, invisibility and lack of recognition may prevent them from finding personal value or a sense of achievement in daily life and work, further fueling conflicts within the family.


Image source: Freepik


Yet these inequalities are not the result of deliberate conspiracies; no one intends for women to live in a society that is more dangerous and harsher than the one men live in. The real reason is an unbelievably widespread cultural bias—people simply fail to realize that women are the missing data samples, the "invisible" ones.. Yet the strange thing about women's invisibility is that women are not a minority, yet they must live in a society centred around men. From the lack of well-lit, safe streets to the absence of childcare facilities in workplaces, almost everything seems designed for men. How can such a dilemma be resolved? The author's answer is to rethink, collect more data that includes women in basic samples, and study and ask women what they truly want.


It has been three years since the book was published, and even longer since the author completed the manuscript. For a data-driven work, it inevitably has limitations in timeliness and universality, yet this book still holds significant real-world value. Because to this day, the various data biases highlighted in the book show no sign of changing. The truth is: a world designed with men as the standard and center continues to make life difficult for women. As long as gender data gaps remain unfilled, so-called "gender equality" will forever remain superficial "neutral equality."

Work Limitations: Author and Cross-Gender Topic Controversy


Image source: Freepik


Although Invisible Women reveals the plight of women as an overlooked group, author Caroline has been criticized for excluding women who are not cisgender—such as intersex, transgender, and non-binary individuals—from the book. Critics argue the book reflects gender binarism, dividing biological sex and gender into only two categories: male and female. The book's premise assumes only the neglect, exploitation, and threats caused by cisgender men toward cisgender women. A clear example is the book's critique of how women are assumed to be the same as men when collecting data across fields, ignoring biological, socialized, and habitual differences between the sexes. Critics argue that Caroline similarly overlooks how LGBTQ communities suffer marginalization due to society's default norms.


This criticism has gradually escalated into accusations that Caroline is transphobic. While Caroline indeed did not address data collection for transgender people in her book, critics initially failed to recognize that her omission might have been intentional—until Caroline expressed exclusionary views toward transgender people in public forums: Caroline once stated on Twitter that she does not wish to be called cisgender but simply a woman. She refuses to use the term "cis" in her publications because it means "not transgender." However, Caroline has deleted most of her similar tweets, leaving readers unable to investigate the reasons behind her transphobia.


These cases evoke comparisons with J.K. Rowling, who has also been deeply embroiled in controversies over transphobia. Rowling sparked online backlash after expressing strong disapproval on Twitter regarding the use of the phrase "those who menstruate" instead of "women" in an article headline. In response, she stated, "If sex isn't real, then gay attraction isn't real either, If sex isn't real, then the lived reality of women worldwide is erased, While I support transgender people, erasing the concept of sex deprives many of their ability to speak for themselves," implying that allowing transgender women into women's spaces undermines cisgender women's rights.


Image source: Freepik


But in fact, transgender rights and women's rights are not a zero-sum game., On the contrary, both groups share the same goal: challenging the restrictions imposed by traditional patriarchy on the freedom of anyone who is not a cisgender heterosexual man. Caroline's and J.K. Rowling's denial of transgender people does not address the structural problems of patriarchy; instead, it fuels mutual attacks between transgender communities and women's communities.



Conclusion

This book provides extensive statistical data on how decision-making systematically overlooks, obscures, or fails to address gender differences. In the paperback edition, nearly 80 pages of endnotes offer readers the opportunity to follow up on each reference, further exposing the fact that big data treats women as "others."


In the book's preface, the author writes: "At its core, Invisible Women is a call for change." While some readers may believe the era of gender equality has already arrived and that change is no longer necessary, this book serves as a powerful corrective to such thinking. However, this is not a book about policy recommendations or calls to action; it is solely about exposing data bias.


Image source: Freepik


But at the very least, it will inspire those fighting against inequality and motivate others to become more aware of gender bias. Despite its limitations in overlooking the transgender community, this book encourages readers to rethink the world's profound flaws and its boundless possibilities.


It is time for women to be seen.


As Caroline writes in the book: Addressing women's needs is not only a matter of justice, but also a simple economic issue.
