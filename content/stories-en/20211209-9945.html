I. Introduction


The United States, as a nation characterized by racial and ethnic diversity, faces social issues constantly influenced by changes in its racial composition. According to the U.S. Census Bureau's definitions and classifications of race and ethnicity, apart from White Americans, the U.S. currently recognizes five major racial and ethnic groups: Asian,, Black or African American, (Black or African American), Hispanic or Latino, (Hispanic or Latino), American Indian and Alaska Native, (American Indian or Alaska Native), Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander (Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander).


Among them, Asians constitute the largest minority group, numbering approximately 24 million (Neil G. et al., 2021); other data indicate that by 2021, the number of people identifying as Asian in the U.S. was nearly three times higher than 30 years ago (Robert et al., 2021), making it the fastest-growing demographic group.


The more than twenty million Asian Americans are a diverse population whose ancestors come from over 20 countries in East Asia, Southeast Asia, and the Indian subcontinent, including China, Korea, Japan, India, the Philippines, Vietnam, among others. As the largest ancestral group within the Asian population, people of Chinese origin account for 23%a significant portion of the Asian population (U.S. Department of Commerce, 2021). Therefore, when exploring domestic violence issues within the context of American society, it is essential to recognize the distinct challenges different ethnic groups face in experiencing and addressing domestic violence.



In terms of prevalence, domestic violence is considered to potentially affect individuals of any race, age, sexual orientation, religion, or gender, and may occur in various types of relationships, including married, cohabiting, or dating couples. Additionally, the impact of domestic violence extends across


all levels of socioeconomic and educational backgrounds (UN: COVID-19 Response). According to the 2010 summary report of the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's National Intimate Partner and Sexual Violence Survey, an average of 24 people in the United States experience physical violence, rape, or stalking by an intimate partner every minute.


Among Asian Americans, the probability of experiencing such harm from an intimate partner is 19.6%, while for Black Americans it is 43.7%, and for American Indian or Alaska Native women it is 46% (Tjaden et al., 2020). Within the Asian American female population in the U.S., as many as 55%of Asian American women have experienced domestic violence (Brian, 2018).


Domestic violence, as a widespread issue, can occur across all ethnicities and cultures, and the pandemic has further exacerbated this situation. According to the World Health Organization, data from the United Kingdom, the United States, and other countries indicate that since the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic, the number of domestic violence cases has increased (Times, 2021).


When discussing domestic violence, it is necessary to clarify how it is interpreted and defined in different contexts. There is currently no single authoritative, systematic definition of domestic violence. Many similar terms are used interchangeably to describe and discuss this concept—for example, domestic violence may also be referred to as: abuse, battering, intimate partner violence, or family, spousal, relationship, or dating violence..


UNDP (United Nations Development Programme) uses seven paragraphs to introduce the definition of domestic violence, with a key definition stating: Domestic violence refers to violence occurring in private life, typically among individuals connected by love, blood, or legal ties. In short, domestic violence is a violation of human rights (UNDP, 1996).


In the context of the U.S. legal system, the U.S. Department of Justice has established the Office on Violence Against Women, and provided a legal definition of domestic violence: The term "domestic violence" includes acts of violence committed by the victim's current or former spouse or intimate partner, someone with whom the victim shares a child, someone who cohabits or has cohabited with the victim as a spouse or intimate partner, or any act against an adult or youth victim as defined by jurisdictional domestic or family violence laws. (Office on violence against women, 1995).


A local anti-domestic violence organization in Pennsylvania—the Pennsylvania Coalition Against Domestic Violence (PCADV)—states that domestic violence is a pattern of coercive behaviors used by one person in an intimate or family relationship to gain power and control over another person. In summary, we can arrive at a (PCADV, 2000).

In a multi-ethnic country like the United States, domestic violence, as a widespread social problem, presents more complex and unavoidable realities. Among existing accessible data, there is a lack of direct statistics specifically related to domestic violence against Chinese American women. When attempting to focus on Chinese or Asian American women in discussions of domestic violence, we find that most prior research treats the Asian population as a homogenized group, conducting overall analyses. Although these ethnic groups share the label "Asian," they possess different histories, cultures, values, and languages. The


core issue here is that due to the complexity and diversity within the racial category, it is difficult to gain insight into the specific lived experiences and unique challenges faced by Chinese American women. This paper aims to examine the situation of domestic violence against Chinese American women in the U.S., as well as compare it with other ethnic groups..

Profile of victimized women


As previously mentioned, in the United States, Chinese immigrants constitute the largest proportion among Asian populations, yet research on domestic violence against Chinese immigrant women remains limited..


At the practical level, the low reporting rate within Asian communities is also an issue that cannot be ignored. Liu Yuanfen, Executive Director of the New York City Gospel Grace Women and Children Care Center, once stated that among emergency calls in New York City in one year, 270,000 were related to domestic violence, of which 5,000 originated from Chinese households. However, due to the Chinese cultural belief of "not calling the police unless something major happens," she believes the actual number of domestic violence incidents is far higher than 5,000 calls (World Journal, 2019).


For example, many domestic violence cases are only exposed when children exhibit problems at school, at which point schools or neighbors intervene by calling the police. Therefore, this section attempts to reconstruct the profile of victimized Chinese women from fragmented and scattered research reports and literature, and subsequently specifically examine the practical difficulties they face.


(1) Personal circumstances

Age, education and occupation, cultural adaptation, and immigration status


The average age of overseas Chinese women who have experienced domestic violence is 33 years old, and age is significantly correlated with domestic violence experiences—younger women face a higher risk of domestic violence than older women (Yang et al., 2020). In terms of education, these women generally have relatively high educational attainment:——nine out of ten hold bachelor's degrees or higher, with seven out of ten holding master's degrees or above. Some studies also confirm that women with master's degrees are more likely to experience domestic violence (Yang et al., 2021). At the same time, however, their overall income levels are low—, 40%women's income falls at USD 20,000 or below. In terms of employment, 40%of victimized women work full-time, 30%are students, while the proportions who are unemployed, part-time workers, or retired are very small.


From the perspective of cultural adaptation, due to living in a foreign country, immigrant women often have limited English proficiency, do not know the proper ways to access legal aid in American society, and the cultural differences between their home country and host country, along with their relatively low level of adaptation to a heterogeneous society, become triggering factors for domestic violence (Chunrye et al., 2020). More seriously, based on this, many women adopt an attitude of "better not to make trouble," thus hesitating to seek help from the police. Abused Chinese women live in constant fear and have no one to confide in, a difficult situation.


Additionally, immigration status may also be a contributing factor to domestic violence. On one hand, studies show no correlation between immigration status and experiencing domestic violence, yet women who have stayed in the U.S. longer or arrived at an older age face a reversed increase in risk of domestic violence. (Yang Li, 2020).


On the other hand, from the perspective of abused immigrant women, changes in marital power balance caused by immigration can influence the risk of domestic violence. Due to factors such as cultural differences, men in immigrant couples may develop a sense of power imbalance in marriage (Chan & Leong, 1994; Kim, 1996; Miller, 1992; Rhee, 1997). The more they lose decision-making power in intimate relationships, the less they believe they should help domestic violence victims, and the more they think abusive men should not be held responsible or punished for beating their wives..



(2) Marital status

Partner/spouse characteristics, and family power structure


Scholars have proposed two possible reasons, for men's domestic violence against women: one is that gender culture in society tacitly allows men to dominate women, and men use sexual violence to exercise this socially constructed privilege; the second is that when men feel their power is threatened or stripped away by their female partners in marriage, they resort to violence to compensate for this sense of powerlessness.


Among abused women, over 80%of their male partners are Chinese Americans, with an average age of 35 (Yang Li, 2020). The male partners' educational distribution is relatively even, with about 30% holding bachelor's, master's, and doctoral degrees respectively. In terms of employment, nearly 70%of abusive men have full-time jobs, which is lower than the proportion of employed students among victimized women. In terms of income, 10%the distribution across the USD 20,000 to 100,000 range is relatively even (Yang Li, 2020), generally showing a normal distribution pattern.


However, it should also be noted that this conclusion is relative, and some academic studies have reached completely opposite findings regarding occupational status and income levels. For example, Hornung et al. (1981), in measuring the impact of education level and occupational achievement on domestic violence, found that the risk of domestic violence is higher when women rank higher than men in these two aspects.


Regarding internal family power structure, power is reflected in the degree of voice each spouse holds when coordinating household matters. Power itself is a complex, multidimensional structure, making it difficult to measure the correlation between gender and family rights through a single standard..


Under normal circumstances, economic income level is a key factor influencing household decision-making power. Couples in which one party holds absolute dominance experience higher levels of conflict and violence than egalitarian couples, and more notably, the probability of women being abused is highest in families where women hold dominant power. For example, Hotaling et al. (1986) found that women who earn more than their husbands are more likely to suffer physical abuse in their relationships.


In terms of marriage patterns, it remains common among Chinese Americans in the U.S. for husbands to be older than their wives, often by several or even over ten years. Many newly arrived Chinese immigrant women gradually expand their social circles and gain more knowledge about women's rights, often becoming more independent in thought and reducing their dependence on their male partners in marriage. Older husbands may develop fears that their wives will leave them, and express this anxiety through domestic violence (Ren Chengqi, 2007).


American-Chinese lawyer Deng Hong, who has handled many U.S. domestic violence cases, once stated that domestic violence occurs at a relatively high rate in Chinese American families, especially among new immigrant families, with the vast majority of victims being women, and divorces in abusive families often only occur after incidents escalate and police become involved.



(3) Family circumstances

Relatives and children, family influence


From the perspective of family support, before experiencing domestic violence, Chinese American women in the U.S. often have relatively low levels of social support (Yang Li, 2020). Their families usually reside in mainland China, meaning they are effectively disconnected from their original social networks. After suffering domestic violence in the U.S., it is difficult for them to receive timely emotional comfort or practical assistance..


Anti-domestic violence workers from social organizations report frequently receiving complaints from newly arrived immigrant victims, some of whom are isolated by their local communities or have their freedom restricted by abusive partners—prevented from going to work, contacting the outside world, or even going out to buy groceries. If they disobey, abusive partners threaten to withhold documentation needed for green card applications.


From the perspective of children in the nuclear family, studies show that the number of children is not correlated with whether Chinese American women experience domestic violence. However, when abused women consider whether to continue their marriages, children's well-being accounts for a certain proportion of their decision-making.


Jessica Chae, a staff member at DC SAFE, a Washington-based anti-domestic violence advocacy organization, said that the most common thing she tells abused Chinese women during conversations is, "Have you considered applying for child custody?" (Reference News Network, 2015). It is evident that Chinese American women with children, when considering divorce due to domestic violence, are easily influenced by their children and cannot make independent decisions solely for their own protection. This is closely related to traditional Chinese intergenerational culture. are also closely connected.



III. Why don't they seek help?

The self-rescue dilemma faced by Chinese diaspora women


As previously mentioned, when we attempt to reconstruct or depict the profile of Chinese diaspora women experiencing domestic violence, the cultural and practical challenges also come to light, becoming an indispensable part of understanding the current situation. Discussing domestic violence is no longer merely about physical violence, but requires us to turn toward broader social structural factors and some pressing real-world difficulties.


Therefore, building on the previous sections, this part further analyzes the self-rescue dilemmas faced by Chinese diaspora women when confronting domestic or intimate partner violence. Why don't they seek help? What factors hinder their steps toward seeking assistance—these are the questions this section attempts to answer.


(1) The influence of sociocultural background


When we take gender perspective as an entry point to explore the issue of domestic violence faced by women, we actually overlook the victim's cultural and racial identity as an important variable, which influences women's self-awareness, family and marriage values, and ways of resolving conflict.


Fortunately, an increasing number of studies are now focusing on the relationship between culture, ethnicity, and victimized women, centering research on Asian/Chinese communities. This effectively helps us understand how the culture we live by shapes our specific experiences when facing domestic violence. Overall, Asian communities have relatively fewer judicial reporting records (Homes, 2015). Compared to the actual number of victimized women, the reasons behind this gap deserve our reflection and exploration..


01

The "family-centered" concept in cultural traditions


The collectivist culture of "family-centeredness" has deep and multifaceted impacts on women. Whether a marriage is harmonious and the family peaceful becomes a key measure of a woman's life success. Therefore, when facing domestic violence, Chinese diaspora women are more inclined to compromise and retreat in order to preserve family unity and harmony.


Intimate partner violence not only causes serious harm to the victim's physical and psychological well-being but also triggers mechanisms of stigmatization. Overstreet against the victim. Quinn It points out that intimate partner violence (IPV) imposes on women cultural stigma,, internalized stigma, and anticipated stigma—three dimensions of stigma (2013).


Among these, cultural stigma is a structural form of stigma related to ideology, which renders the victim's experience unreasonable, leading her to believe that she is the cause of the abuse. This cultural stigma in Asian families specifically refers to the idea that abuse and domestic violence are private matters, and exposing them externally violates family privacy—"family shame should not be aired in public.".


Abused women often worry this will bring shame to the family. Being in an unhealthy marriage or intimate relationship signifies personal failure. Victims often feel too ashamed to speak up, fearing loss of face. For example, one interviewee said that letting others know her (boyfriend) was like this would make her feel deeply ashamed and afraid of being looked down upon (Li et al., 2021).



02

Perceptions and understandings of domestic violence:

Upholding unequal marital systems


Yick In 1997, a study found that although domestic violence was not widely accepted, half of the respondents still believed that using violence was acceptable under certain circumstances—such as a wife's extramarital affair, emotional outbursts, or disruption of established family roles (e.g., a wife making financial decisions without her husband's consent) (Yick, 1997).


A significant number of men believe that if women do not follow "prescribed" roles, men can use physical violence to "discipline" them (Song, 1996; Tran, 1997). In other words, the root of domestic violence lies in not treating the other person as an independent individual,, and the core of this concept is treating women as the property of men, "objectifying" women as accessories.


Under such a context, domestic violence becomes "a non-issue." Women's inherent subjectivity is thus erased within this collectivist culture. Research shows that in overall domestic violence cases, physical violence accounts for 47.4%, emotional violence 28.1%, sexual violence 21%, and economic violence 3.5%(Chunrye et al., 2020). The Asian community, often seen as a "model minority," appears to have effectively managed domestic violence, but the reality is otherwise. One hidden factor is that Chinese communities often do not recognize psychological, verbal, or controlling behaviors as components of domestic violence, and only forced sexual acts are considered sexual abuse (Midlarsky et al., 2006).


Even when women recognize domestic violence as a problem, it remains difficult for them or their family members to acknowledge the existence of abuse (Lee, 2000). In a specific case cited by researchers, after divorcing her former husband, the victim was still stalked and had her freedom of movement controlled—she was not allowed to leave home unless she went to work. When she told her family, they believed she was not truly abused and insisted that her marriage was what mattered most (Midlarsky et al., 2006).


When facing domestic violence, the most direct and effective self-intervention strategy may be to leave the abuser. This concept of self-protection is rooted in the Western individualist value system that prioritizes the self, conflicting with the Chinese "family-centered" values (Midlarsky et al., 2006). Chinese diaspora women in the U.S. are still influenced by the ideology of "maintaining family integrity."


Some researchers argue that one of the greatest challenges in addressing domestic violence in Asian families is reconciling the Western advocacy of independence and individualism with the Asian ideal of family harmony. Most Asian women still hope to find ways to restore family harmony (Yoshioka & Dang, 2000). "Leaving" is not an easy choice for them..


(2) Practical difficulties in seeking help


The influence of cultural background on how Chinese diaspora women handle domestic violence seems invisible and unnoticeable, yet when they attempt to reach out, the help available remains limited. A comprehensive literature review (Chunrye et al., 2020) shows that the impact of acculturative stress on this group's vulnerability to domestic violence accounts for 17.1%, lack of social capital accounts for 11.4%, and social isolation accounts for 8.6%.


. More importantly, the weakness of social capital, combined with language barriers and cultural insensitivity, due to racial discrimination faced by ethnic minorities, makes it difficult for abused minorities to access local social aid and support systems in the U.S.—such as legal intervention, public education, community programs, mental health services, food assistance,, etc. At the same time, the cultures and policies of the countries of origin of abused ethnic minority women rarely offer them practical solutions, and when some of these women suffer harm, they may voluntarily decline services due to various factors, further worsening their predicament and making escape more difficult.


01

Informal help-seeking: limited social support


Research has found that due to distrust of local authorities and fear of racial discrimination, ethnic minority victims of domestic violence are more likely to rely on informal support such as family, friends, and community leaders rather than seeking help from formal services (Ragavan et al., 2020).


However, it must be noted that because victims have very limited social support and are economically dependent on their partners, informal help-seeking often proves ineffective. They often feel ashamed about being abused, unwilling to be scrutinized, and even fear damaging their partner's reputation.


Scholars generally agree that limited social support networks are a major barrier preventing victims from seeking informal help (Ahmad et al., 2009; Bauer et al., 2000; Li et al., 2021). This is especially pronounced for immigrant women, who often lose critical emotional and instrumental support after migration (Ahmad et al., 2009), making it easy for them to become trapped in abusive situations.


Leaving their original environment means losing existing social relationships and family networks. In a foreign country, they often lack close friends who can offer emotional support or advice (Li et al., 2021). This makes seeking external help even more difficult. More than one Asian immigrant woman mentioned similar struggles during interviews, such as saying: "I don't know who could help me…" "...I don't have many friends. As a housewife, I'm always at home." (Bauer et al., 2000).



02

Formal help-seeking:

Lack of information, awareness, and effectiveness of rescue


Besides cultural influences that make victimized women reluctant to speak about their experiences, lack of knowledge about available resources also places them in a passive position when seeking formal help. They don't know whether shelters for abused women, social services, or other resources exist and are accessible (Bauer et al., 2000). Meanwhile, Chinese immigrant women also lack awareness of seeking psychological counseling (counseling services) and social protection agencies (social services) (Satyen et al., 2019). For example, several Chinese social workers in the U.S. working in domestic violence rescue mentioned that the utilization rate of shelters among abused Chinese women is very low. (Lee, 2000).


In addition, Asian women's attempts to find a way out often stop at using psychological counseling to heal emotional trauma, and even such efforts are often ineffective. Due to significant sociocultural differences, victims find it difficult to receive effective help from social welfare agencies or counselors. In most studies, language barriers are repeatedly mentioned as a key limiting factor.


On one hand, victims struggle to clearly express their problems in a non-native language. Lack of English proficiency creates numerous daily challenges for these women who have traveled far from home, and overcoming language barriers to access psychological counseling or social services is even more unrealistic for them.


On the other hand, professionals who provide assistance, such as counselors and social workers, often fail to understand or empathize with the difficulties faced by immigrant women. For example, participants in one study felt that differences in cultural values made it difficult for counselors to offer truly effective advice (Li et al., 2021).


03

Fear of deportation


The pressure of cultural integration faced by Chinese women who have crossed oceans is often multi-layered, and among these challenges, having stable and legal immigration status (Stable Legal Status) is an undeniable and critical factor. Female victims with unprotected immigration status (such as undocumented or non-permanent legal status) are particularly vulnerable, lacking stable income, language skills, and familiarity with local American culture (Lee, 2000).


Isolated and helpless, they often do not leave their abusers due to fear of deportation (Dutton et al., 2000). Abusers also exploit this fear to control or abuse them. Research has found that when victims lack residency rights or citizenship, they are more likely to suffer more severe abuse (Kim & Sung, 2016).


Faced with complex immigration policies and laws, victims often feel even more powerless. Many states have mandatory arrest policies for domestic violence cases, which sometimes result in the arrest of both the male perpetrator and the female victim. If an immigrant woman seeking police intervention is convicted through this process, she may also face the risk of deportation (Raj & Silverman, 2002), a consequence she cannot afford.



04

The pandemic as an aggravating factor for domestic violence


As previously mentioned, the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic worsened the existing situation of domestic violence: a surge in those seeking help, coupled with increased difficulty in accessing assistance. Due to lockdown policies during the pandemic, couples who previously worked outside the home were confined together, and with added risk factors such as job loss, the likelihood of domestic violence increased. Women and children who were victims no longer had workplaces or schools as temporary shelters, and existing support organizations were also unable to operate normally due to lockdowns. In effect, they were trapped with nowhere to escape..
