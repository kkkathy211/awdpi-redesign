Pioneer of American Christian Feminism and Evangelical Writer

Letha Dawson Scanzoni Passes Away


On January 9, American evangelical writer Letha Dawson Scanzoni died of heart failure at a care facility in Charlotte, North Carolina, at the age of 88. She was one of the founding figures of Christian feminism, and her works not only called for reflection on the critical issue of gender equality in religion but also advanced social movements.


Born in Pittsburgh in 1935, Letha converted to Christianity at age 11. After earning a doctorate in sociology, she taught at Indiana University, specializing in religious studies. She was not only active in religious scholarship but also engaged in social movements through vigorous writing. She firmly believed that women should not be deprived of a voice in religious affairs, nor should they unconditionally submit to their husbands. She contributed to the magazine *Eternity*, challenging traditional views within evangelical Christianity and offering new perspectives on social issues. Her seminal 1974 work, *All We're Meant to Be: A Biblical Approach to Women's Liberation*, reinterpreted the Bible within modern contexts and became a manifesto of Christian feminism.


In 1975, she co-founded the "Evangelical and Ecumenical Women's Caucus" (now renamed Christian Feminism Today), dedicated to social equality. In another major work co-authored with theologian Virginia Ramey Mollenkott in 1978, *Is the Homosexual My Neighbor?*, she sought to de-stigmatize LGBTQ+ Christians through biblical interpretation, offering them love and hope, reflecting her unwavering pursuit of social inclusion and equality.


Letha's life demonstrated her active contributions in religious and social spheres, and her works played a significant role in advancing gender equality, laying crucial intellectual and practical foundations for subsequent social equality movements.

Leiden University research finds high

employment rates among low-income women are associated with reduced income inequality trends


Supported by the Federation of Dutch Trade Unions (FNV), Leiden University professors Egbert Jongen and Heike Vethaak conducted a study revealing key findings on household income trends in the Netherlands: Although income inequality in the Netherlands has increased by 12% over the past 40 years, the rate of increase has been relatively slow. This trend is largely attributed to the growing number of low-income women entering the labor market.

 

The study found that income inequality among men has significantly worsened, while income inequality among women has clearly decreased due to the high labor force participation of low-income women. For low-income groups, unemployment or illness are the main reasons preventing employment, and this group is more likely to lose the protection of permanent employment contracts. Meanwhile, increases in average household disposable income are primarily linked to higher female employment rates and rising incomes. Over the past 40 years, women's employment rate has grown by 2% annually, contributing to a 1.1% annual increase in average household disposable income. Women's incomes have grown by 3.4% annually, compared to only 0.3% for men. However, over the past 20 years, employed women's weekly working hours have increased by 0.8%, reducing the time and energy they can dedicate to family care.

Chinese teacher in exchange program attacked with

chemical liquid in Hawaii, suffers severe burns, suspect arrested


On the evening of January 23, a young Chinese teacher was doused with an unknown chemical liquid on a sidewalk near the Ala Moana Center in Honolulu, Hawaii, suffering severe burns over large areas of her body, including her neck and throat. She is currently receiving treatment in hospital. Local officials confirmed the victim is a Chinese language teacher at a private school, in her second year participating in an international exchange program in the U.S.

 

After the attack, the victim immediately sought help at a nearby gym, where staff treated her wounds in the shower until medical personnel arrived. The victim told eyewitnesses at the gym that she did not know the attacker and had no idea why he targeted her. An eyewitness, holding back tears during a media interview, recalled, "Her clothes were burned off, she was nearly naked. She must have been in so much pain." Honolulu police said the suspect is 29-year-old Sebastian Mahkwan. At the time of the attack, he was wearing a black hooded jacket and camouflage pants and fled the scene on foot. He was later seen riding a bicycle, shirtless.

 

The case has been charged as attempted second-degree murder. Sebastian Mahkwan was arrested on the 25th and charged with attempted second-degree murder and first-degree assault. He made his first court appearance on Monday (29th) local time but refused to admit guilt.

California man accused of domestic violence and murder of Chinese wife,

remains possibly found in Santa Cruz Mountains


On January 16, Los Gatos police in California announced that a 56-year-old man suspected of murdering his 40-year-old Chinese wife has been arrested, and the victim's body was possibly discovered in the Santa Cruz Mountains.

 

Previously, police received a report from the employer of the victim, Yu YingYing, stating they had been unable to contact her since New Year's Eve. On January 8, the Los Gatos-Monte Sereno Police Department began investigating the missing person case. Police said they contacted her 56-year-old husband, John Maxey Yeager, who later provided false information. During the investigation, police reviewed surveillance footage from a neighbor's home and found that Yu had been subjected to domestic violence by Yeager on New Year's Eve and attempted to escape. Yeager was arrested on the 11th in connection with that incident. On the 13th, police confirmed based on community tips that Yeager had previously traveled to the Santa Cruz Mountains. The investigative team located what appeared to be human remains in the area, and the identity will be formally confirmed by the Santa Clara County medical examiner.

 

Yeager is currently held in a detention facility in San Jose and will appear in court for his initial hearing on March 15.

Ali Wong, "Queen of Chinese-American Comedy," wins Golden Globe and Emmy, becomes first Asian woman to win lead actress award


At the Golden Globe Awards ceremony on January 7 in the United States, Ali Wong, the highest-paid Chinese-American stand-up comedian, known as the "Queen of Chinese-American Comedy," won the Best Actress in a Limited Series award at the 81st Golden Globe Awards for her outstanding performance in *Beef*, marking the first time an Asian actor has won a performance award in the limited series category at the Golden Globes.

 

The Netflix limited series Beef is a brilliant comedy series starring Ali Wong and Korean-American actor Steven Yeun. The two play participants in a road rage incident, whose already chaotic lives become increasingly entangled, gradually descending into their own personal hells. Ali Wong not only stars in the show but also serves as an executive producer.

 

Ali Wong's award is absolutely well-deserved, as her performance elevates the show's unique comedic tone throughout. The New York Times highly praised Ali Wong's performance, calling her work one of the most exciting, astonishing, and insightful performances of the past year.

 

On the evening of January 15, the 75th Emmy Awards were held at the Peacock Theater in Los Angeles. Ali Wong won the Emmy Award for Outstanding Lead Actress in a Limited or Anthology Series or Movie for her performance in Beef, becoming the first Asian American actress in history to receive this honor. This marks another time she and Steven Yeun have jointly won Best Actress and Best Actor awards, following their wins at the Golden Globes.

Asian American parents sue New York Department of Education,

alleging racial discrimination in STEM program


A group of Asian American parents has recently filed a lawsuit against the New York State Department of Education, challenging a STEM after-school program called the Science and Technology Program (STEP). They argue that the program's admissions criteria, which target low-income and "historically underrepresented" students, exclude Asian and white students.


The Science and Technology Program aims to provide eligible students in grades seven through twelve with additional laboratory research opportunities, tutoring, and college application guidance. The program requires participants to be Black, Hispanic, or Native American, or to have a household income below a specific threshold. The complaint states: "A Hispanic child from a millionaire family is eligible for the STEP program, while an Asian child whose family income is just at New York State's low-income level is not—solely because of race." In the 2021–2022 academic year, the STEP program admitted over 12,209 students, the majority (over 10,000) being Black or Hispanic.


The plaintiffs are asking the court to prohibit the use of "racial classification" in determining student eligibility for the program, arguing that it is unfair to Asian and white students. William Jacobson, the plaintiffs' attorney and founder of the Equal Protection Project, said: "Asian and white students often face additional barriers when applying for this program. We believe this is illegal—educational programs should treat all students equally." However, Nyah Berg, Executive Director of New York Appleseed, an organization advocating for school racial integration, argued that the case represents a "distortion" of the protections for minority rights under the Fourteenth Amendment of the U.S. Constitution. She emphasized that Asian and white students are already overrepresented in STEM fields, and warned that this lawsuit could further undermine protections for systematically oppressed groups.
