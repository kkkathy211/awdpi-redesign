Guest self-introductions

Doris: I am currently residing in a rural area of the UK. I have spent many years working in education, culture, and enterprise management, transitioning from scientific research into socioeconomic and talent management and development fields—a slash career practitioner. I am also a single mother who experienced domestic violence during the pandemic and is now raising my child independently. I currently serve as the head of the UK branch at Avoice. I first learned about Avoice through online searches and consultations, stayed in long-term contact, and joined the organization after being drawn to its public welfare mission upon seeing a volunteer recruitment announcement.

Zhezhe: I was once a survivor of domestic violence. I experienced abuse during my first year of high school, which gradually led to depression and PTSD (post-traumatic stress disorder), causing me to take leaves of absence and return multiple times during high school and university, before eventually graduating from UCLA. My personal experience has long troubled me, but it also motivated me to engage with social issues such as women's protection.


01. Domestic Violence Post-Pandemic

Q: What was your motivation for joining Avoice?

Doris: I came to the UK after working in China, pursued further studies, and decided to stay. During the pandemic, I experienced severe domestic violence. This experience allowed me to see myself and other women in similar situations struggling to rise from pain and rebuild their lives. I hope to be a force that guides women to stand up from suffering—that is why I joined Avoice.

From what I understand, many women experiencing domestic violence feel fear and resistance when trying to resolve the issue. In this process, many people around them may remain silent, indifferent, unsupportive, or fail to understand. Many women may also choose silence to protect others and their children. Yet the person who needs protection the most—herself—lacks support and strength.


Q: How has the situation of domestic violence changed after the pandemic compared to before?

Doris: Due to the sudden outbreak of the pandemic, many women were forced to return home or change jobs to work in pandemic relief and healthcare, leading to shifts in their economic status and family dynamics. After returning home, women spent significantly more time at home, and overall family stress increased dramatically. Globally, family conflicts and domestic violence cases surged sharply.

According to UN data from 2020, approximately 243 million women aged 15 to 49 worldwide experienced sexual or physical violence by an intimate partner in the year before and after the outbreak of the coronavirus. Such violence is estimated to have cost the world 1.5 trillion USD. After the pandemic, this number continued to rise. Reports of domestic violence increased by 30% in France. Canada, Germany, Spain, and the UK also saw increased demand for emergency shelters. In South Korea, reports of domestic violence decreased, but this does not mean abuse declined—rather, most victims faced barriers to reporting.

A quote from the UN Secretary-General deeply resonated with me. He said that during the pandemic, for many women and girls, the greatest threat was not the virus, but their own homes—the place where they should feel safest. Because of pandemic lockdown policies, many victims were under control or had limited contact with others, causing most violent incidents to remain hidden within households, difficult for others to see or for rescue agencies to detect in time. Victims in immediate danger had to resort to reporting via email or even text messages, sometimes without even the chance to make a phone call.
In some countries, the number of women seeking support services increased, but at the same time, healthcare providers and police systems faced staff shortages. These institutions became overwhelmed, many local support teams were paralyzed or underfunded, and numerous shelters had to close due to the pandemic.

02. Challenges and Solutions in Domestic Violence
Q: What are the common and significant issues and needs faced by overseas Chinese women experiencing domestic violence?
Doris: Around the world, existing domestic violence support systems proved extremely fragile during the pandemic, with many delays, making it difficult to meet the diverse needs of overseas Chinese women.
First, safe housing is needed. This was the biggest issue during the pandemic. Many women had to stay with their abusers, and others could not provide timely assistance. Sometimes, due to insufficient local shelters and pandemic lockdowns, victims could not reach a safe place to escape the abuser.
From what I understand, various rescue organizations also tried their best to help, but coordination was extremely difficult. They conducted a series of interviews and assessments for victims, which sometimes took a very long time. During this period, victims had to endure further abuse, potentially leading to escalated violence by the abuser.
Another need is timely social attention and advocacy. Due to language barriers and social isolation, these Chinese women often depend on their spouses. The spouse may exert economic and other forms of control, prohibiting her from seeking help from others or even leaving the abuser's side. Victims may also lack sufficient understanding of local domestic violence laws and regulations, and lack awareness of their rights.
Currently, most overseas Chinese women experiencing domestic violence seek help through embassies or local Chinese friends, but they rarely access formal institutions such as judicial departments, local law firms, or health services. Only 10% of women directly seek help from the police. From what I understand, the police also faced difficulties handling these cases during the pandemic.
Another concern is immigration status. Many Chinese women belong to marginalized groups and worry that once they report abuse, their children or entire family might be deported, or they may lose access to social resources, making it difficult to survive locally. In the UK, local support organizations such as Women's Aid can refer victims to immigration lawyers for assistance. However, visa applications and processing involve a lengthy procedure.
Another concern is the safety of children and family members. Many women, especially those with children, may escape themselves but cannot take their children with them. For mothers, it is emotionally difficult to sever family ties, which is a major reason they choose silence.
Additionally, they urgently need language support, legal aid, financial assistance, psychological counseling, judicial support, and restraining orders. While such resources exist, it was extremely difficult to implement them effectively during the pandemic.

Q: For overseas Chinese women who may be in the depths of domestic violence, how can they save themselves?
Doris: I have a simple survival guide for overseas women experiencing domestic violence. It is based on the UK context, so it has some specificity, but also general applicability as a reference.
First, we need to safely communicate the situation of domestic violence to others. When you cannot contact the outside world at home, you can send messages via your phone or the internet. When going out, you may notice that in the UK there are some helplines. There are also safety gestures and safety passwords. Once a woman says this password, it indicates she is in danger, and someone will help her make contact.
Second, collect evidence promptly. As a survivor, I really want to share this point. The safest way to collect evidence is to have a home surveillance camera. It can provide crucial evidence when you file a report, helping protect your rights in various aspects.
Furthermore, through conversations with many women who have had similar experiences, I learned that many did not realize they were already in danger before the violent acts occurred. The essence of domestic violence is control over women. Before physical violence occurs, the abuser may have already placed the victim in a vulnerable and dangerous position, just using subtle methods so the victim doesn't realize it. By the time violence erupts, the abuser already has full control over you. In such circumstances, leaving is difficult and requires preparation. First, report to the police promptly and always carry important emergency contact numbers. During the pandemic, maintain open communication with neighbors or people you know, so you can receive emergency help if any risk arises.

Finally, if you decide to leave your partner, careful planning is essential. According to some online cases, when the abuser learns the victim intends to leave, he may become extremely violent—this is the most dangerous moment. In other words, knowing how to leave safely is extremely important. Therefore, you should promptly secure your key personal documents and seek legal counsel and judicial assistance through official channels. If you have nowhere to go, there are now emergency shelters available in the UK, or you can go to a safe place—such as a friend's home or somewhere the abuser doesn't know—before taking further action.


In the following section, Zhezhe shares with us her personal experiences and reflections as a survivor of domestic violence.

03. The Harms of Domestic Violence and Self-Protection

Q: When you were young, did you ever experience violence from a family member? What kind of impact did that experience have on your life and studies, and how long did that impact last?

Zhezhe: At first, I thought that once it happened, it was over. But in reality, the impact of domestic violence is quite severe. I've always considered myself a brave girl, and at the beginning, I thought I wouldn't be affected. But gradually, I realized I couldn't sleep alone anymore, because the violent incident had occurred in my room in the middle of the night. Later, I met a counselor who told me this was actually a very serious event—because being attacked in your own room at night, when you're most deeply asleep and in the place where you feel safest, is deeply traumatic. It also involves your level of undress at the time. Fortunately, I was wearing relatively proper sleepwear, but my sense of safety was severely damaged.

During that time, even when my mother stayed with me while I slept, my nerves remained tense. I would startle at the slightest sound, and every night was the most stressful time. One day at school, while reading a fashion magazine, I came across a news story about a female celebrity in the US who had been blinded by domestic violence. I suddenly felt darkness closing in, even though I was in a bright classroom surrounded by classmates. I felt plunged into endless darkness, utterly hopeless and deeply shaken.

From then on, I unconsciously began paying close attention to news about women's personal safety. I no longer saw such news as something distant and unrelated to me. I began to realize these incidents could happen to anyone. I became much more vigilant.

In fact, during high school, I was already diagnosed with depression and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), because I was easily overwhelmed by social news and would collapse emotionally. I also felt the world was no longer safe or kind, and I often fell into despair. The lack of understanding from others, moral coercion, and victim-blaming made me feel extremely isolated. At the time, I felt my worldview was completely shattered. I thought the world was surreal. My academic performance began to decline due to depression, PTSD, and a sense of hopelessness. My thoughts became scattered, I couldn't concentrate, and I developed many physical symptoms—such as stomach problems, constant dry heaving, loss of appetite, and worsening health. When I was studying abroad in the US, I saw the news about Zhang Yingying's case and completely broke down. I couldn't even leave the house to go to school because I felt so unsafe and emotionally shattered. Later, I returned to China and was hospitalized. After diagnosis, I slowly recovered with medication.


Q: After experiencing that violence, did you speak out to others—such as your parents or friends? How did they react?

Zhezhe: What made this experience feel surreal to me was that domestic violence survivors often struggle to be understood. This is something that further hurts survivors. Others who haven't experienced it truly cannot understand your feelings and fears, nor the impact domestic violence has on you. I've experienced victim-blaming, moral coercion, and people who simply didn't take it seriously—I've been through it all.

At first, my father absurdly labeled the incident as "kids fighting," rather than a serious act of violence. He was completely dismissive, refused to let me see a psychologist, and even thought there was no need to go to the hospital. When I went to the hospital emergency room, the doctor looked at me with a curious, gossipy gaze. Some classmates even made jokes about it or casually urged me to forgive the abuser.

Moreover, when I went abroad to study due to a lack of safety, one classmate even said I had delusions of persecution. My parents and many relatives believed I had to forgive, otherwise I was overreacting. There was even a bizarre idea that you must forgive in order to let go, and that to free yourself, you must also let the abuser go. It wasn't until later, after repeated communication and when the abuser (my brother) began to frighten my mother, that she became the first person who could truly understand me—because she began to feel fear herself. My father, as an adult man who is physically strong, still cannot understand.

My takeaway from this experience is: never doubt your genuine feelings. Two people who have experienced the same level of domestic violence might react differently, but that doesn't mean if your reaction is stronger, you are mentally fragile. The violence itself is wrong—not how the survivor reacts. Any authentic reaction from a survivor is valid and correct. I hope people who haven't experienced domestic violence can try to understand survivors' feelings. And I hope survivors themselves won't be brainwashed by others.


Q: How can one prevent and identify abusers early on?

Zhezhe: First, I believe abusers do have identifiable traits. I once searched for this on Zhihu, and I recommend everyone take a look. Abusive men often have strong controlling tendencies, emotional instability, short tempers, and get angry over small things. They often have two faces—they switch personas, and when they do, you feel like they've become a completely different person. That's when you should be on high alert.

If you're careless, you might think it's just a momentary impulse or outburst, but that's not the case. Abusers often have their own twisted logic and are difficult to reason with. They frequently demand that you agree with their views. They are often chauvinistic, disrespectful toward women, like to shift blame, are self-centered, and constantly test your boundaries. Then they apologize—quickly and seemingly sincerely—but they never change. They may also have a dark side, or tend to assume the worst about people. Many abusive men are actually skilled at hiding their true nature and often appear mild-mannered in public. So I think you shouldn't let appearances lull you into complacency.


Q: There are still many women around the world who have experienced domestic violence and remain under its shadow. As someone with firsthand experience, what advice do you have for women trapped in such depths?

Zhezhe: After experiencing domestic violence, there are two things you need to keep in mind:

First, never forgive the abuser just because they appear to be crying bitterly and apologizing sincerely. Survivors often have a mindset of trying to change the abuser to recover from their losses. It feels as if the abuse then has meaning, as if they're saving someone, as if they're being noble. But in reality, the only way to stop evil is to leave. Forgiveness equals silent approval, indulgence, and implies that domestic violence is acceptable. This prevents the abuser from learning any lesson.

Moreover, the moment the abuser first lays hands on you, the relationship is no longer equal. Domestic violence overturns the unwritten rules of civilized society. And because in some places, domestic violence is ignored or condoned—both legally and socially—the abuser, even after becoming a barbarian, pays very little price. Leaving is the only way to minimize losses. I also don't believe survivors and abusers should continue to coexist. If they can resort to violence, it means they no longer see you as an equal human being. Domestic violence is never just a momentary impulse. Even if it were, someone so easily triggered is not someone you should stay with.

Second, don't let others brainwash you. No matter how many people try to persuade you with victim-blaming, moral coercion, or similar views, don't listen. Don't doubt yourself. You can research how such cases are handled abroad—how they are sentenced and managed.

If you've already suffered domestic violence, my advice is:

First, call the police. If the police try to downplay it, record the officer's badge number, demand they follow proper procedures, request an official incident report, and require the abuser to submit a written statement of remorse to the police station or relevant community organization.

Second, go to the emergency room immediately to have your injuries documented as evidence. Use this evidence to claim maximum rights during divorce litigation. In many major cities now, there are fast-track online channels for filing domestic violence cases, such as "Rongyisu" in Chengdu.

Third, if needed, find yourself a good psychotherapist. Ideally, the therapist should have experience in trauma healing, such as EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing), DBT (Dialectical Behavior Therapy), and exposure therapy. However, it's crucial to find a reliable professional, as trauma can easily trigger re-traumatization. If you suspect you have a mental health condition—such as phobia, anxiety, depression, or post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)—you should seek medical help promptly; medication can be very helpful.


04. Q&A Session

Q:  Emotional neglect and refusal to communicate are also forms of domestic violence. How should one address or differentiate this? How can we identify some hidden patterns of domestic violence, such as passive responses, emotional blackmail through accusations, and domestic violence in same-sex relationships, which tend to be more concealed?

As domestic violence gains more social attention, a physically abusive man who uses force is relatively easier to identify, and family, friends, and society can quickly respond and offer help. However, other forms of abuse masked as "for your own good," "I'm protecting you," or "I love you too much" — such as emotional blackmail and gaslighting — are still difficult for me to recognize. Is there a simple way to tell the difference?

Doris: Before many acts of domestic violence occur, the abuser's behavior is often very subtle. I encourage everyone to look up "gaslighting." Verbal and emotional abuse is just the initial stage. The abuser uses unpredictable language or emotional shifts to manipulate feelings, a tactic especially common in intimate relationships. In such relationships, you usually trust your partner deeply, making it easy to fall into a state of manipulation and deception. The abuser's emotional unpredictability can leave the victim feeling deeply depressed and severely damage their self-confidence.

The abuser may also offer occasional kindness, such as apologies, making arguments seem like a way to strengthen the relationship. In reality, you've unknowingly entered a vicious cycle of control and submission, so intervention is needed at this stage. I strongly recommend viewing your situation from a third-party perspective, which may help you gain a clearer understanding. Otherwise, it's extremely difficult to escape such emotional control once you're immersed in the relationship. Throughout the process, the other person constantly criticizes, blames, and even belittles you, making you feel you've done something wrong and leading to endless self-reflection. This is actually a method and pathway the abuser uses to control you.


Q: How can we help someone who has long lived under the subtle shadow of domestic violence rebuild their life and confidence?
Doris: Offer long-term support to the person seeking help. Seek professional support and psychological counseling, and connect with supportive friends who can provide strength. I hope everyone can pay more attention to the sisters around them who might be experiencing such situations.

Zhezhe: Actually, I think the most important thing is, if you can firmly avoid doubting yourself from the beginning, you can largely walk away. I think gradually building correct awareness, reading more articles from Avoice, and learning more about psychology can help. In addition, social support is still essential. Gradually build a new circle that is separated from the abuser, and make friends with people who have the right attitude toward anti-domestic violence. Then you'll realize you're right, that you've done nothing wrong, and you'll regain confidence.

At that time, a psychologist from the United States gave me great support; they could understand my feelings and acknowledged the seriousness of the situation, which I found very comforting.

Doris: Many women have high expectations for love and marriage. I hope that no matter what role you're in, you won't lose yourself—stay clear-headed. No matter your role, no matter where you are, you must clearly know where you are and who you are. Never forget that you are yourself, never forget to be yourself, never forget to love yourself, because ultimately, only you can save yourself.

Guest Self-Introduction

Zhajikuai (Fried Chicken): I'm in China, studying at university, currently based in Nanjing, and this year is essentially my gap year. I've been exploring the public welfare sector over the past two years. I joined the Avoice Overseas Chinese Women Protection Project at the beginning of the 2020 pandemic and am now the head of the Case Coordination Department. Besides managing this department and liaising with those seeking help, I also oversee volunteers.



01. Sharing Experience in Case Coordination Work
Q: How did you join the Avoice organization?
"

For me, the driving force comes from aligning knowledge with action. I believe advocating for women's rights must be reflected in action, not just words or expressing opinions. Discussion is very important, but I think it must be projected into real life.

"

Zhajikuai: There are several background reasons.
The first was the immediate context: during the pandemic, I followed many news stories about domestic violence, such as how women abroad were being abused during lockdowns.
The second reason is more long-standing. Over the past two or three years, we've seen many serious incidents harming women in the Chinese-speaking online world, and I've participated in many discussions on online platforms, thinking about how we could help these women and prevent such incidents. After discussion, I felt I wasn't doing enough and began reflecting on my own values, wondering if I could take action and do something meaningful.
For me, the driving force comes from aligning knowledge with action. I believe advocating for women's rights must be reflected in action, not just through talking or expressing opinions. Discussion is very important, but I think it must be projected into real life. At that time, I happened to see the Avoice founder post information about the project in a group, and I thought it was a great opportunity. It was also during the pandemic, when I couldn't attend school, so I had more free time. I joined the project with a simple intention—to try, and to help women.

Q: What exactly does your case coordination work at Avoice involve?
Zhajikuai: That brings us to the functions of our Case Coordination Department. Currently, Avoice functions as a safe and fast online coordination platform. The coordination management team consists of frontline volunteers, somewhat like front desk receptionists.
Injured women reach out to us through Avoice's online promotional information and contact our assistant. Our assistant creates a WeChat group and assigns a volunteer to learn about the situation from the female survivor. Then, based on the woman's specific circumstances and the severity of harm, the volunteer helps connect her with various internal and external resources provided by Avoice.
Our organization has many internal resources, including cooperating legal advisors and psychological counselors, multilingual volunteers, as well as external resources. We've built extensive overseas assistance networks and Chinese-language databases. For example, international survivors often need help from local organizations, and our volunteers use information from our database to match them with suitable groups.

02. Moments of Light in Volunteer Work

Q: During the process of coordinating with these survivors, what experiences have left a particularly deep impression on you?

Zhajikuai: As the head of the Case Coordination Department, I conduct interviews with our volunteers. During interviews, my first question is usually: Please briefly introduce yourself. The second question is: Why do you want to join us? Why do you want to join this department? Perhaps these are very cliché questions, but I've heard many deeply moving answers.

This experience has left a profound impression on me. Sometimes, you understand that volunteers may need to say nice things to pass the interview. But when I hear applicants and volunteers say, "I want to contribute to gender equality," "I want to help women who've been harmed," or "I want to make a difference," no matter how many times I hear it, I still feel deeply moved. Regardless of whether the volunteer ultimately joins us or continues with related work in the future, I believe that at that moment, they genuinely want to help these women. I feel a connection between us—we share a common goal.

Q: During the process of coordinating with these survivors, what experiences have left a particularly deep impression on you?

Zhajikuai: I think this is a great question, because I rarely ask myself whether it's worth it. Instead, I ask myself: Have I made a difference? This is also related to the nature of our department's work. We've recently been discussing how to meet volunteers' expectations. Everyone joins our organization with a simple wish—to contribute to helping women. But when I first started coordinating with survivors, I felt somewhat powerless, and even worried about being negatively affected emotionally..

For example, my first female survivor in 2020 had a very complex situation. To protect her privacy, I can't share many details, but as a new volunteer, all I could do was listen to her story and help connect her with relevant resources. It was emotionally exhausting to follow up, because there were many moments when I thought: her situation is so complicated—can I, as a young person from a newly established organization who isn't even by her side, really help her? As the liaison manager, most of my work involved connecting her with other internal and external resources, and I often felt that the real problem-solving was being done by teammates from other departments. When connecting with other individuals seeking help later on, I also had this feeling. that some people's problems were extremely difficult, and we couldn't help them resolve everything right away.

But gradually, I began to notice something. For example, the first woman I supported expressed gratitude when I followed up with her for the first time. She said she was deeply thankful that I still remembered her and cared about her situation. Although we hadn't yet helped her solve her biggest problem, when she said that, I was deeply moved. I clearly remember another woman I had supported said something similar: "When you feel helpless, having someone standing behind you offering support truly feels amazing."

It was precisely the feedback from each person seeking help that made me understand the value of what I do, which is to listen to their stories and offer them support within my capacity. support. For these women who have been harmed, this is extremely important. The process and experience of supporting women have shown me the significance and value of this liaison management work.
