Perspective


The two agencies have distinct mandates, and forcibly merging them could not only weaken their respective functions and funding security but also fail to truly safeguard the best interests of women and girls worldwide.

Authors: Shannon Kowalski, Jessica Stern

March 12, 2026


As the United Nations struggles with a funding crisis, the UN Secretary-General has proposed merging UN Women—the specialized agency responsible for gender equality and women's empowerment—with the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA), the agency dedicated to sexual and reproductive health. However, this proposed merger poses multiple risks for women and girls globally.




Currently, backlash against gender equality has reached unprecedented levels worldwide: Over one-quarter of countries face significant risks of regression in core rights for women's bodily safety and legal equality. In this context, weakening the UN's capacity to address these issues would undoubtedly result in serious setbacks.


Merging two distinct functions risks weakening both


Bringing two mandates under one risks weakening both


It is essential to remember that each agency was established with a clear and specific mission: UN Women primarily focuses on developing global norms, laws, and policies related to gender equality, coordinating actions at the global level, and implementing programs in countries to advance women's economic empowerment, political participation and leadership, and the prevention of gender-based violence, among other transformative issues.


Established in 2010 by consolidating four previous UN mechanisms on gender equality, the agency was created to address long-standing challenges such as insufficient resources for gender equality work, fragmentation within the UN system, and slow global progress.


 UNFPA primarily focuses on program implementation, working to improve access to sexual and reproductive health services, especially in crisis and conflict settings. The agency also addresses challenges arising from demographic changes, including aging populations, migration, and the needs of adolescents. Within this framework, UNFPA promotes bodily autonomy for women and girls and works to eliminate harmful practices such as child marriage and other forms of violence.


On the surface, a merger might appear to enhance efficiency, but in reality, UN Women and UNFPA have significantly different operational mandates. Although sexual and reproductive rights are deeply intertwined with gender equality, the practical overlap in their day-to-day work is limited.


UNFPA primarily operates at the country level, partnering with national ministries of health, planning departments, demographers, and organizations working on sexual and reproductive health and rights; in contrast, UN Women works at the national, regional, and global levels, collaborating with national gender equality bodies, feminist organizations, and gender-focused institutions to advance related agendas.


Merging the two could instead weaken the UN's voice and leadership on gender equality. An independent agency specifically dedicated to advancing the human rights of women and girls would be absorbed into a multi-issue system, potentially downgrading gender equality from a core mission to just one of many important issues.


Risks at the funding level


The money issue


Notably, the merged entity may not necessarily attract more resources. If "increased efficiency" is used as a justification for the merger, donor countries that currently fund both agencies may find it difficult to maintain existing funding levels, let alone increase their contributions.


Against a backdrop of widespread cuts to development budgets and reduced spending by donor nations, funding for gender equality, sexual and reproductive health, and rights has already declined sharply. Proceeding with a merger at this moment could exacerbate, rather than reverse, this trend.


While UN Women is the designated agency for gender equality, all UN system entities must advance the rights of women and girls within their respective mandates—this is precisely what is needed to achieve substantive change.


Following the UN's comprehensive system-wide evaluation of gender equality efforts in 2023, the Secretary-General launched the "Gender Equality Acceleration Plan" to strengthen resource allocation and inter-agency coordination on this front. Yet, only 36% of UN agencies have met their self-set benchmarks for gender equality funding, and less than 25% of national-level frameworks include projects that integrate gender equality.


These failures in implementation cannot be resolved through institutional merger—what is truly needed is political leadership and accountability. On the contrary, a merger could further "silo" the UN's gender equality work, concentrating responsibility within a single agency and weakening the breadth and strength of system-wide action.


Potential risks in the merger negotiation process


The risk of negotiations over the merger


Any merger proposal must be approved by UN member states through a vote in the United Nations General Assembly (UNGA). Supporters claim that the mandates of the two agencies would remain intact post-merger, but the dynamics of the negotiation process and the final voting outcome are, in reality, unpredictable and uncontrollable.


Just this week, during negotiations on the outcome document of the UN Commission on the Status of Women (CSW), the United States and several other countries explicitly opposed language related to marginalized women and girls, actions to address inequalities, and mechanisms linking gender and human rights. They also opposed references to responsible technology, artificial intelligence, and even the right to privacy.


This stance continues a series of previous U.S. government policies, including the significant expansion of the "Global Gag Rule," the cessation of funding for UNFPA, the weakening of protections for LGBTQ+ rights, and even threats to destroy taxpayer-funded contraceptive supplies.


It is foreseeable that the U.S. government and other anti-gender-equality forces could exploit this merger process to further dismantle related UN agendas. In 2026, underestimating the influence of such opposition would be dangerous.


In fact, there are many viable alternatives to enhance the UN's overall impact in advancing the rights of women and girls. For example, joint planning and budgeting mechanisms could strengthen inter-agency coordination and effectiveness on cross-cutting issues—such as in women's and girls' economic empowerment, where UN Women, UNICEF, UNDP, and the International Labour Organization all play critical roles.


Additionally, increasing representation of gender equality organizations on UN Women's Executive Board would help enhance their participation in governance and decision-making.



Clearly, the current situation is unsustainable. Yet, disappointingly, the leaders of the UN's "UN80 initiative" have not exhausted all possible reform pathways, instead proposing only a merger plan that has neither been advocated for nor supported by women's rights and gender equality organizations.


The UN indeed needs to evolve and fulfill its mission more effectively. However, neither UN Women, UNFPA, nor the global cause of women's and girls' rights should become casualties of this latest reform. The UN Secretary-General must halt this proposal before it is too late.
