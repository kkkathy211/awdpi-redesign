When a classic love tragedy is rewritten,

can women have a different ending?


On October 23, 2026, the bilingual Chinese-English original play *Juliet & Jul-Yingtai* (*Juliet and Zhu Yingtai*), produced by Vermilion Theater, premiered in Jersey City, New Jersey. After the performance, Asian Women Development Plan International (AWDPI) was invited to participate in a post-show dialogue, joining the creative team and audience to discuss domestic violence, survivor rights protection, and the real-world challenges Asian women face when seeking legal assistance.


The panel was moderated by Dou Rui, Executive Producer of the play, with WU Tiantian, Director of AWDPI's U.S. Office, and LI Jialu, an actress and survivor of intimate partner violence, as panelists.


When classic stories reflect reality:

Can women rewrite their own destinies?


*Romeo and Juliet* and *Liang Shanbo and Zhu Yingtai* are well-known tragic love stories in Eastern and Western literature. In both tales, the female protagonists struggle between familial expectations, social norms, and personal choices, ultimately ending their lives for love.


*Juliet & Jul-Yingtai* breaks this predetermined ending, allowing the two women—spanning cultures and time—to meet. Through dialogue on trauma, memory, freedom, and self-determination, the play re-examines women's positions within intimate relationships and social structures.


The story on stage also raises critical real-world questions: When women face violence and control, do they have the freedom to leave? And what kind of support can society offer them?




From stage to reality:

Recognizing the complexity of domestic violence


During the panel, WU Tiantian from AWDPI's U.S. Office shared insights from the organization's anti-domestic violence work, introducing the audience to various forms of domestic violence and the difficulties survivors may encounter when seeking protection.


Domestic violence is not limited to spouses or romantic partners; it can also occur among other family members. Violence is not only physical harm but may also involve psychological, emotional, and economic control and abuse.


The discussion particularly focused on protection orders (Order of Protection)


in the U.S. legal system. WU Tiantian pointed out that the public often holds two misconceptions about protection orders: one is that they are merely "a piece of paper" that cannot truly help survivors; the other is that once obtained, a protection order completely resolves safety concerns.


In reality, protection orders are legally enforceable and serve as important protective tools, but they do not eliminate all risks. Since survivors and abusers may still share work, family, or other life connections, true safety often requires a combination of legal support, community resources, and ongoing safety planning.




Listening to survivors' voices:

Why is the path to help so difficult?


Actress LI Jialu shared her personal experience as a survivor of intimate partner violence during the panel.


She spoke about how, beyond physical and psychological trauma, survivors may face complex legal procedures, disputes over relationship recognition, and challenges in obtaining effective protection.


She also shared how a protection order functioned in her real life, helping the audience better understand the significance and limitations of legal protection measures.


For many Asian women living in the United States, language and cultural differences may further increase the difficulty of seeking help.


Even with strong English communication skills, accurately describing their experiences and understanding legal terminology during emergencies, police interviews, or court proceedings can still be a significant challenge.


Language barriers should not become obstacles to survivors accessing protection and legal remedies.



AWDPI:

Supporting more survivors with understanding and assistance


During the panel, WU Tiantian introduced AWDPI's work in supporting Asian domestic violence survivors.


For many survivors encountering the U.S. legal system for the first time, knowing how to find a lawyer, understand legal procedures, obtain reliable translation, and identify available resources can feel overwhelming.


AWDPI is committed to connecting survivors in need with legal and language support resources, including helping them find lawyers or interpreters who can communicate in Chinese, reducing the impact of language and information barriers on the help-seeking process.


WU Tiantian emphasized: Survivors of domestic violence should never be blamed. What they need is understanding, respect, and practical help.


Anti-domestic violence work is not only about legal protection but also about building a community environment that is willing to listen, believe, and support survivors.


Taking the conversation beyond the theater, ensuring support continues


Art not only tells stories but also creates space for experiences that are often invisible or unspeakable in daily life to be expressed.


This post-show dialogue for *Juliet & Jul-Yingtai* connected theatrical creation, survivors' real-life experiences, and the practical work of nonprofit organizations, extending the anti-domestic violence conversation from the stage into real life.


AWDPI thanks Vermilion Theater for the invitation, moderator DOU Rui and actress LI Jialu for their honest sharing, and every audience member who chose to listen and engage in the discussion.


We believe change begins with seeing, and support begins with listening.


Every woman deserves the right to safety, dignity, and self-determination. No survivor should face violence alone.


AWDPI will continue to focus on domestic violence issues faced by Asian women, promoting awareness, resource connection, and community advocacy, so that more people in need know: They are not alone.
