01

Concept Origin — Social Psychology Field


Battered Women Syndrome (also translated as Battered Woman Syndrome) describes a psychological condition in women resulting from prolonged exposure to physical, emotional, and sexual violence and abuse by an intimate partner. Initially a concept within the field of social psychology, it is classified as a subtype of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).


Battered Women Syndrome was first proposed by Dr. Lenore Walker, an American clinical forensic psychologist and feminist pioneer. After long-term follow-up with over 400 domestic violence survivors and relevant case handlers, Walker collected and analyzed the cognitive and behavioral patterns of the subjects. She concluded that when a woman endures prolonged and continuous domestic violence and abuse, her psychological and behavioral responses undergo specific changes (such as partial loss of discernment and self-control, lack of clear understanding of the nature and consequences of her own actions). When the violence and abuse reach a critical threshold, the victim may ultimately turn into an offender, using violence to resist violence.


Typically, Battered Women Syndrome exhibits two common characteristics:


(1) The Cycle of Violence


Domestic violence and abuse follow a cyclical pattern. Within this recurring cycle, violent behaviors unfold in three distinct stages:


1. Increasing tension and sense of crisis in the relationship

2. Occurrence of severe violent behavior

3. The "loving remorse" phase


Because of this cyclical nature, women enduring long-term domestic violence often can accurately predict or anticipate when the next episode of violence will begin—meaning victims have a relatively clear awareness of the impending violent acts.



(2) Learned Helplessness


This theory originated from an experiment conducted by psychologist Martin Seligman in the 1960s. In the experiment, Seligman placed several dogs in an electrified metal cage and randomly adjusted the electric current. After a period of futile struggle, the dogs, unable to escape the locked cage, eventually gave up resisting and simply pressed their faces to the ground to avoid greater pain. Even after the cage was opened, the dogs no longer attempted to escape.


Walker applied this concept to explain why women in abusive relationships often find it extremely difficult to escape such situations through their own efforts. Long-term exposure to violence and abuse undermines the agency of victimized women, forcing them to gradually become passive and submissive, no longer believing they can change their situation through effort, and losing confidence in their ability to escape violence.

02

Development and Evolution — Legal Field


As gender equality and women's protection have advanced, waves of action against domestic violence have provided abused women with various forms and degrees of help and support—from the establishment of community shelters to the introduction of protection orders and specialized laws. In this process, the concept of "battered woman syndrome" has been repeatedly used by scholars and lawyers in the field of domestic violence as a defense in criminal proceedings to argue for the justification or exemption of victimized women who use violence against, injure, or kill their abusers. In judicial and legislative practice, "battered woman syndrome" has gradually become associated with self-defense, evolving from an initial purely social-psychological concept into a legal concept. A major breakthrough came in 1994 with the passage of the U.S. Congress's Violence Against Women Act, which explicitly required judges to eliminate gender bias in case adjudication and to change their understanding of self-defense in cases involving battered woman syndrome. Today, most U.S. state courts explicitly accept "battered woman syndrome" as expert evidence.



03

Identification Criteria


Research shows that the following six criteria can be used to determine the presence of "Battered Women Syndrome," with the first three symptoms being identical to those of post-traumatic stress disorder:


1. Intrusive recollection of the traumatic event

That is, regardless of whether the same stimuli are present, victims repeatedly re-experience the traumatic event through nightmares, daydreams, hallucinations, or flashbacks, as if the event is still "happening."

2. Hyperarousal and heightened anxiety

Excessive vigilance against further harm, resulting in difficulty falling or staying asleep, persistent irritability and anxiety, and inability to concentrate.

3. Avoidance behaviors and emotional numbness

Taking all possible measures to prevent the situation from worsening, actively avoiding topics, places, or people associated with the violent trauma, significantly reduced interest in and participation in social activities, extremely limited outlook and expectations for the future, and a clear restriction of activities imposed by the abuser. Common avoidance behaviors include depression, reduced activity, and social isolation.



4. Abuser's control and isolation of the woman, destruction of her interpersonal relationships

Abusers manipulate battered women through various means and channels, including verbal abuse, threats of violence (including threats of suicide or harm to the woman's family and friends), controlling spending, restricting meetings with family and friends, forcing "accompaniment" to and from work, limiting or prohibiting the woman from going out alone, and excessively frequent phone contact. The frequent occurrence of these behaviors ultimately makes it extremely difficult for the battered woman to contact family or friends outside the abuser, and she often submits to the abuser's control to reduce violence, protect family, friends, and pets.

5. Physical discomfort and negative attitudes toward one's body image

All forms of violence affect women's physical and mental health, further impacting their self-image and damaging their self-esteem. Specific manifestations include dissatisfaction with appearance or weight, frequent control of food intake, and even the development of anorexia or bulimia.

6. Sex-related issues

Abusers often use sexual coercion to humiliate and pressure women, thereby completing or reinforcing psychological control.



04

Survivors Treatment and Empowerment Program


The Survivors Treatment and Empowerment Program (hereinafter referred to as STEPs) is a carefully designed, scientifically validated psychological treatment program dedicated to helping female domestic violence survivors better understand the impact of violence on their lives, psychological states, and behaviors, and to provide guidance on coping with abusive relationships. STEPs is essentially a form of psychoeducation that achieves therapeutic effects by reducing psychological confusion and re-regulating emotions. Even non-specialists in mental health can organize and implement the program after proper training.


STEPs is typically facilitated by a trained organization or leader, involving group meetings or discussions with around 12 domestic violence survivors. Each session consists of the following three parts:


1. Knowledge Education: Providing information about domestic violence and its impacts;

2. Discussion and Exchange: Participants discuss their personal experiences and share solutions;

3. Skill Building:: The trained organization or leader teaches specific skills to protect and support their recovery.



STEPs consists of 12 independent modules, all grounded in contemporary feminist thought and trauma theory, with no strict sequence required during implementation. The earlier modules emphasize the negative impacts of discrimination and oppression accompanying domestic violence on women's lives, highlighting the need for women to reclaim the power eroded and stripped away during prolonged abuse and violence. The later modules draw on research related to Battered Women Syndrome to summarize lessons and overcome the psychological effects of intimate partner violence.


→ Below is a brief introduction to some of the knowledge education and skill-building components covered in selected modules:


● Defining Domestic Violence


1. Learn different terms used when discussing domestic violence and learn to define the types of domestic violence women experience (including physical abuse, sexual abuse, psychological abuse, stalking and harassment, violence against third parties such as children, and violence against pets or property);

2. Identify the categories of domestic violence experienced, clarify the consequences caused by such violence, and stop self-denial or minimizing the violence endured.


● Overcoming Negative Thinking and Developing a Safety Plan


1. Learn how to take as many safety measures or plans as possible and identify thought patterns that hinder proactive action;

2. Reject blame imposed by the abuser and stop believing or accepting anything told or imposed by the abuser;

3. Safety measures include but are not limited to:

① Design a practical and executable safety plan: Since most violent incidents begin at home, start from there to design detailed escape steps, identify safe locations to go to (such as friends' or family members' homes), and prepare necessary items for yourself (and children) in advance;

② Rehearse escape: Many victimized women are unaware of their legal rights and believe the abuser's threats about taking the children or seizing all property. They can carry personal records, employment information, and related documents to consult lawyers or community workers in advance;

③ Understand relevant laws and regulations.

● Thinking, Feeling, and Acting


1. Three abnormal patterns of thinking:

① Negative views of oneself and others: unconsciously believing oneself to be worthless and constantly seeking evidence to support this belief;

② Continuously expanding or worsening negative emotions: Superficially, it may seem logical and close to reality, but in essence, it continuously intensifies or amplifies negative emotions, supporting and validating negative evaluations or views of oneself or others. In extreme cases, it may become a compulsive thought pattern that is difficult to stop.

③ Irrational thinking: making unrealistic evaluations of oneself or others, including but not limited to exaggerating facts, denying or minimizing the abuser's behavior, overemphasizing the negative aspects, or labeling oneself or others. Sometimes, irrational thinking can be identified by frequent use of absolute words such as "always," "never," "forever," "none," or "everything."


2. Breaking free from abnormal thinking patterns: one can try the following methods to cope with negative thoughts and transform them into positive, hopeful beliefs:

① Thinking in specific and detailed terms;

② Checking whether there is evidence to support the thought;

③ Questioning one's negative beliefs;

④ Listening to others' opinions;

⑤ Learning to feel joy for oneself;

⑥ Understanding and reflecting on the origins of one's thinking patterns.



3. Skill-building:

Keeping a "thought diary" to record thoughts that naturally arise in daily life, analyze the underlying thinking patterns, and examine how these patterns affect one's feelings and behaviors.

After writing down one's thoughts, assess whether they are negative thoughts directed toward oneself or others, and whether they are absurd or irrational.


● Positive thinking, managing anger


1. Public education—understanding anger

Anger often stems from irrational or rationally negative thought patterns. Uncontrolled anger usually results from small triggering events that are not immediately addressed, gradually accumulating until they erupt. People living in violent relationships often suppress their anger to avoid further abuse from the perpetrator, and some long-term abused women have become accustomed to or skilled at suppressing and controlling their feelings of anger. However, suppressed anger does not simply disappear; instead, it leads to eventual indirect buildup and explosion. Many women who have long suffered violence become "obsessed" with repeatedly thinking about and recalling all the injustices they have experienced, fixating on how to obtain justice from the abuser. Being constantly trapped in such thoughts can hinder women's ability to continue living normal lives. Replacing negative thoughts with positive ones is an important step in managing these feelings, and freeing oneself from the anger caused by violent experiences is a necessary step for women who have experienced domestic violence to transform from "victims" to "survivors."


2. Skill-building: stopping repetitive thoughts

Freeing oneself from cyclical, negative, and pessimistic thoughts through attention diversion and replacing them with positive thoughts.


● Pursuing what you want in a healthy way


1. Confidence training: Clarifying what you want, how to effectively try to obtain it, and how to accept your powerlessness when all efforts have been exhausted without success.

2. Transforming anger into confidence: Expressing why you feel angry and honestly explaining how their behavior has affected you, rather than simply insulting, blaming, or venting emotions.

3. Confidence checklist: Listing specific situations in which you are able to express confidence or show emotional expression.



● Building healthy relationships


Setting boundaries: Defining the scope and degree to which one person can expect help from another, and how much one can receive from the other, while preventing exploitation of or by others when continuing to offer help.


An equal relationship does not mean mechanically pursuing reciprocity, but rather means that each party contributes uniquely to maintaining the relationship and must be respected for their contribution. Most people need personal space to think and act freely. In an equal relationship, each person can negotiate according to their own wishes and desires to confirm and decide the roles they play and tasks they undertake within the relationship. If a task must be completed by one party, the decision should be made through communication and negotiation, rather than being assigned solely based on gender differences.
