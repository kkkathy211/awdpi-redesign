I. Introduction



1.1 Research Background

Since the reform and opening-up policy in 1978, Chinese society has undergone a rapid modernization process. Profound changes in economic systems, social structures, and ideological frameworks have granted individuals greater autonomy in making independent choices regarding marriage, family relationships, and gender roles. (Graham & Graham, 2001; Yi, 2004) In particular, women who grew up after 1978 have benefited from the de-collectivization of interpersonal relationships and family life, demonstrating stronger agency and individual identity in shaping their life paths—standing in sharp contrast to traditional female roles in collectivist society. (Gaetano, 2018).

However, transformations in social structure have not completely eradicated gender inequality deeply rooted in culture and institutions. Divorced women in China still face strong traditional gender norms and social stigma, such as being labeled as "waste." Their marital failure is often attributed to personal traits rather than structural or systemic factors. (Hung, 2020) This tendency to individualize structural hardships not only intensifies women's psychological burdens but also reflects the persistent expectation that women must prove their worth through marital success.

The continuation of this value framework places divorced women in a more complex emotional and identity crisis when reconstructing the self: they must simultaneously confront external discrimination and labeling from the social environment, while also grappling with deep-seated internal shame and self-doubt. This tension between tradition and modernity has become an essential context for understanding contemporary Chinese women's marriage choices, identity positioning, and gender practices.


1.2 Research Significance

This study aims to explore how divorced women in China experience identity transformation and reconstruct their life pathways before and after marriage within the context of "compressed modernity." Specifically, it examines how, under the continued discipline of traditional patriarchal structures, these women reshape their gender consciousness and marital values under the influence of modern individualistic values, and make more coherent life choices.

Focusing on the identity transformation and gender practices of divorced Chinese women under the backdrop of "compressed modernity," this study seeks to deepen understanding of feminist theory and practice in non-Western societies. Current academic research on women's experiences during modernization has largely centered on Western contexts, leaving underexplored the mechanisms through which Chinese women—shaped by state-led development, collectivist traditions, and Confucian gender norms—form their marital values and self-identity.

By investigating divorced women's pre- and post-marital decision-making paths, gender awareness, and identity negotiation, this study reveals how they seek balance and breakthroughs between traditional patriarchal structures and modern individualistic values. This contributes to the localization of feminist theory within the Chinese sociocultural context and responds to how "compressed modernity" manifests tensions, conflicts, and potential spaces for transformation along gendered dimensions.

Furthermore, the research findings are expected to provide a theoretical foundation for understanding the deep structures of gender inequality in contemporary Chinese society, and offer empirical support and critical perspectives for the formulation of gender-related policies and social advocacy practices.


II. Theoretical Framework: Compressed Modernity and Chinese Women's Gender Practices



Unlike the "gradual, linear" path of modernization in the West, East Asian societies have experienced a state of "compressed modernity"—where processes of industrialization, democratization, individualization, and globalization are highly compressed in time and space, coexisting simultaneously. (Chang, 2022) For example, state-led modernization encourages women's participation in the labor market and emphasizes economic independence, yet decision-making power over economic resources within marriage remains predominantly male-dominated. In this social structure, traditional values (such as collectivism, family-centered ethics, and patriarchal authority) coexist with modern ideas (such as personal freedom, gender equality, and self-awareness,) creating intense mutual tensions. This "structural juxtaposition" places individuals—especially women—under deep institutional contradictions and decision-making dilemmas in daily life.

Scholars have provided in-depth interpretations of women's subjectivity under compressed modernity. Chang & Song (2010) proposed the concept of "individualization without individualism" (individualization without individualism) to describe how, under globalization and neoliberalism, East Asian women appear to gain more freedom in life choices, but these choices are often made within the boundaries of familial ethical structures. For instance, the process of women's "individualization" is frequently channeled back into the pursuit of a "better marriage." This form of individualization does not truly break free from the disciplinary framework of family-centered ideology, but instead represents a constrained, institutionally embedded individualization. Huang & Liu (2022) further developed the critical concept of "individualism without feminism." (individualism without feminism) They point out that under state-driven market reforms and consumerism, an increasing number of Chinese women have achieved economic independence, yet this "individualism" has not led them to develop systematic awareness of structural gender oppression. On the contrary, many women attribute success to individual effort rather than collective resistance or structural change. In this context, feminism is marginalized and even stigmatized as "overly radical" or "inappropriate." Thus, this is a form of individualism detached from gender politics, reflecting a fragmented state of female self-awareness and revealing the weak foundation of collective feminist consciousness in Chinese society. These theories challenge the Western discourse that equates economic autonomy with gender liberation, highlighting the incomplete nature of women's autonomy in East Asia.

This study applies the theory of "compressed modernity" specifically to the group of divorced Chinese women born after 1978, exploring how they struggle, suffer, and rebuild between traditional and modern values. As Xu (2022) pointed out, Chinese women undergoing rapid social transformation since the reform and opening-up have continuously evolved in gender consciousness and self-identity. Divorced women epitomize this process: they are both victims of patriarchal structures and active agents in self-repair and identity reconstruction.

By examining their life choices and shifts in perspective before and after divorce, this study reveals how they navigate conflicts among compressed temporal structures, residual Confucian norms, and emerging feminist awareness. Through the interplay of individual experiences and structural tensions in the East Asian context, this research resonates with and expands understandings of "non-Western feminist pathways."


III. Literature Review



3.1 Chinese Women and the Patriarchal Marriage Structure

When analyzing the identity reconstruction of divorced Chinese women, using compressed modernity and patriarchal structures as analytical frameworks helps us understand the deep social roots of their experiences. Chang (2022)'s concept of "compressed modernity" highlights that East Asia's modernization process differs from that of the West, as economic, political, cultural, and social changes occur rapidly within a compressed timeframe, resulting in the coexistence of modern individualistic values and traditional collectivist values. This coexistence is not a simple binary opposition, but a complex process of interweaving and negotiation, forming the unique tensions of contemporary Chinese society. Walby (1989), in her groundbreaking work, pointed out that the family is the most hidden yet most resilient fortress of patriarchy, continuously reproducing gender inequality through daily interactions and emotional relationships. This theoretical framework provides a crucial lens for understanding gender dynamics within Chinese marriages.

The family, as a field of power, contains a complex system of control strategies—from emotional manipulation in intimate relationships, to unequal distribution of decision-making power, to the systemic devaluation of women's labor. ( (Mow, Jie, 2004). Hays (1996)'s concept of "intensive motherhood" also offers insight into Chinese women's marital experiences. She observed that modern society increasingly reinforces expectations of motherhood, demanding that women invest substantial time, energy, and emotional resources into child-rearing—a phenomenon particularly pronounced under China's former one-child policy. The unequal distribution of emotional labor is a prominent feature of Chinese marriages. Erickson (2005), through research on emotional work in intimate relationships, found that women generally bear the primary responsibility for maintaining relationships, regulating emotions, and providing psychological support—labor that is often seen as "natural" and "taken for granted," thus remaining unrecognized and undervalued. Jackson, Liu, and Woo (2008), in their study of urban Chinese families, identified similar patterns, noting that Chinese wives are expected not only to manage their own emotions but also to attend to their husbands' and the entire family's emotional needs. This role as "emotional manager" intensifies their psychological burden. A particularly striking finding is that even in dual-earner households, women still shoulder the majority of emotional labor, reflecting the contradiction between advances in workplace gender equality and lagging gender practices within the household. Jamieson (1999)'s theory of "intimacy disclosure" offers another valuable perspective. She noted that modern intimate relationships increasingly emphasize emotional communication and self-disclosure, yet this trend sharply contrasts with traditional Chinese men's tendency to avoid emotional expression. Evans (2010), in studying urban Chinese marriages, observed that many women complain about their husbands' lack of emotional engagement and willingness to communicate, leading to declining relationship quality.

Moreover, power inequality within marriage is another core issue. Komter's (1989) concept of "invisible power" reveals hidden inequalities in household decision-making, pointing out that even in seemingly equal relationships, men often exercise power by setting agendas and defining the scope of what counts as "reasonable" discussion. Jiping Zuo (2005), in studying urban Chinese couples' power dynamics, found that despite increasing female economic independence, significant gender disparities in household decision-making power persist, especially regarding major financial decisions and children's education. This phenomenon profoundly reflects the implicit continuation of patrilineal logic in China's modernization process. This perspective helps us understand the deeper political implications of divorced women's housing difficulties—not merely as a shortage of economic resources, but as an unequal distribution of spatial power under patriarchal structures. Riley (1994), in her cross-cultural research, argues that marital power structures in China are shaped by broader social structures, including gender segregation in the labor market and gender bias in social policies. This view enables us to situate intra-marital power inequality within a wider social context.

The social stigma faced by divorced women is another important issue. Goffman's (1963) classic theory of stigma provides a framework for understanding this phenomenon, showing how stigmatized identities affect individuals' social interactions and self-perception. Coltrane and Adams (2003) further analyze gender differences in divorce stigma, finding that women typically face more severe moral judgment and social exclusion. Lamb (1999) shows that divorced women are often labeled as "failures," a stigma that not only comes from external social evaluations but also becomes internalized as part of self-identity. In the Chinese context, this stigmatizing effect is particularly pronounced. Brandtstädter and Santos (2008) point out that in Chinese society, marriage is not merely a personal choice but also a familial responsibility and social expectation; thus, divorce is often seen as a violation of collective norms. Yang and Kleinman's (2008) theory on the relationship between "face" (mianzi) and stigma is especially applicable to understanding the Chinese context. They argue that in face-conscious cultures, stigma affects not only the individual but also the entire family network, explaining why divorced Chinese women often face pressure and misunderstanding from their families of origin. Regarding identity reconstruction, Vaughan's (1986) "identity transition" theory offers a valuable framework. She describes divorce as a complex process of transition from a "spousal identity" to an "individual identity," involving the reorganization of social relationships, lifestyle adjustments, and the reshaping of self-concept. Smart and Neale (1999) further note that this identity transition is full of contradictions and tensions, especially for mothers who must navigate multiple, often conflicting, identity expectations. Hopper (2001) emphasizes that successful identity reconstruction depends not only on individual adaptability but also on the availability of social support networks and cultural resources. This perspective is particularly important for understanding the experiences of divorced Chinese women, who often rebuild their sense of self within limited institutional support and persistent cultural prejudice.



3.2 Self-Reconstruction and Gender Consciousness Transformation Among Divorced Women

The process of recovering from the trauma of divorce is also a process of disengaging from patriarchal harm and reconstructing the self. Chan, Hung, and Kung (2004) 's research indicates that divorce is a crucial opportunity for self-reconstruction. The study finds that women typically go through a phased transformation during post-divorce recovery: "loss of self—self-denial—rediscovery of self—rebuilding a new self." (Chan et al., 2004) This transformation is not linear, but unfolds gradually amid intense social stigma, self-blame, and imbalances in family roles. Initially, many women attribute divorce to personal failure due to their perceived inability to fulfill traditional roles, sinking into deep self-doubt. (Chan et al., 2004) However, divorce does not entirely undermine women's agency. On the contrary, this traumatic experience prompts some divorced women to redefine their "selves" and assign new meanings to marital failure. Some begin to view the end of marriage as an active choice—to escape harm, end injustice, or protect their children. For example, some justify divorce by stating that "love has faded," believing it meaningless to maintain a loveless marriage; others make rational decisions to prevent their children from suffering in conflict; and still others emphasize that they have made every possible effort to preserve the family, thus framing divorce as a responsible conclusion. Through these narratives, divorce is no longer a symbol of shame and failure, but a process in which individuals fight for justice and dignity for themselves and their families amid oppression, thereby repairing their damaged self-worth. ( Chan et al., 2004) As Hung (2022) puts it, divorce can be positive—a way to prevent further harm and gain freedom. Divorce is seen as a "symbol of brave and responsible women." (Hung, 2012).

The identity reconstruction of divorced women is not only reflected in structural adjustments in real life but also finds new pathways of expression on digital platforms. Shen and Chen's (2023) field study of the social media platform "Xiaohongshu" (Little Red Book) shows that an increasing number of divorced women are using "positive self-presentation" to shed society's negative labels on divorce, reshaping their personal images as free and confident individuals, and transforming "divorce" from a symbol of failure into one of growth and rebirth. Wang (2021) further points out that some female streamers on Douyin (TikTok) have built implicit mutual-support networks through "humorous deconstruction" and sharing personal experiences. These online communities not only expand women's public discursive space but also, to some extent, alleviate depression and feelings of isolation among divorced women. Indeed, these digital practices constitute a significant part of divorced women's "self-reconstruction": they not only resist stigmatization through digital expression but also redefine themselves and heal trauma through the interplay of virtual and real worlds, completing the regeneration of their identities.

At the same time, the reinforcement of single motherhood, participation in community support networks, and the mobilization of cultural resources help divorced women find new identity anchors between tradition and modernity. In this transformative process, the "victim" identity is gradually replaced by identities such as "moral agent" and "brave woman," revealing the potential for the growth of female subjectivity within oppressive structures. ( Chan et al., 2004) In the past, they were expected to be "good wives" and "good mothers"; once they step outside this framework and give new meaning to their lives, they can be liberated from patriarchal oppression. The self-reconstruction process revealed by the above studies provides important empirical insights for analyzing how Chinese women reshape their gender identities after divorce within the context of compressed modernity.


IV. Research Methodology



4.1 Methodological Position

This study follows a constructivist stance. Constructivism asserts that reality is pluralistic due to the constructive roles of different cognitive subjects (Lee, 2012) and emphasizes that phenomena and meanings are "socially constructed." (Veal & Burton, 2014) This suggests that people's ideas are shaped by themselves and society, rather than existing independently of objective reality. This position helps researchers understand how divorced women's views on gender, love, and sexuality are continuously constructed and reshaped within the context of China's compressed modernity and reform and opening-up.

This study aims to explore how Chinese divorced women's beliefs and behaviors change before and after divorce under the context of compressed modernity. To this end, the author adopts qualitative research methods, focusing not on "numbers" but on "language information conveyed in oral or written form." (Veal and Burton, 2014) Qualitative research allows researchers to immerse themselves in the specific details of a particular time and space, enabling an understanding of the "complex, dynamic, unpredictable" world through stories. (Patton, 2015) Therefore, this method helps researchers uncover novel stories and details, gaining deep insight into the heterogeneous narratives of Chinese divorced women's past marital and emotional experiences.

4.2 Research Method

This study employs semi-structured interviews, which include relatively fixed "structured conversations" as well as "open-ended, non-directive" questions. (Bryman, 2016) This qualitative method offers dual advantages: on one hand, structured conversations provide a clear research framework, situating micro-level factors—divorced women's individual views on gender, love, and sexuality—within the macro context of China's compressed modernity, thus offering a clear analytical structure. On the other hand, compared to fully structured interviews, semi-structured interviews are dynamic exchanges of information between interviewer and interviewee, with flexible question sequences that can be adjusted based on respondents' answers, (Ryan, 2018) thereby preserving space for exploration and facilitating the discovery of narratives that may deviate from traditional gender relations and social norms in divorced women's past marriages and complex life experiences.

This study designed three types of interview questions. The first type aims to understand divorced women's marital experiences and emotional lives after divorce: the former includes reasons for divorce and the impact of divorce on life, while the latter includes intimate relationships developed after divorce, providing background information for subsequent analysis. The second type focuses on the evolution and practice of love, gender, and sexuality before and after divorce, as well as the underlying motivations. The third set of questions centers on the tension between individualism and collectivism, aiming to explore how divorced women seek balance under such "compressed modernity" amid the interplay of feminism and traditional Chinese values. Sample questions include, "How do you balance 'individual freedom' and 'family responsibility'?" and "How do you redefine women's sense of self?" These three categories progress from surface to depth, starting from personal experiences to examine changes in personal beliefs and their motivations, and then placing individual behaviors and beliefs within the framework of individualism and collectivism for analysis.

Considering the current difficulty of conducting divorce-related surveys in China and the sensitivity of the topic involving participants' personal privacy, this study adopted a snowball sampling method, with the sample population consisting of divorced women. This group was born around 1978, at the beginning of China's reform and opening-up, and grew up amid intense social changes under China's compressed modernity, witnessing the nation's social development and the process of women's independence and liberation since 1978. Therefore, their behaviors and beliefs are shaped by both traditional patriarchal structures and modern individualism, making them typical cases for studying compressed modernity. In practice, the sample included 10 women born after 1978, divorced, and either raising children or living independently. Five, Research Findings



5.1

Before Divorce: Marital Dilemmas under Patriarchy

Marriage, which should be an intimate relationship built on equality and respect, often becomes a site of gender oppression. Women not only face economic control and the deprivation of voice, but are also expected to bear full responsibility for emotional care and family stability. Emotional indifference, physical violence, moral discipline, and intergenerational oppression intertwine, making marriage one of the core mechanisms sustaining the patriarchal system.

5.1.1 The Dual Dilemma in Marriage: Emotional Deprivation and Violence

Under the patriarchal marital structure, women often face the dual dilemma of emotional deprivation and violent oppression. As a form of emotional connection, marriage should ideally provide psychological comfort and emotional support to both partners. However, in reality, many women's emotional needs go unmet. In this study, 70% of respondents reported a lack of communication with their husbands and unmet emotional needs, which was a major factor leading to marital breakdown—consistent with Evans (2010). Men's "emotional cold violence" in marriage typically manifests as avoiding communication, shifting emotional stress, and ignoring their partner's needs. Some respondents attempted to encourage their partners to express emotions through dialogue, but eventually realized "he is unwilling to communicate and does not know how to listen."



This cold violence is not only a form of emotional oppression but also a consequence of socially constructed gender roles. In patriarchal culture, men often lack emotional expressiveness and tend to avoid or remain indifferent when facing emotional conflicts, while women are expected to unconditionally tolerate and endure. ((Erickson, 2005). As respondent Xiao Li said: "Women need love and companionship in a relationship, but men's views on marriage may be more traditional, more patriarchal, thinking that since I earn the money, you should endure my emotional violence."

Moreover, when men's emotional expression is blocked, they tend to transfer their sense of vulnerability and frustration through violence, becoming perpetrators within marriage. ( Logoz et al., 2023) Respondent Xiao Hong mentioned that her ex-husband lost control during a family dinner due to teasing from friends and attempted to physically assault someone. This violent tendency manifested in daily life as choking her and controlling her movements.

Faced with the above-mentioned dual dilemmas, women's legitimate demands within marriage are often ignored or suppressed. This stems not only from a lack of communication between individuals but also from the long-term entrenchment of gendered social culture.

5.1.2 Patriarchal Ethics and Moral Discipline: The One-Sided Amplification of Women's Responsibility

Under patriarchal marital ethics, women are often required to unconditionally give and endure, while men, even when clearly at fault, are treated with social leniency.

Thirty percent of respondents faced personal condemnation from society after their marriages failed. Respondent Xiao Dan, who divorced due to her husband's gambling debts, was criticized in her rural community for "failing to manage her husband." This moral blame places the entire responsibility for marital failure on women, reflecting gendered differences in the stigma of divorce. As Coltrane and Adams (2003) pointed out, divorce in sociocultural contexts is often not seen as a neutral personal choice but carries clear gender bias: women are more easily labeled as "failed wives" or "irresponsible mothers" after marital breakdown, while men face far fewer moral judgments. Moreover, the stigma against divorced women not only affects their personal social evaluation but often extends to their family networks, creating multiple layers of oppression. As respondent Xiao Nan described, despite her ex-husband's infidelity causing her to contract HPV, her parents still insisted that "men's infidelity is normal" and urged her to stay in the marriage. This phenomenon confirms Yang and Kleinman's (2008) observation on the relationship between "face" and stigma: in the Chinese context, marriage is not merely about maintaining an individual relationship, but is also seen as a symbol of preserving family "face" and social recognition.

The emphasis on women's responsibilities in marriage does not stem solely from individual male oppression but reflects a more systemic mechanism of gender discipline. One typical form of oppression is the tense mother-in-law and daughter-in-law relationship. Some mothers-in-law exhibit strong control over their daughters-in-law's private lives, such as demanding they hand over personal income or interfering with their hobbies. Notably, mothers-in-law themselves are women who have often experienced similar oppression, yet they may unconsciously perpetuate control over younger women. This phenomenon partly arises because the suffering endured by the previous generation of women in marriage has not been adequately acknowledged or reflected upon, leading them to rationalize past violent experiences and replicate them onto the next generation (Wang, 2022). As respondent Xiao Zhen said: ("My mother-in-law) having endured the rain herself, still wants others' umbrellas to get wet," vividly revealing the intergenerational transmission mechanism of violent experience.

The ethical logic that unilaterally assigns responsibility to women while downplaying men's wrongdoing reflects patriarchal cultural discipline of gender roles. It not only deprives women of subjectivity and legitimate rights within marriage but also makes it difficult for them to gain understanding and support from society and family after marital breakdown, leaving them trapped in ongoing moral judgment and self-doubt. (Lamb, 1999).

5.1.3 Economic Exploitation in Marriage: The Conflict between Modernity and Traditional Beliefs

Economic control is one of the key means by which men maintain dominance within the family. Even when women achieve economic independence, major financial decision-making power often remains outside their hands. (Zuo, 2005) This "invisible power" held by men (Komter, 1989) was evident in the interviews. Respondent Xiao Zhen recalled that her ex-husband was addicted to video games and had no formal job, yet her in-laws demanded she hand over her entire salary for household expenses and strictly controlled her financial behavior. This structural economic exploitation turns marriage into a tool of female oppression. Another respondent, Xiao Zhu, also mentioned facing opposition from her husband regarding career planning; her ex-husband wanted her to abandon her career and become a full-time housewife, citing "contributing to the family," but in essence, this was a denial of her economic independence and self-development.

Although women's participation in socioeconomic spheres has increased in recent years, the distribution of economic power within marriage has not changed accordingly. As Riley (1994) It points out that gender power structures within marriage are often shaped by societal structural factors, making it difficult for women to fully escape the patriarchal expectation of "submissiveness" even when economically capable. Under such structural oppression, women's economic independence is not widely recognized within families as a positive asset, but is instead frequently perceived as a challenge to the existing family order, thereby triggering more covert or even more intense gender conflicts.

5.2

After Divorce: The Awakening of Modern Individualism and Self-Reconstruction

Divorce is not only a reshaping of the traditional marriage system, but also a critical juncture for Chinese women to reconstruct self-identity and awaken subjectivity under the context of compressed modernity. After experiencing patriarchal discipline and gender oppression within marriage, divorced women are more likely to exhibit distinct characteristics of modern individualism. They no longer accept traditional gender role expectations of being a "good wife" or "good mother," but actively pursue self-worth and freedom of choice across economic, emotional, and intimate relationship dimensions, confirming what Hung ( 2012, 2022) stated: "divorce is a way to gain freedom."

5.2.1 Identity Awakening: No Longer Defining Oneself as "Someone's Wife" or "Mother," but Returning to "Who Am I?"

Divorce becomes an opportunity for many women to re-examine their self-positioning. Within marriage, women are more often socially expected to be labeled as "someone's wife" or "mother," but after divorce, many interviewees reported having more space and time to reflect on the question of "who am I," beginning to reconstruct their self-identity and the meaning of life. At the same time, half of the ten interviewees mentioned they no longer wished to bear multiple responsibilities within marriage, including emotional support and daily care for spouse, children, and in-laws. Xiao Tian, in particular, emphasized that after her divorce, she chose not to take her child and not to continue family caregiving responsibilities, insisting on choosing freedom. Her mother also supported her, saying "it's okay to be a little selfish," and she believed this choice was about taking responsibility for herself. Xiao Dan expressed a similar stance: "In the family, you have to consider everyone's emotions. I don't want to do that anymore. I just want to be alone. I feel really happy and free living by myself."

These narratives indicate that after divorce, women begin to break free from institutional family expectations that bind their life choices, gradually constructing a value system centered on the "self." This process of identity awakening, as Vaughan (1986) pointed out, is a transition "from spouse to individual." Especially within the context of compressed modernity, women's critical reflection on traditional gender role divisions and their gradual construction of a new discourse system centered on women represent the concrete emergence of modern individualized consciousness.



5.2.2 Enhanced Autonomy in Economic and Emotional Life: Pursuing Self-Worth and Freedom in Lifestyle Choices

After ending a marital relationship, women often face a "dual reconstruction" in economic and emotional life. In this study, interviewees generally reported a significant increase in economic control after divorce. A lifestyle previously centered on the family gradually gives way to arrangements centered on the "self," with women paying more attention to their own needs and quality of life. For example, Xiao Zhen mentioned experiencing economic control from her ex-husband's family during marriage: "They had a strong desire to possess my money, saying my income should go to their family." In contrast, after divorce, she gained full control over her income and expenditures. This economic autonomy brought her greater psychological security and a stronger sense of agency in life. Xiao Ru also noted: "I've become more economically independent, with money in my own hands, allowing me to plan my life more rationally." This transformation is not merely about changes in income usage, but also involves a reconstruction of life attitudes, rhythms, and modes of self-identification.

Economic autonomy not only enhances women's control over life but also lays the foundation for emotional autonomy. No longer dependent on a spouse for security, women begin to independently consider their lifestyle and future plans. Several interviewees said they could now freely arrange their daily rhythms, with increased frequency of socializing with friends and traveling—personal space and time previously constrained by marriage now released. This change reflects a significant shift among women from "family-centeredness" to "individual value orientation." This reconstruction of lifestyle is precisely the concrete manifestation of "individualization without individualism" (Chang & Song, 2010) in compressed modernity. Women formally gain freedom of choice, yet still value relational bonds such as kinship and friendship. Their individualization is not an isolated self detached from social relationships, but rather a redefinition of freedom and choice within relationships. Precisely for this reason, divorced women's journey—from gaining economic autonomy to reshaping interpersonal relationships centered on themselves—carries profound social significance in constructing authentic individual identity.

5.2.3 Re-choosing Intimate Relationships and Transcending Gender Order: Redefining Happiness and Freedom

Divorce also prompts women to rethink the meaning of intimate relationships and the modern marriage system. In this study, interviewees' attitudes toward marriage are no longer uniformly oriented toward "remarriage," but instead show a trend toward diverse choices. Some choose to remain single, enjoying the freedom and autonomy that single life brings; others maintain romantic relationships but explicitly reject remarriage, unwilling to re-enter institutional frameworks that may be filled with gender inequality. For example, Xiao Nan met her partner through a social platform and established a "low interference, high companionship" relationship, emphasizing that this form is freer and safer than marriage. Xiao Tian stated: "Dating is great; I don't want to get married again," valuing intimacy in romantic relationships more than the institutional constraints of marriage. Xiao Li also pointed out: "I'd rather be alone than be trapped again." She emphasized that being single does not mean failure, but rather an active choice that brings freedom and a sense of control.

Once marriage is no longer seen as a necessity in life, women as subjects begin to prioritize intimate relationships based on equality and communication, while rejecting the structural oppression of women within the modern marriage system, thus constructing a more autonomous and open vision of family relationships. The "stigma removal" process proposed by Goffman (1963) and Lamb (1999) is gradually emerging among this generation of women. Women no longer feel shame for leaving marriage, but instead confidently and self-sufficiently redefine happiness and success. This reconstruction of marriage and intimate relationships constitutes a powerful critique of traditional gender order.

By seeking self-worth after divorce, pursuing economic independence, redefining intimate relationships, and refusing to treat marriage as the sole destination in life, divorced women not only achieve liberation at the individual level, but also provide deeply insightful cases for the construction of female subjectivity under compressed modernity.


| VI. Conclusion and Discussion |

This study, based on the theory of "compressed modernity," focuses on Chinese divorced women born after 1978, exploring how they negotiate identity and reconstruct the self between traditional patriarchal structures and modern individualism. Through literature review and in-depth interviews with ten women, the study reveals that women often face difficulties such as emotional neglect, violence, economic control, and intergenerational oppression before marriage, with the marriage system serving as one of the core mechanisms of patriarchal power. Divorce, in contrast, becomes a crucial turning point for women's identity awakening and enhanced autonomy—they gradually break free from traditional roles such as "someone's wife" or "good mother," shifting toward a subjectivity centered on the "self," demonstrating stronger individual awareness and emotional autonomy. At the same time, divorced women challenge the marriage system and gender order by re-choosing intimate relationships and reshaping their concepts of happiness. This study not only enriches the application of "compressed modernity" theory in gender research, but also provides vivid empirical cases for exploring female subjectivity within the Chinese context.

6.1 Theoretical Level: The Tension Between Tradition and Modernity and the Reconstruction of Subjectivity

"Compressed modernity," as an important theoretical framework for understanding the situation of Chinese women, reveals the coexistence in time and space of traditional patriarchal systems and modern individualization processes. This study finds that this tension is particularly evident in the experiences of divorced women. On one hand, the family, as the most stable fortress of patriarchy, continues to exert disciplinary power in areas such as emotional labor, decision-making authority, and gender role identification. Interviewees commonly experienced a triple oppression of "invisible violence," "moral responsibility," and "economic control," with their marital failure often perceived as a personal flaw rather than a structural issue.

On the other hand, divorce becomes a practical opportunity for women to break through this oppressive structure. They engage in a series of "anti-traditional practices" after divorce: actively rejecting remarriage, emphasizing individual value, and reconstructing intimate relationship structures. These practices demonstrate women's agentic subjectivity in struggling and healing within compressed modernity, responding to Chang & Song's (2010) concept of "individualization without individualism" and Huang & Liu's (2022) notion of "individualism without feminism." This study further argues that although patriarchal structures have not been completely dismantled, women are actively reorganizing their life paths and gender identities, offering empirical support for exploring "feminism in non-Western contexts."


6.2 Practical Level: Shifting Support Systems and Social Discourse

Research findings indicate that divorced women still face numerous obstacles in the process of reconstructing their subjectivity, urgently requiring greater societal support at both institutional and cultural levels. First, in terms of psychological and social support, dedicated counseling systems, emotional recovery mechanisms, and re-employment training programs should be established for divorced women to assist them in navigating identity transitions and economic rebuilding. Particularly for women from rural backgrounds or those unemployed, such support can alleviate the multiple inequalities they face.

Second, public education systems and gender awareness campaigns must confront the reality of gender inequality within marriage institutions. By implementing family education courses, workplace gender training, and intimate relationship communication programs, public understanding of "modern marriage structures" can be enhanced, weakening the moral judgment that equates divorce with failure.

Finally, media and digital platforms play a crucial role in shaping public discourse. They should de-stigmatize the identity of divorced women by reporting on their positive transformations and promoting gender-equal narratives around marriage. For instance, female content creators on platforms such as "Xiaohongshu" (Little Red Book) and "Douyin" (TikTok) are reshaping the public image of divorced women through humor and narrative strategies, building mutual support networks and shared resource spaces. These practices reflect women's capacity to reconstruct discourse in digital spaces and offer new breakthroughs against traditional gender narratives.


6.3 Research Limitations and Future Directions

This study has achieved certain breakthroughs at both theoretical and practical levels, yet limitations remain. First, the sample size is limited, with respondents primarily concentrated in eastern coastal regions and large to medium-sized cities, failing to cover a broader range of geographic and social groups, thus restricting the study's generalizability. Future research could conduct more systematic comparative studies across urban-rural divides, regional differences, and occupational backgrounds. Second, this study focuses on women born after 1978, without fully examining intergenerational differences in gender attitudes, marriage practices, and social adaptation across age groups. Given that "compressed modernity" affects different generations with layered progression and varying psychological responses, future studies could further compare the evolving strategies of marriage choices among women from the "post-80s," "post-90s," and "post-00s" cohorts, to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the historical trajectory of social transformation and female subjectivity construction. Moreover, the impact of digital technology on the reformation of female subjectivity is becoming increasingly significant. Future research could deepen cross-analytical explorations of algorithmic culture, media affect, and digital intimate practices, thereby expanding the theoretical explanatory power of the "compressed modernity" framework within media and gender studies.
