 NO.2 

[United States Chapter]Are you or someone you care about experiencing domestic violence?


1. Legal definition

  • Domestic violence is a pattern of abusive behavior in any relationship where one person seeks to gain or maintain power and control over an intimate partner. Domestic violence can include physical, sexual, emotional, economic, psychological, or technological acts or threats, or other coercive patterns of behavior that affect others in intimate relationships. This includes any act of intimidation, manipulation, humiliation, isolation, threats, coercion, blaming, harming, injuring, or hurting someone.

  • Domestic violence can happen to anyone, regardless of race, age, sexual orientation, religion, gender, or gender identity. Domestic violence affects people across all socioeconomic backgrounds and education levels. It occurs in heterosexual and same-sex relationships and may happen between married, cohabiting, dating, or parenting intimate partners.

  • Domestic violence not only affects those who are abused but also has significant impacts on family members, friends, colleagues, other witnesses, and the entire community. Children who witness domestic violence are among those severely affected by this crime. Frequently experiencing violence at home not only makes children vulnerable to numerous social and physical problems but also teaches them that violence is a normal way of life—thereby increasing their risk of becoming the next generation of victims and abusers.

  • According to legal definitions, domestic violence (Domestic violence) refers to abuse committed against any of the following individuals:

    1. Spouse or former spouse

    2. Current or former cohabitant

    3. Person who has or had a dating or engagement relationship with the defendant

    4. Person with whom the defendant has a child

    5. Child of one of the parties or a child in a proceeding under the Uniform Parentage Act

    6. Person related by blood or marriage


2. Current status of domestic violence in the United States

  • Data from UN Women shows that up to 70% of women in the United States have experienced physical or sexual violence from an intimate partner.

  • The National Coalition Against Domestic Violence in the United States reports that 1 in 3 women has experienced violence from an intimate partner. Among women who are murdered, 1 in 3 is killed by an intimate partner.

  • According to a report published on October 17, 2019, by the organization Everytown for Gun Safety, against the backdrop of widespread gun availability, the United States has become the most dangerous country for women among high-income nations. In 2015, 92% of women gunned down in high-income countries were from the United States, and nearly half of women fatally shot were killed by current or former intimate partners. During the COVID-19 pandemic, many victims faced worsening economic hardship and prolonged isolation with abusers, making it even harder to access help and support. (Source: http://www.news.cn/globe/2022-04/13/c_1310541286.htm)


3. Common types

  • Physical abuse : Hitting, slapping, pushing, grabbing, pinching, biting, pulling hair, etc., are forms of physical abuse. This type of abuse also includes denying a partner medical care or forcing them to drink and/or use drugs.

  • Sexual abuse: Coercing or attempting to coerce any sexual contact or behavior without consent. Sexual abuse includes, but is not limited to, marital rape, assaulting sexual parts of the body, forcing sexual acts after physical violence, or treating sex in a degrading manner.

  • Emotional abuse: Undermining a person's sense of self-worth and/or self-esteem. This may include, but is not limited to, constant criticism, undermining a person's capabilities, verbal abuse, or damaging a person's relationship with their children.

  • Economic abuse : Controlling or restricting a person's ability to access, use, or maintain economic resources to which they are entitled. This includes using coercion, fraud, or manipulation to limit access to money, assets, credit, or financial information; unfairly using personal economic resources such as money, assets, and credit; or exerting undue influence over a person's financial and economic decisions, including forcing violation of joint or other financial obligations, misuse of power of attorney, guardianship, or conservatorship, or failing to act in the best interests of someone for whom one has a fiduciary duty.

  • Psychological abuse : Elements of psychological abuse include, but are not limited to—causing fear through intimidation; threatening physical harm to oneself, the partner, children, or the partner's family or friends; destroying pets and property; and forcing isolation from family, friends, or school and/or work.

  • Technological abuse: Using any form of technology, including but not limited to: internet-connected devices, online spaces and platforms, computers, mobile devices, cameras and imaging programs, applications, location-tracking devices, communication technologies, or any other emerging technologies, to harm, threaten, control, track, harass, impersonate, exploit, extort, or monitor others, either as individual acts or as a pattern of behavior.

1.2 Preserving Evidence

  • When you begin applying for a protection order, you should preserve the following evidence as appropriate:

    • Take photographs of any visible physical injuries.

    • Obtain copies of any police reports.

    • Determine whether anyone witnessed the abuse and ask them to serve as a witness.

    • If you need to seek financial assistance (also known as "emergency family aid"), prepare recent pay stubs, records of living expenses (mortgage, rental agreements, utility bills, car loans, childcare costs), and any income or bank information related to the abuser (e.g., pay stubs).

    • It is recommended that you contact the police promptly to discuss initiating criminal proceedings.


2. [Restraining Orders] (Protection From Abuse) ]

2.1 Introduction

  • A protective order refers to any court order that includes one or more of the following restraining orders, whether issued ex parte, after notice and hearing, or as part of a judgment: an order prohibiting specific abusive behavior; an order prohibiting a person from entering a residence; or an order prohibiting other specific conduct.


2.2 Eligibility Criteria

Under Maryland law, for example, you are eligible for a protective order if you meet any of the following conditions:

    1. The respondent (abuser) is your current or former spouse

  • The "respondent" refers to the alleged abuser, the person against whom you are seeking the protective order.

  • You do not need to live with the respondent.

  • Former spouses are eligible for relief regardless of how long they have been separated or divorced. You are eligible regardless of whether you or your former spouse have a new or different spouse.

    2. The respondent (abuser) is your cohabitant

  • The "respondent" refers to the alleged abuser, the person against whom you are seeking the protective order.

  • A "cohabitant" is someone who, within one year before filing the petition, had a sexual relationship with the respondent and lived with the respondent in the same household for at least 90 days.

  • The 90-day period does not need to be continuous (not necessarily 90 consecutive days). For example, the cohabitant and respondent may have lived together for 30 days each in September, December, and July.

  • The 90-day requirement within one year applies only to the cohabitation requirement. There is no requirement that the abuser and the person seeking relief maintain a sexual relationship for 90 days within the year.

  • The cohabitant and respondent may be of the same sex.

    3. A person related to the respondent (abuser) by blood, marriage, or adoption

  • The "respondent" refers to the alleged abuser, the person against whom you are seeking the protective order.

  • For example, if you are the respondent's son, daughter, niece, or nephew, you may be eligible for legal protection.

    4. The respondent's (abuser's) parent, stepparent, child, or stepchild, or a person who lived with the respondent or the person seeking relief for at least 90 days within one year before filing the petition

  • The "respondent" refers to the alleged abuser, the person against whom you are seeking the protective order.

  • This category is similar to the cohabitation category. A stepchild may be eligible for relief if they lived with the abuser for at least 90 days within one year.

    5. An adult who lacks full legal capacity

  • An adult who lacks full mental or physical ability to meet their daily needs.

    6. A person who shares a child with the respondent (abuser)

  • There is no requirement to have been married or cohabited.

  • You do not need to have lived continuously with the respondent at the time of the abuse.

    7. A person who had a sexual relationship with the respondent (abuser) within one year before filing the petition.

    8. A person who claims the respondent (abuser) committed rape, sexual offense, or attempted rape or sexual offense within six months before filing the petition.

2.3 Application Procedures

(1) Allegheny County PFA Application Procedure

  • Go to the Allegheny County Family and Juvenile Court, Room 3030, 440 Ross St., Pittsburgh, between 8:30 a.m. and 11:00 a.m. on weekdays.

  • Individuals seeking to file a PFA should arrive early.

  • A legal counselor from a women's center and shelter, along with a family law attorney from Neighborhood Legal Services, will be available at the Family Court Safety Assistance Center in the family court to provide assistance and information.

    (2) Beaver County PFA Application Procedure

  • Go to the Court Administrator's Office on the second floor of Beaver County Courthouse, 810 3rd St, Beaver, between 8:30 a.m. and 9:00 a.m. on weekdays.

  • Court staff will provide forms and clerical (but not legal) assistance to help you file a petition with the court on your own behalf or on behalf of a minor child.

  • In addition, a volunteer from the Beaver County Women's Center is usually present to guide applicants through the process and answer questions about domestic violence.

  • After obtaining a temporary protection order, you may call our office for assistance.

    (3) Butler County PFA Application Procedure

  • Go to the Butler County Courthouse, 124 W Diamond St, Butler, between 8:30 a.m. and 9:00 a.m. on weekdays. The PFA office is located in the Domestic Relations Division on the first floor.

  • Submit the application to a family court judge around 11:30 a.m.; you should plan to spend the entire morning to ensure service is completed.

  • If you have any questions about domestic violence or the PFA process, you can contact the volunteer on site.

  • After obtaining a temporary protection order, you may call our office for assistance.

Lawrence County PFA Application Procedure

  • Go to Lawrence County Courthouse, 430 Court Street, New Castle, between 8:00 a.m. and 11:00 a.m. and 1:00 p.m. to 3:00 p.m. The PFA Coordinator/Lawyer Referral, part of court administrative services, handles all petitions for protection from abuse.

2.4 Legal Effects

     (1) What protections does the court order through a PFA?

  • Order to stop abuse

  • Remove the abuser from the home / arrange a method for the abuser to retrieve personal belongings

  • Establish custody, partial custody, and visitation rights

  • Order the abuser to pay support

    • This may include health insurance, rent, mortgage payments, etc.

    • For child support, you should file a separate appeal

  • Order the abuser to stay away from your workplace, school, and residence

  • Order the abuser to stop harassing, stalking, or contacting you (including in person, by phone, mail, or social media)

  • Order the abuser to surrender weapons, ammunition, and any firearms

    • This includes any firearm licenses or carry permits

  • Order the abuser to compensate for economic losses or damages


    (2) How long does a PFA last?

  • An emergency protection order lasts only until the weekend of its issuance

  • A temporary protection order lasts 10 days, during which the judge will schedule a hearing

  • Extended temporary protection orders and final protection orders last from one day to three years


    (3) What happens if the defendant violates the PFA?

  • You must immediately report any violation to your local police or magistrate.

  • If sufficient evidence exists that the defendant has violated the PFA, they will immediately notify the court and schedule a hearing.

  • The hearing will be held before a judge, who will determine whether the defendant violated the PFA. If so, the defendant will face the following penalties:

    • Extension of the PFA; a maximum fine of $1,000; up to six months of imprisonment

3.[Shelter (using California as an example)]

3.1 Introduction

  • If you are experiencing domestic violence, you and your children are eligible for 24-hour safe or confidential emergency housing, including but not limited to arrangements at hotels or motels, safe havens, and safe houses.

  • Shelter programs based on domestic violence should provide all of the following basic services to victims of domestic violence and their children:

    • 24-hour shelter available seven days a week.

    • Call the crisis hotline 24 hours a day, seven days a week.

    • Temporary housing and food services.

    • Provision of psychological support and peer counseling.

    • Referral to existing community services.

    • A transfer center operating during regular business hours to assist victims of domestic violence who need support services.

    • Arranging for school-aged children to continue their education during participation in domestic violence shelter programs.

4. [Free Legal Aid]

4.1 Introduction

  • The U.S. legal aid system is primarily composed of two distinct and independently operated systems: criminal legal aid and civil legal aid. The criminal legal aid system is mainly funded and implemented by government-established Public Defender Offices. It is established as an essential component of human rights protection and as a necessary element of the criminal justice process.

  • In the United States, the National Legal Aid & Defender Association (NLADA) is a national organization responsible for setting legal aid standards, training public defenders, organizing specialized seminars on criminal legal aid, and providing informational and technical support to public defenders. The civil legal aid system, on the other hand, is funded by the government-established Legal Services Corporation (LSC) and implemented through nonprofit legal aid organizations and private attorneys.

  • Legal aid offices (also known as legal services) exist across the United States. These are nonprofit organizations that provide free legal assistance to individuals who cannot afford to hire a lawyer. While many legal aid offices only assist low-income individuals, some have more flexible income eligibility rules. Many legal aid offices also offer self-help resources to help you get started.


  • Legal aid typically handles cases involving the following:

    • Domestic violence – If your partner is abusing you, legal aid can help you obtain protective orders, child custody orders, and divorce.

    • Family law – If you have a child custody or divorce case, legal aid may be able to help. Call your local legal aid office or request the judge to appoint a legal aid attorney to represent you in court.

    • Housing – If you are facing eviction or foreclosure, legal aid may be able to help.

    • Public benefits – If you have issues with welfare, food stamps, Medicaid, Supplemental Security Income (SSI), or Social Security, legal aid may be able to help.


  • Many legal aid offices may also be able to handle other issues, including immigration, consumer, and disability matters. Some legal aid offices specialize in one area of law, such as disability law or housing law. Some receive government funding, which may limit the types of cases they can accept.

    • In addition, legal aid "clinics" run by U.S. law schools also provide legal assistance to the public, becoming one of the distinctive features of the U.S. legal aid system. Currently, most U.S. law schools operate legal aid clinic programs. Qualified law students, under the supervision of clinic faculty, may represent clients in court.

5. [Pursuing Criminal Liability Against the Abuser]

5.1 Introduction

  • If a defendant is convicted of assault or abuse, the criminal consequences typically include not only imprisonment and fines, but also one or more of the following:

    (1) Mandatory minimum jail time

  • Even if the defendant is charged with a misdemeanor and is a first-time offender, most counties in California require a minimum of 30 days in jail for a domestic violence conviction.

    (2) Payment of victim restitution and the California Domestic Violence Prevention Fund

  • In California, defendants convicted of domestic violence may be ordered by the court to pay restitution to the victim, which may include the victim's: medical bills; mental health counseling fees; lost wages; and/or property damage. The defendant must also pay $500 to the government to fund the California Domestic Violence Prevention Fund program.

    (3) Attendance in various "anger management" programs

  • Even if the defendant is sentenced for a misdemeanor and the judge allows probation, judges almost invariably require defendants convicted of domestic violence to actively participate in anger management programs.

    (4) Permanent criminal record

  • For non-citizen defendants, a significant consequence is that a domestic violence conviction will permanently remain on their criminal record. Anyone conducting a routine background check will be able to see the conviction, which will undoubtedly create difficulties for the defendant in employment, housing, loans, or accessing other social benefits.

    (5) Loss of custody rights

  • In California, defendants convicted of domestic violence typically lose custody rights over their children and are granted only visitation rights. A domestic violence conviction is not the sole basis for a family court judge's custody decision. However, if one parent has been convicted of domestic violence within the past five years, the judge must find that domestic violence occurred. (See Family Code Section 3044)

    (6) Loss of firearm rights

  • A defendant convicted of domestic violence generally loses the right to possess firearms. Once convicted, the defendant cannot restore the lost firearm rights.

    (7) Misdemeanor conviction for domestic violence

  • A misdemeanor conviction under California Penal Code Section 273.5 (corporal injury to a spouse or cohabitant) results in a lifetime ban on firearm possession (see California AB3129). However, under federal law, certain domestic violence offenses—even if classified as misdemeanors—result in a lifetime prohibition on firearm possession upon conviction.

    (8) Felony conviction for domestic violence

  • California Penal Code Section 29800, known as the "felon in possession of a firearm" law, imposes a lifetime firearm ban on anyone convicted of a felony in any state or country. If you are convicted of a domestic violence felony, both California state law and federal law will prohibit you from legally possessing firearms.


5.2 Criminal charges the abuser may face

  • Taking California, United States as an example, common "domestic violence" offenses include: assault, abuse, threats, and neglect, some of which are misdemeanors and others are felonies. However, most of these offenses fall under California's "wobbler" category—meaning they can be charged as either a misdemeanor or a felony—depending primarily on: the circumstances of the offense; the severity of the victim's injuries; and the defendant's criminal history.


Below are the most common domestic violence offenses under the California Penal Code:

  • Penal Code 273.5: Corporal Injury to a Spouse or Cohabitant

    Inflicting "corporal injury" on a partner, even minor injury, is illegal. This is a felony offense, and the punishment for a first offense may range from 1 to 4 years in prison.

  • Penal Code 243(e)(1): Domestic Battery

    Using force or violence against an intimate partner is a misdemeanor. Unlike Penal Code 273.5, this charge does not require visible physical injury to the victim.

    • Domestic battery is a misdemeanor. Penalties include fines up to $2,000 and/or up to one year in jail.

  • Penal Code 273(d): Child Abuse

    Physically punishing or injuring a child is a crime. In California, apart from minor, reasonable disciplinary spanking, any physical punishment—intentional or not—that is cruel or causes injury is considered child abuse.

    • For a first offense of child abuse, sentencing may include 1 to 3 years in prison.

  • Penal Code 273a: Endangering a Child

    It is a crime to willfully permit a child to be in a situation that results in: injury, or endangers the child's health or safety.

    • Endangering a child is a misdemeanor, punishable by up to 6 months in jail. However, if the child suffers serious bodily injury, the offense becomes a felony, punishable by more than 1 year in prison.

  • Penal Code 270: Child Neglect / Failure to Provide Care

    It is a crime for a parent to intentionally or negligently fail to provide necessary care (e.g., food, shelter, medical care) to their young child. Child neglect is a misdemeanor, punishable by a fine of up to $2,000 and/or up to 1 year in jail.

  • Penal Code 368: Elder Abuse

    Any of the following acts against a person aged 65 or older constitutes elder abuse (which may be charged as a misdemeanor or a felony): physical abuse; emotional abuse; neglect (of care, food, shelter, medical needs); endangerment; or financial fraud.

    • When charged as a misdemeanor, elder abuse carries a maximum penalty of 1 year in jail. In particularly serious or egregious cases, it may be charged as a felony, punishable by up to 4 years in prison.

  • Penal Code 422: Criminal Threats

    Under California law, a threat that causes someone to fear imminent serious harm may be charged as a misdemeanor or a felony. A misdemeanor carries a maximum of 1 year in jail, while a felony may result in up to 4 years in prison.

  • Penal Code 646.9: Stalking

    It is a crime to stalk or harass someone in a way that causes them to fear for their safety or the safety of their family. Depending on the defendant's criminal history, this offense may be charged as a misdemeanor or a felony. Penalties include 1 to 3 years in prison.

  • Penal Code 591: Cutting or Damaging Telephone Lines

    It is a crime to intentionally cut or damage telephone lines or equipment—for example, when an abuser prevents a victim from making a phone call. This offense may be charged as a misdemeanor or a felony. If convicted as a felony, penalties include a fine of up to $10,000 and/or up to 3 years in prison.

  • Penal Code 601: Trespassing (Private Property)

    It is a crime to criminally threaten someone and, within 30 days, enter the victim's home or workplace to harass or threaten them. Trespassing may be charged as a misdemeanor or a felony. A felony conviction may result in up to 3 years in prison.

  • Penal Code 647(j)(4): Malicious Distribution of Intimate Images

    The act constitutes "revenge porn" when someone intentionally distributes intimate or nude images of another person (e.g., an ex-girlfriend or ex-wife) with the intent to cause emotional distress. The offender may be fined $1,000 and sentenced to up to 1 year in jail.

  • Penal Code 653.2: Posting Harmful Information Online

    Posting false and harmful information online is a relatively new misdemeanor offense, also known as "indirect electronic harassment." It includes publishing or emailing harmful information about someone with the intent to provoke online attacks, harassment, or defamation by the public.

    • In domestic disputes, when one party seeks revenge against the other online, they are often charged under Penal Code 653.2. Penalties may include a fine of up to $1,000 or up to 1 year in jail.

(2) Prosecution Procedure

  • 2. If the police do not investigate or do not file charges, you may file a complaint directly with a local court commissioner or judge.     • Identify the defendant with certainty (specific information about the defendant)

    • If an arrest warrant is issued, the responsible law enforcement agency will locate and arrest the defendant.

  • 5. Your complaint may result in the arrest or detention of the defendant. Once your complaint is accepted, you cannot withdraw it, and the commissioner who received it has no authority to withdraw it. The prosecution can only be dismissed by the state prosecutor or through court proceedings.

6. [Divorce]

  • The United States is a federal system, where the federal government mainly provides general principles regarding marriage and family laws, while specific marriage laws are primarily enacted by individual states. The Uniform Law Commission (formerly the National Conference of Commissioners on Uniform State Laws) promulgated the Uniform Marriage and Divorce Act in 1970. This legal document establishes the principle of dissolving marriages and serves as an important reference for U.S. states in formulating their family laws.

6.1 Types of Divorce

  • Including uncontested (administrative) divorce, contested divorce, and legal separation.


6.2 Grounds/Conditions for Divorce

  • Uniform Marriage and Divorce Act §102: Treats the irretrievable breakdown of the marriage as the sole basis for divorce, making divorce laws effective in addressing real marital problems.

  • Uniform Marriage and Divorce Act §305[Irretrievable Breakdown]

    • Condition: When both spouses petition or swear under oath that the marriage has reached an irretrievable breakdown.

      • If one party petitions for divorce on the grounds of irretrievable breakdown and the other party does not contest it, the court will conduct an investigation into whether the marriage has indeed broken down.

      • If one party denies that the marriage is in a state of breakdown, the court shall consider all relevant factors and conduct an investigation. Further proceedings on the case shall be held within 30 to 60 days, or within the shortest time the case can be placed on the court's docket.


6.3 Divorce Procedure

  • Uniform Marriage and Divorce Act (U.S.) §302 [Divorce; Legal Separation]

    • The court shall enter a decree of divorce under the following circumstances:

      (1) The court determines that, at the time the proceeding is commenced and before the next proceeding begins, one party to the marriage has resided in the state or has been stationed in the state on active military duty for 90 days;

      (2) The court finds that the marriage is irretrievably broken, supported by evidence that the spouses have lived apart for 180 days before the commencement of the next proceeding, or that there are serious marital conflicts that negatively affect one or both spouses' attitudes toward the marriage;

      (3) The court has jurisdiction, to the extent necessary, over matters concerning child custody, child support, spousal support, and property division; or over a separate hearing provided to resolve such matters.

      (4) If one party requests legal separation instead of divorce, the court shall grant it unless the other party objects.


  • Uniform Marriage and Divorce Act (U.S.) §330 [Procedure; Pleadings; Defenses; Abolition of Existing Defenses]

    • In a petition for divorce or legal separation, the ground shall be that the marriage is irretrievably broken, and the petition shall include:

      (1) The age, occupation, residence, and duration of residence in the state of each party;

      (2) The date of marriage and the place where the marriage was solemnized;

      (3) A statement that the marriage is irretrievably broken;

      (4) The names, ages, and addresses of all living children of the marriage, and whether the wife is pregnant;

      (5) Any arrangements regarding child custody, visitation, child support, and spousal support;

      (6) The relief requested.

    • Either spouse, or both spouses jointly, may initiate the proceeding.

    • If one spouse files the petition, a summons shall be served on the other spouse, who may file an answer with supporting evidence within 30 days after service.


  • Uniform Marriage and Divorce Act (U.S.) §314 [Decree]

    • A decree of divorce or legal separation takes effect immediately upon issuance. However, an appeal may be filed by either party contesting the divorce decree.

    • Six months after a decree of legal separation is issued, the court shall, upon request by either party, convert it into a decree of divorce.

    • Upon the request of a woman whose marriage is dissolved by divorce or annulment, the court may issue an order restoring her former or maiden name; if there are no children of the marriage, the court shall issue such an order.


  • Uniform Marriage and Divorce Act (U.S.) §315 [Independence of Decree or Temporary Order]

    • If one party fails to comply with a decree, temporary order, or injunction, the other party's obligation to pay support or allow visitation shall not cease; however, the aggrieved party may seek an appropriate order from the court.


6.4 Child Custody

  • Uniform Marriage and Divorce Act (U.S.) §401 [Jurisdiction; Petition]

    • A court of this state has jurisdiction to decide child custody matters if:

      (1) This state was, at the time of filing, the child's home state, or had been the child's home state within the preceding six months, and the child is no longer in the state due to removal or retention by a person claiming custody or for other reasons, and a parent or person acting as a parent continues to reside in the state; or

      (2) (i) The child and at least one parent, or the child and another party to the custody proceeding, have a significant connection with this state, and (ii) substantial evidence is available in this state concerning the child's present or future care, protection, training, and personal relationships, so that exercising jurisdiction serves the child's best interests; or

      (3) The child is present in the state and: (i) has been abandoned, or (ii) is in an emergency situation due to abuse, neglect, or being threatened with abuse, neglect, or abandonment, requiring immediate protection; or

      (4) (i) No other state has jurisdiction under prerequisites (1), (2), or (3), or another state has declined to exercise jurisdiction because this state is a more appropriate forum for determining custody, and (ii) exercising jurisdiction by this court is in the child's best interests. The mere presence of the child in the state or the presence of one party in the state is not sufficient for the court to obtain jurisdiction over custody; the conditions in (3) or (4) above must also be met. A custody proceeding may be initiated in court by:

    • (1) One of the parents, in connection with

    • a petition for divorce or legal separation; or

      a petition for custody of the child in the county or judicial district where the child permanently resides or is found; or

      • (2) A non-parent, in the county or judicial district where the child permanently resides or is found, but only if the child is not under the custody of either parent.

      • Uniform Marriage and Divorce Act (U.S.) §402 [Best Interests of the Child]

      The court shall determine child custody in accordance with the best interests of the child and shall consider all relevant factors, including:


  • (1) The wishes of either or both parents regarding custody;

    • (2) The child's wishes regarding custody;

      (3) The interaction and relationship between the child and each parent, siblings, and any other person who may significantly affect the child's best interests;

      (4) The child's adjustment to home, school, and community; and

      (5) The mental and physical health of all individuals involved.

      The court shall not consider conduct of a proposed custodian that does not affect the custodian's relationship with the child.

      Uniform Marriage and Divorce Act (U.S.) §403 [Temporary Orders]

    • A party involved in a custody proceeding may apply for a temporary custody order.

  • If a divorce or legal separation proceeding is dismissed, any temporary custody order shall become void, unless one parent or the child's guardian applies to continue the proceeding as a custody case, and the court, after a hearing, finds that the parents' circumstances and the child's best interests require a custody decree.

    • If a custody proceeding is initiated without a petition for divorce or legal separation, any temporary custody order shall become void if the proceeding is dismissed.

    • Uniform Marriage and Divorce Act (U.S.) §407 [Visitation]

    • A parent not granted custody shall be entitled to reasonable visitation rights, unless, after a hearing, the court finds that such visitation would seriously endanger the child's physical, mental, moral, or emotional health.


  • Uniform Marriage and Divorce Act (U.S.) §409 [Modification]

    • A motion to modify a custody decree may not be filed within two years after the decree of divorce or legal separation, unless an affidavit demonstrates that current circumstances may seriously endanger the child's physical, mental, moral, or emotional health, in which case the court may allow the motion.

To promote the amicable resolution of disputes between spouses regarding separation or divorce, the parties may enter into a separation agreement, which may include provisions on: the division of all property, spousal support, and child support, custody, and visitation.

      (1) Optional Clause One

The court shall also consider each party's contribution to, or dissipation of, the appreciation or depreciation in value of their respective assets, as well as contributions made by one party through homemaking to the family as a whole.

  • In the proceeding, the court may protect and promote the best interests of the child, without regard to the division of assets jointly or separately held by the spouses in a separate fund or trust established for the support, maintenance, education, or public welfare of any minor, dependent, or incapacitated child.

  • United States Uniform Marriage and Divorce Act §307 [Property Distribution]

    • In cases of divorce, legal separation, or proceedings for the distribution of property, when a court that lacks personal jurisdiction over an absent spouse or lacks authority to adjudicate property issues issues a decree of divorce or legal separation, the court shall allocate one spouse's separate property to the other spouse. 6.6 Child Support

  • Uniform Marriage and Divorce Act (USA) §309 [Support of Children]

    In proceedings for divorce, legal separation, support, or child support, the court may order one or both parents who have a duty to support their children to provide reasonable or necessary support for the children, without regard to misconduct in the marital relationship, after considering all of the following relevant factors:

    (1) The financial resources of the child;

    (2) The financial resources of the custodial parent;

    (3) The standard of living the child would have enjoyed if the marriage had not been dissolved;

    (4) The physical and emotional condition of the child and the child's educational needs; and

    (5) The financial resources and needs of the non-custodial parent.

  • Uniform Marriage and Divorce Act (USA) §310 [Representation of Children]

    The court may appoint a representative for a minor or dependent child to represent the child's interests regarding support, custody, and visitation. The court shall issue an order regarding the costs of support, attorney fees, and payments, naming the representative as the payee. The order shall require one or both parents to pay; if the obligated party is unable to bear these costs, the expenses, attorney fees, and payments shall be paid by [an appropriate agency].


6.7 Spousal Support

  • Uniform Marriage and Divorce Act (USA) §308 [Maintenance]

    The court may issue a maintenance order only if it finds that the party seeking maintenance meets one of the following conditions:

    (1) The party seeking maintenance lacks sufficient property, including marital property awarded to them, to meet their reasonable needs, and is unable to support themselves through appropriate employment, or as a custodial parent, the circumstances or condition of the child make it inappropriate for the custodian to work outside the home;

    (2) The time necessary for the party seeking maintenance to acquire sufficient education or training to enable them to find suitable employment;

  • After considering all of the following relevant factors and without regard to misconduct in the marital relationship, the court shall determine the amount and duration of maintenance it deems just:

    (1) The party lacks sufficient property to meet their reasonable needs; and

    (2) The party is unable to support themselves through appropriate employment; or, as a custodial parent, the condition or circumstances of the child prevent the custodian from working outside the home.

    (3) The standard of living established during the marriage;

    (4) The duration of the marriage;

    (5) The age, physical, and emotional condition of the party seeking maintenance; and

    (6) The ability of the party from whom maintenance is sought to meet their own needs while meeting the needs of the other party.


  • Uniform Marriage and Divorce Act (USA) §316 [Modification and Termination of Maintenance, Support, and Property Distribution Provisions]

    • Except as provided in Section 306, any provision for maintenance or child support may be modified if a change in circumstances is demonstrated that renders the provision unreasonable.

    • Unless otherwise agreed in writing or specifically provided in the decree, the obligation to pay future maintenance terminates upon the death of either party or the remarriage of the party receiving maintenance.

    • Unless otherwise agreed in writing or specifically provided in the decree, the obligation to pay child support terminates when the child becomes independent. When a parent obligated to pay child support dies, the amount of support may be modified, terminated, or replaced with a lump-sum payment, as appropriate and equitable under the circumstances.

    https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/immigrate/family-immigration/nonimmigrant-visa-for-a-fiance-k-1.html 


2.2 Impact of Domestic Violence/Divorce on Visas

[In cases where you are already married]:

  • If you have been married to your U.S. spouse for more than two years, you will receive permanent resident status from U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS). From that point onward, your immigration status will no longer depend on your U.S. citizen spouse.

  • Normally, you are required to jointly file Form I-751 to remove conditions on residence to obtain permanent resident status. However, the law allows conditional residents to request a waiver of the joint filing requirement under three circumstances:

    • Removal of the conditional resident from the United States would result in extreme hardship; or

    • The marriage has been legally terminated (other than by death), and the failure to timely file the application to remove conditions was not due to the applicant's fault; or

    • During the marriage, the U.S. citizen or lawful permanent resident spouse subjected the conditional resident to battery or extreme cruelty.

  • Note that in all three waiver cases, you must still file Form I-751 and prove that your marriage was entered into "in good faith" and not for fraud.

  • See the USCIS website for information on how to apply to remove conditions on residence based on marriage: https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/immigrate/family-immigration/immigrant-visa-for-spouse.html#ExternalPopup

  • In summary, whether or not you have been married to your U.S. spouse for more than two years, domestic violence and legal divorce do not affect your right to apply for permanent resident status. Once you obtain permanent resident status, your immigration status will no longer be dependent on your spouse.


[In cases where you are not yet married]:

  • According to "Information on Legal Rights Available to Immigrant Victims of Domestic Violence in the United States and Facts about Immigrating on a Marriage-Based Visa": These immigration benefits each have specific eligibility requirements. Please consult an immigration attorney who serves victims of domestic violence to discuss how these benefits may apply to you or to seek assistance.

  • In addition, this handbook is based in part on the International Marriage Broker Regulation Act (IMBRA), a U.S. law that changed marriage-based immigration procedures to help protect foreign fiancé(e)s and spouses. IMBRA requires the U.S. government to provide foreign fiancé(e)s and spouses immigrating to the United States with information about their legal rights and to disclose any criminal history or history of domestic violence of their U.S. fiancé(e) or spouse. One of IMBRA's goals is to provide accurate information about the immigration process and about how to seek help if a marriage becomes abusive, thereby helping to protect foreign fiancé(e)s and spouses from violence by the partner who sponsored their visa.

  • Full text of IMBRA available at: https://www.congress.gov/bill/109th-congress/senate-bill/1618/text
