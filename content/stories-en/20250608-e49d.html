 Column Introduction:


In May, we continue to focus on issues of gender and ethnicity. Young women in South Korea have transformed the streets into protest theatres, reshaping the landscape of the presidential election; Helsinki Central Station once again commemorated the pioneering voices of the "Beijing Train" for women's advancement. Meanwhile, outstanding female scientists from five continents and Asian women artists are jointly contributing new ideas and breakthroughs for the future. However, hate and violence against Asians persist. The recent anti-Asian attacks in Sydney and New York, along with data showing that over half of Asian Americans still face hate, serve as stark reminders of the challenges Asians confront. It is precisely for this reason that the passing of 102-year-old Veronica Chan, the "Mother of Asian Women's Football," and the oral history collection by the Houston Asian American Archive at Rice University are especially precious. The former spent her life proving that women deserve a place on the football field, while the latter preserves the stories of Asian immigrants, ensuring they remain visible on the stage of history.

South Korean Women Voters in the Presidential Election

Take Center Stage


Last December, former President Yoon Suk-yeol was impeached by the Constitutional Court in April 2025 after briefly declaring martial law and being accused of "inciting misogynistic sentiment," sparking massive demonstrations. Among the protesters, women in their early twenties were the most active and dominant group, turning the streets into protest stages with K-pop chants and light sticks, demonstrating women's mobilizing power in South Korea's public discourse.


This year's two presidential candidates, Lee Jae-myung and Kim Moon-soo, have both avoided addressing women's issues directly, with Kim emphasizing "equal military conscription for men and women," advocating greater workplace benefits for men who complete military service, blaming low birth rates on women who "prefer raising dogs to having babies," and hiring individuals known for anti-feminist views for his campaign—actions that have provoked backlash among women. Lee leads in opinion polls and verbally acknowledges structural discrimination against women, pledging to combat dating violence, digital sexual crimes, and requiring companies to disclose gender pay gaps. Yet he avoids more controversial topics such as an "anti-discrimination law" or "no means no" rape legislation to avoid alienating young male voters who feel "reverse discrimination."


Faced with the current grim reality of gender inequality, many interviewed women said they feel they are "only choosing between bad and worse." Many will vote for Lee simply because "at least it can prevent conservative regression," holding onto the belief that "if he fails, women can take to the streets again." Despite the despairing marginalization of women's demands, South Korean women voters are gradually raising powerful political voices—through the ousting of Yoon and in the new presidential election—elevating women's issues to a core national agenda.

2025 "L'Oréal-UNESCO For Women in Science"

International Awards Announce Five Global Laureates


The "L'Oréal-UNESCO For Women in Science" program, established in 1998, is now in its 27th year, annually honoring five outstanding female scientists representing Africa and the Arab States, the Asia-Pacific region, Europe, Latin America and the Caribbean, and North America.

 

This year's laureates are, from Africa and the Arab States: Professor Priscilla Baker from the University of Cape Town, who pioneered highly sensitive micro-sensors for detecting environmental pollutants, with applications in pharmaceuticals, food, health, and energy. Asia-Pacific region: Professor Xiaoyun Wang from Tsinghua University, who revealed vulnerabilities in classical hash functions and helped establish new international standards, enhancing security for bank cards, passwords, and e-commerce. Europe: Professor Claudia Felser from the Max Planck Institute in Germany, who discovered new magnetic materials, laying the foundation for "topological quantum chemistry," advancing data storage and green energy. Latin America and the Caribbean: Professor Maria Teresa Dova from the National University of La Plata, who contributed to the discovery of the Higgs boson and elevated Argentina's international standing in high-energy physics. North America: Professor Barbara Finlayson-Pitts from the University of California, Irvine, who uncovered new mechanisms of photochemical smog formation, with findings incorporated into air quality policies.

 

Although women researchers globally still only make up 31.1%, and the gender gap remains significant, the program has supported over 4,700 female scientists to date, including Nobel laureates; through scholarships, mentorship programs, and other initiatives, it helps women overcome career barriers. Addressing global challenges such as climate change, public health, and digital security requires female scientific talent, and sustained investment and inclusive research ecosystems must be fostered across all sectors. Held in Helsinki

The past "Beijing Train" commemoration event, "30 Years, 1 Journey," took place on May 8, 2025, at Helsinki Central Railway Station, reaffirming the commitments of the 1995 Beijing Declaration and Platform for Action. Finnish political leaders, the Regional Director of UN Women, and 200 policymakers, activists, and youth advocates from Europe and Central Asia called for urgent development and implementation of


action agendas on eliminating violence, poverty reduction, digital inclusion, climate justice, and women's leadership, amid global backlash against gender equality. The#JourneyForEquality exhibition launched at the event Beijing+30 will run until May 22, guiding approximately 200,000 daily visitors through the history of regional women's movements. Speakers emphasized the theme of "the journey unfinished" through intergenerational dialogue: from Aitbek Amangeldi, the Kazakhstani influencer who campaigned to criminalize domestic violence, to Khrystyna Kit, the lawyer providing legal aid to survivors of sexual violence in war-torn Ukraine, and Adina Nivukoski, the Finnish youth advocate promoting girls' decision-making rights—all stressed that systemic change requires collective effort. The gathering warned that, at the current pace, achieving global gender equality may take another 300 years;

 

only through collaboration among governments, civil society, and the younger generation, placing gender equality at the heart of all policies, can commemoration be transformed into tangible action. Source: Anti-Asian Violence

Similar group attacks targeting Asians, especially Chinese individuals, by youth in Sydney are not isolated incidents. They reflect deep-rooted racial discrimination and lax law enforcement in the area.


Survey data shows that four out of five Asian Australians have experienced racial discrimination. The author urges victims to report incidents, calls on communities to continue pressuring police and media, emphasizes that "Chinese people are not easy targets," and recommends that students and Chinese residents travel in groups at night to avoid becoming victims again. On the afternoon of May 2, in Queens, New York, a strange young woman lured two Chinese American teenage girls—who had just finished playing basketball—into a wooded area under the pretense of helping them find a lost phone. They were then surrounded and brutally beaten for two hours by about 15 teenagers wielding iron rods, had their hair shaved as humiliation, and had videos of the assault posted online for bragging. Mary was struck in the back of the head with a baseball bat and lost consciousness immediately; the sisters also had their phones, sneakers, and jackets stolen. Police only arrived after a passerby called 911.

The case was filed only as a robbery, with two suspects temporarily arrested and the rest still at large, sparking criticism from the victims' families and the community over the juvenile justice system's leniency toward minors and law enforcement's tendency to "downgrade" such crimes. The report cites multiple recent cases across the U.S. where videos of Asian teens being beaten in groups were shared online, pointing out that


social media amplifies a "carnival of anonymity," turning violence into entertainment driven by online traffic, lowering the cost of violence and emboldening young perpetrators.

 

Chinese American lawmakers, community organizations, and mental health experts have all condemned the violence, calling for improved punishment mechanisms, stronger self-defense education for youth, and legal and psychological support for the affected families.

 

At a press conference, the victims' mother wept, saying her daughter's "trust in the world has been shattered." Messages circulating in parents' community groups warn, "Our children can never let their guard down outside," highlighting the fear and anger Asians feel in the face of systemic indifference and the fragility of public safety. A recent survey by Stop AAPI Hate and the University of Chicago's NORC research center reveals

that in 2024, 53% of Asian Americans and Pacific Islanders in the United States experienced hate incidents based on ethnicity, a slight increase from 49% in 2023.


A recent survey by Stop AAPI Hate and the University of Chicago's NORC research institute shows, in 2024, 53% of Asian Americans and Pacific Islanders in the United States experienced race-based hate incidents, a slight increase from 49% in 2023. Respondents generally believe that anti-immigrant rhetoric and the highly polarized atmosphere during the election period have fueled anti-Asian sentiment.


The report points out that victims rarely report incidents: 40% do not even disclose them to family or friends. In cases involving threats or violence—potentially illegal acts—66% are not reported to law enforcement, often due to the belief that "reporting is useless." Meanwhile, actions taken by the U.S. government under Trump's administration targeting Asians, such as efforts to cancel Chinese students' visas, attacks on birthright citizenship, and statements by Vice President Vance, have been mimicked by perpetrators in settings like schools and public transportation.


Despite the severe situation, 82% of respondents still expressed confidence in the community's collective efforts to combat racism. Most people actively participated in anti-discrimination activities over the past year and emphasized the importance of cross-ethnic solidarity. Cynthia Choi, co-founder of Stop AAPI Hate, calls on all sectors to "consistently and resolutely" resist policy rollbacks, warning that otherwise rights may be gradually stripped away.

Mother of Asian Women's Football

Veronica Chan passes away


Veronica Chan Yiu-kam (Chen Yaoyin), known as the "Mother of Asian Women's Football," passed away at home in Tin Shui Wai on the morning of June 1, 2025, at the age of 102. Chen founded the Hong Kong Ladies Football Association (HKLFA) and in 1968 co-founded the Asian Ladies Football Confederation with Malaysia and Singapore, serving as its first president. She later facilitated the hosting of the inaugural Asian Ladies Football Championship in Hong Kong in 1975. She dedicated her life's resources and energy to promoting women's football. Last year, she received the "Lifetime Achievement Award" at the 34th AFC Congress in Bangkok. As early as 2003, she was hailed by then-FIFA President Sepp Blatter as the "Mother of Women's Football in the World."


Besides women's football, Chen Yaoyin was also active in Hong Kong's men's professional football scene, having owned several First Division teams including Yuen Long, Rangers, Caroline Hill, and Hoi Fung, earning her the title of "world's first female team owner". She once chartered a helicopter to airlift Malaysian goalkeeper Chow Chee Keong to Hong Kong Stadium for a 1972 friendly match against Santos—a bold and generous act that became legendary in Hong Kong football history. The Hong Kong Football Association and numerous figures in the football community remember her "for dedicating most of her life, wealth, and energy to Hong Kong and Asian football", and have pledged to carry forward her vision of promoting women's football.

Asian women gaining visibility in the art world


This Artsy feature article, written by Mimi Wong, takes the occasion of Asian American and Pacific Islander (AAPI) Heritage Month 2025 to invite curator and gallery director Kathy Huang to recommend eight "emerging Asian American women artists to watch" . Huang notes that in recent years, Asian artists have increasingly gained visibility in major cities like New York, and their creative themes have expanded beyond "identity narratives" into abstraction, material experimentation, and interdisciplinary research, reflecting their diverse everyday lives and cultural experiences.


Ragini Bhow explores perception and space through "Gothic" geometric paintings and sculptures; Shuyi Cao blends Chinese and Western natural and scientific elements in her sculptures and installations; Kyung-Me creates subtle psychological scenes using grayscale, gongbi-style interior drawings; Wen Liu transforms Chinese medicinal herbs, resin, and clay into body-like wall sculptures; Anna Ting Möller uses organic materials such as kombucha leather and silk to explore motherhood and identity; Jacqueline Qiu weaves "tactile landscapes" using looms and natural fibers; Huidi Xiang deconstructs cartoon figures and industrial materials into humorous sculptures; Ang Ziqi Zhang, inspired by electronic music, constructs "rhythmic" abstract paintings with layered symbols and fluorescent colors.


Though these artists come from diverse Asian backgrounds, their works can be appreciated on a purely aesthetic level while also reflecting deeper experiences of migration, gender, and globalization.

The birth and mission of the Houston Asian Archive


Anne Chao, a historian at Rice University's Chao Center for Asian Studies, created and expanded the "Houston Asian Archive" (Houston Asian American Archive, HAAA) , to preserve oral histories and rare documents of Asian immigrants in the Greater Houston area. Chao points out that although Houston hosts one of the largest Asian communities in the U.S., there has long been a lack of systematic historical preservation. For over a decade, she has worked with students and interns to conduct interviews and collect photographs and documents, amassing over 500 audiovisual oral histories and a large collection of physical archives—so extensive that some materials have been moved off-campus from the university library for storage.


The report highlights representative stories preserved by HAAA: the Gee family, who pioneered a dual "grocery store to white-collar" development path; Vietnamese boat people who fled their homeland; and the Japanese American Kondo family, who rebuilt their lives after World War II internment and expanded their sushi business in Houston. Chao hopes to further broaden the scope of the collection so that the everyday lives and struggles of more Asian communities can be seen,, while also helping young volunteers rebuild their own identity through the process of documenting others' experiences.
