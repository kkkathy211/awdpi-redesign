The controversy has revealed only the tip of the iceberg regarding the complex relationship between sports events and domestic violence cases. Research shows that sporting events can act as amplifiers in already violent relationships. Influenced by emotions tied to competitive culture—such as winning and losing—perpetrators are more likely to commit domestic violence during major tournaments, with women being the primary victims.


Why does the number of domestic violence cases surge during sports events? Why do the emotions of watching a game turn into harm against intimate partners? How can we prevent gender-based violence, including domestic abuse?


The women behind the scenes of football tournaments

who suffer in silence


Before this year's World Cup kicked off, the UK Crown Prosecution Service issued a warning: based on past data, incidents of domestic violence rise during major football events like the World Cup, and they urged victims to seek help promptly.


This warning was confirmed during this year's World Cup.


According to statistics from the UK National Police Chiefs' Council, during the 2026 World Cup, a total of 2322 football-related criminal offences were recorded across England, nearly one-fifth of which were domestic violence cases. However, this figure does not represent the total number of domestic violence reports received during the same period, but only those cases where football was recorded as an aggravating factor.


UK domestic violence organisation Women's Aid cited relevant studies showing that incidents of domestic violence in the UK tend to increase after England matches, rising by approximately 38% when the team loses and by about 26% when the team wins or draws. Tanisha Janegaram, helpline manager at Respect—the UK's perpetrator behaviour intervention service—also observed an increase in call volume to their helpline during match periods. 37%.


One domestic violence survivor who received support from the UK charity Woman's Trust shared that on match days, she and her children would live in constant fear, carefully gauging their partner's mood; the partner would force the family to watch the game together and prevent them from going out as they pleased.


Furthermore, drawing from global research on intimate partner violence, women have long borne a disproportionately high risk of violence within intimate relationships.


Research by the World Health Organization shows that globally, about one in three women experience physical or sexual violence in their lifetime, with most of this violence perpetrated by intimate partners. Data released in 2025 by the UK Office for National Statistics also reveals a clear gender disparity: women account for 72.1% of victims in police-recorded domestic abuse crimes, and in all types of domestic violence cases, the proportion of female victims exceeds that of males..


Under the "amplifier" effect,

power imbalances spiral out of control and

perpetrator accountability is diminished.


During sporting events, heightened emotions, crowd gatherings, and alcohol consumption all contribute to an increased risk of domestic violence.


Intense matches captivate audiences, causing viewers to become highly tense, holding their breath as every move on the pitch triggers strong emotional reactions. In such a charged atmosphere, fans on opposing sides may develop disagreements over results or refereeing decisions, escalating from verbal arguments to physical altercations. If these uncontrolled emotions are brought into the home, they may easily give rise to domestic violence.


Beyond the impact of emotional intensity, quantitative studies have also exposed the link between alcohol and violence.


The likelihood of drinking increases during match viewing, and group drinking becomes more frequent; the physiological stimulation caused by alcohol becomes one of the catalysts for extreme behaviour. A University of Warwick study analysing ten years of crime data (2010–2019) from West Midlands Police found that reports of alcohol-related domestic violence increased by 47% after England victories, confirming a clear association between alcohol and domestic violence during sporting events.


However, domestic violence incidents during sports events should not be simplistically understood as perpetrators "losing control" or "acting in the heat of the moment." Behind such violent acts lie deep-rooted gender norms and unequal power dynamics within intimate relationships.


From this perspective, sporting events are more like "amplifiers" of risk. Factors such as match outcomes, alcohol, and collective emotions may increase the likelihood of conflict. Under pre-existing gendered power structures, these factors can further intensify tensions within households, placing women and children—those in subordinate positions—under greater threat of violence.


Additionally, the emphasis on competition, winning, and "masculinity" during tournaments, when combined with existing power imbalances at home, may make previously hidden gender-based violence more visible.


To respond to the surge in domestic violence cases during sporting events and protect victims, BBC reports that UK police launch special operations during high-risk periods such as sports tournaments and holidays to strengthen protection for domestic abuse survivors. For example, officers proactively visit vulnerable groups most at risk of domestic abuse, offering support, safety advice, and ensuring their protection.


However, protection limited to specific high-risk periods is far from sufficient to address the root problem of domestic violence. Such temporary measures fail to tackle the underlying causes.


In the short term, current UK measures help protect known high-risk households but rely heavily on existing reports and police records, making it easy to miss victims who have not yet sought help. Government-commissioned research also shows that while protection orders can enhance some victims' sense of safety, existing evidence cannot fully prove the effectiveness of these temporary policies in preventing perpetrators from reoffending.


Long-term measures deserve greater attention. The UK's "IRIS" programme trains general practitioners to identify domestic abuse and directly refer patients to specialist services; in Austria, after police require perpetrators to leave the home, the Violence Protection Centre proactively contacts victims, and perpetrators must undergo ongoing preventive counselling.


These systems reduce, to some extent, the burden on victims to seek help independently, but their success depends on sustained funding for professional services and coordination across agencies; related programmes still require long-term, independent evaluation.


Nicole Jacobs, Domestic Abuse Commissioner for England and Wales, after surveying over 4,000 survivors, stated: "Who you are and where you live determines whether you can access domestic abuse services." She advocates for the government to assume long-term funding responsibility, rather than leaving service providers to survive on short-term projects.


Stepping beyond the home,

underpaid female footballers

and women fans frequently subjected to abuse


The normalisation and justification of gender-based violence not only leave women trapped and helpless within the home, but also make it difficult for women on the pitch and in the stands to participate freely.


Global Web Index's 2022 survey data shows that among global football fans, men account for approximately 57.5% and women 42.5%. Although the number of female fans is substantial, their presence is still widely overlooked.


A survey by UK football anti-discrimination charity Kick It Out found that over half of female respondents reported experiencing gender-based discrimination on match days, with some also reporting unwanted physical contact, physical violence, and sexual assault.


In online spaces, female fans face similar challenges: over 30% have experienced football-related discrimination online, and some women refrain from posting content due to fear of discrimination.


Beyond fans, female footballers also face severe challenges in terms of career development and opportunities.


Compared to men's football, women's football still lags significantly in commercial resources and professional infrastructure. The 2026 Men's World Cup attracted a total of 6.81 million in-stadium spectators, while the 2023 Women's World Cup drew around 1.98 million.


A 2025 survey by the FIFPRO (International Professional Footballers' Association) of 407 national team players across 41 countries found that 66% of female players earn less than $20,000 annually from football, with nearly a quarter still needing to work outside football; 33% have club contracts lasting one year or less, and 22% do not even have a club contract.


Clearly, for many female players, football remains an unstable profession, and those who participate face significantly higher career risks.


Historically, women have long been present in football. As early as 1921, England already had around 150 women's football clubs. Yet in the same year, the Football Association banned women's teams from using professional pitches belonging to its affiliated clubs, claiming football was "unsuitable for women"—a ban that lasted nearly 50 years.


This history shows that women's past absence from football does not reflect a lack of interest or audience, but rather that they were systematically excluded.



In recent years, some sports organisations have begun attempting to address gender discrimination and domestic violence.


In 2017, UEFA launched the "#EqualGame"#EqualGame campaign, emphasising that football should be open to everyone, regardless of gender, age, race, sexual orientation, physical ability, or social background—everyone should have the right to participate in and enjoy football.


During the 2023 Women's World Cup, FIFA partnered with UN Women to launch the "#WeAreEqual" initiative, promoting gender equality and calling for an end to gender-based violence in and around football. "Football Unites the World" launched initiatives such as "Unite to End Violence against Women" under the framework, promoting them through channels including team captains' armbands, stadium advertisements, on-site big screens, and social media.

Sports should bring joy and passion, not fear and threats. After the final whistle blows and the score becomes history, everyone who has competed on the field, enjoyed the game, or returned to daily life deserves safety and dignity.


As Natalie Fleet, the UK official responsible for women and girls' safety and anti-violence issues, stated,

"No one should live in fear because of a sporting event."
