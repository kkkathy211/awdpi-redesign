With streaked brown long hair, aviator glasses, a fitted top, and bell-bottom pants, she was the most recognizable face of the second wave feminist movement in the United States—Gloria Steinem. She was a feminist leader of the 1970s, a journalist, writer, and activist, hailed by the media as a style icon and "rock star."


On September 2, 2026, Gloria Steinem passed away at her home in New York. Her foundation announced that the house would become a permanent space for organizing activities and activism.


It was in the living room of this residence in Manhattan's Upper East Side, 55 years earlier, that she and other like-minded feminist activists co-founded Ms. magazine, the first national publication in the United States entirely created and run by women to discuss gender-related public issues.


During the era of surging women's liberation movements, she traveled widely, speaking out for various social justice causes: she went on speaking tours across college campuses, helped women of minority ethnicities draft conference platforms in hotel rooms, and marched with farm workers across the desert along the Mexican border.


She left behind a vast and valuable legacy, the most important part of which is her body of writing on feminist thought, political ideas, and personal experiences. By revisiting her works from different periods, this article attempts to trace how one highly influential and controversial feminist participated in and shaped gender politics in the United States and globally after the 1960s.


In 1972, Gloria campaigned in California for state senate candidate Catherine O'Neill.

Gloria on the move

The RV, India, and New York (1934–1968)


In 1934, Gloria was born into a lower-middle-class family in Toledo, Ohio. Her father was a traveling antiques dealer who seemed to have an instinctive fear of settling down.


Before Gloria turned ten, every summer he operated a small resort and dance hall in Michigan, and during fall and winter, he took his wife and two daughters on travels across the country to buy and sell antiques. This unusual lifestyle meant Gloria rarely had the chance to settle in one place. Only after her parents divorced did she return with her mother to Toledo and enroll in a local high school.


Gloria and her mother Ruth in a trailer, 1930s

Photo source: Gloria's Foundation


Gloria's mother Ruth had been one of the few female journalists at a mainstream newspaper in Toledo, which sparked Gloria's early ambition to become a journalist. Gloria's grandmother Pauline, who died when Gloria was five, was the first woman elected to public office in Toledo and had been active in the early 20th-century American women's suffrage movement.


Yet neither of these women managed to free themselves from domestic life. Ruth gave up her career after marriage and eventually developed severe mental illness. These experiences led Gloria to realize that the struggle for women's freedom would not end with the right to vote or to work.


After high school, Gloria followed her sister's path and enrolled at Smith College, a renowned women's liberal arts college in the United States at the time, majoring in government.


In 1957, driven by interest in international politics and communism, and also to escape an arranged engagement, she applied for a scholarship to study in India. During her 18 months in India, she did not remain a passive American student or tourist, but immersed herself in local life: she wore saris and rode in women-only train compartments, traveled to villages with Gandhian activists, and participated in grassroots nonviolent mediation work. It was in post-colonial rural India that this future prominent American civil rights activist first encountered principles of listening, non-hierarchy, and collective decision-making—principles she would later integrate into the American women's movement decades afterward.


One of Gloria's tasks in India was writing a travel guide for foreign readers titled *A Thousand Indias* (1957), commissioned by the Indian government. This assignment allowed her to travel freely across India and deeply engage with local lives, ideas, and customs. Today the book is hard to find, but its title alone reveals Gloria's refusal to portray India as a single, homogeneous entity, instead emphasizing the country's internal complexity and diversity.


Gloria in a sari in New Delhi, where she studied at Miranda House, the top women's college at Delhi University

Photo source: Gloria's Foundation


After returning to the United States, Gloria moved to New York in 1960 to fulfill her mother's unrealized dream of becoming a New York journalist. At the time, journalism still followed strict gender divisions. Female reporters were typically assigned to cover fashion, family, marriage, and private life, while serious political topics were the exclusive domain of male journalists. When Gloria proposed to her editor that she cover a political figure, she was instead assigned to write about his wife; while marching with writers and editors in anti-Vietnam War protests, she was simultaneously writing articles about movie stars and tropical vacations.


Gloria Steinem's *A Bunny's Tale* for *Show* magazine, 1963

Photo source: X/@girlpowertbh


In early 1963, to contribute to *Show* magazine, Gloria went undercover as a Playboy Bunny at a Playboy Club. In this seemingly glamorous and "respectable" job, she endured constant harassment from customers, suffered severe foot injuries from wearing high heels for long hours, and lost over ten pounds in just a few weeks.


She later published these experiences in an article titled *A Bunny's Tale*, exposing the low wages and harsh working conditions endured by the Bunnies. The article brought her instant fame, but also further restricted her opportunities to secure serious reporting assignments. In the years that followed, the label "Bunny" continued to follow her, making it difficult for her to obtain other types of reporting assignments.


That same year, Gloria also co-authored *The Beach Book*. This lighthearted book on beach and leisure culture included topics such as sunbathing tips, beach games, romantic fantasies, literary excerpts, and various oddities about beaches.

This work, so atypical in Gloria's career, unexpectedly became historical evidence of gender discrimination in journalism: at the time, for any woman seeking entry into the field, writing about "women's topics" was often the only available path.


Gloria Steinem, The Beach Book

Photo source: Tori Jones Studio


A star in the women's liberation movement

The podium, the political arena, and the press (1969–1986)


In 1968, Gloria gained her own political column in *New York* magazine. At the same time, between the civil rights movement and the New Left, an independent American women's liberation movement was brewing.


In March 1969, Gloria attended an abortion hearing organized by the radical feminist group Redstockings as a journalist. Listening to women share their personal stories, she recalled her own illegal abortion in Britain shortly after graduation. She found herself taking notes while tears streamed down her face, realizing for the first time that she was part of this movement.


In early April 1969, as a newly awakened feminist, Gloria published *After Black Power, Women's Liberation*. While many participants in the feminist movement at the time found the article's arguments somewhat naive, it achieved broad success. Gloria was subsequently invited to speak on the emerging topic of "women's liberation." She never expected that she, someone with an intense fear of public speaking, would become a professional public activist from that point on.


From then on, she embarked on a nationwide speaking tour with her friend Dorothy Pitman Hughes—a seasoned speaker. Audiences discovered that Gloria was articulate, quick-witted, and skilled at expressing her views through humorous and ironic aphorisms. She once said:

"Actress Lee Grant told me she was married to a Marxist and a fascist, and neither of them would take out the trash." "If women could really get ahead by sleeping with men, there'd be a lot more women in power today."


In 1971, Gloria and Dorothy took the iconic photograph of the second wave feminist movement. Standing side by side, both raised their fists—a gesture borrowed from the Black Power movement—embodying an image of cross-racial feminist alliance. Dorothy was a child welfare advocate and Black feminist activist.

Photo source: Wynn Archive and Farmani Group


Through conversations with audiences, she realized that many women were facing similar struggles in isolation, with nowhere to turn. In 1971, she helped co-found the Women's Action Alliance (WAA) to help grassroots women across the country share information, organizational experiences, and resources.


That same year, she co-founded, along with feminist activists Bella Abzug, Betty Friedan, and Shirley Chisholm, the National Women's Political Caucus (NWPC), whose main goals included advancing the passage of the Equal Rights Amendment (ERA), and recruiting, training, and supporting women candidates for public office. The organization continues to this day to promote women's participation in elections and political systems. In 1972, she also co-founded the Ms. Foundation For Women National Organization for Women's Legal Defense and Education Fund, a national foundation that particularly focused on then-controversial issues such as abortion, contraception, sexual assault, domestic violence, and child custody rights for LGBTQ+ women.


In 1971, the founders of NWPC attended a meeting. From left to right in the photo: Betty Friedan, Elinor Guggenheimer, Eleanor Holmes Norton, and Gloria Steinem.

Photo source: NWPC


Through these activities, Gloria and her friends gradually realized that the United States needed a mainstream magazine capable of promoting the feminist movement. In 1972, she co-founded Ms. magazine, which later became the signature publication of the second wave feminist movement.


The magazine not only discussed issues such as abortion and reproductive rights, domestic violence, sexual harassment, employment discrimination, and equal pay, but also reported news and commentary on politics, diplomacy, global peace and war, and the political and economic conditions of women worldwide.

The magazine was an immediate success, selling out at newsstands across the United States within eight days, and gradually became an international platform for spreading feminist ideas and information. Years later, Gloria recalled in an interview: "When we started, there wasn't even a word for 'sexual harassment'—that was just called 'life'."


Gloria in the newsroom and the first issue of Ms. magazine in 1972.

Photo source: Smith College Special Collections; NY Daily News Archive via Getty Images


Throughout the 1970s, Gloria's life became increasingly occupied by political organizing and running the magazine. She became a full-time feminist activist, which brought her back onto the road. As she later recalled, in the two decades after she began touring as a speaker, the longest she ever stayed continuously in her New York home was eight days.


As the women's liberation movement entered the public eye, Gloria gradually became a celebrity, frequently appearing in newspapers, on television, and on radio programs. Yet this success also drew controversy and criticism from both within and outside the movement:

To conservatives, she was a radical threatening family, motherhood, and traditional gender order; to radical feminists, she was seen as too moderate and commercialized, criticized as "bourgeois feminism"; for women of color and non-Western feminists, issues of race and class were often treated as mere decoration within the mainstream white feminism she represented.


However, these criticisms from different directions precisely reflected her ambition and ability to navigate across diverse issues.


More than a decade after dedicating herself to the feminist movement, Gloria finally published her "first real book" in 1983, "Outrageous Acts and Everyday Rebellion" a collection of her reporting and essays over the years, including her undercover story at the Playboy Club, discussions on female genital mutilation across time and space, personal memories of her mother, and profiles of several prominent women.


In "If Men Could Menstruate" (1978) this famous short essay, she conducted a thought experiment: What would happen if, suddenly, it was men instead of women who menstruated? She argued that menstruation would then become a symbol of masculinity, something to be celebrated and proud of: churches and families would hold coming-of-age ceremonies and celebrations for boys experiencing their first period, Congress would allocate special funding to study menstrual pain, and sanitary products would be government-subsidized or even free. With skillful use of humor and satire, she exposed to readers the social construction and power dynamics behind seemingly natural gender differences.


Outrageous Acts and Everyday Rebellion, Henry Holt & Company, 1983

Photo source: AbeBooks


Bringing Feminism Home

Inner Revolution and the Ongoing Witness (1987–2026)


In the 1980s, with Ronald Reagan's rise to power, the political climate in the United States began shifting to the right.


In 1982, the Equal Rights Amendment ultimately failed to be ratified by the extended deadline, symbolizing the ebbing of the once-powerful women's liberation movement. As a central figure in the movement, Gloria herself faced repeated setbacks during this time. In 1981, her mother Ruth passed away. Five years later, she was diagnosed with breast cancer, yet her work—promoting her new book, fundraising for the magazine, coordinating foundation affairs, and political activism—did not stop.


In the late 1980s, Gloria fell into unbearable exhaustion and depression. On a friend's suggestion, she began psychotherapy. This prompted her to turn her gaze inward for the first time, leading her to write a series of works focusing on inner psychology and personal experience.


In 1986, Gloria published "Marilyn: Norma Jean" a book in which she first showed interest in a psychological perspective. Influenced by child psychologist W. Hugh Missildine's research on "childhood neglect," Gloria linked Marilyn Monroe's emotional struggles in adulthood to her unstable childhood and prolonged lack of consistent care.

In the book, she deliberately distinguished between the publicly familiar "Marilyn" and "Norma Jeane," revealing how gender norms, the film industry, and popular culture collectively constructed the iconic image of Marilyn Monroe as a sex symbol, while obscuring her as a complex woman with deep emotions and desires.


Marilyn: Norma Jean, Open Road Media, 2013

Photo source: internet


Gloria's life bore striking similarities to Marilyn's: both had unstable childhoods and mothers with mental illness. Writing about Marilyn's experience, in a way, allowed Gloria to re-understand herself.


In 1992, she published "Revolution from Within: a Book of Self-Esteem" a book that discussed the formation and social roots of self-esteem from a feminist perspective. In it, Gloria combined personal experience, historical analysis, and psychological self-exploration methods such as the concept of the "inner child." Even in this self-help-style book, Gloria did not abandon her familiar political and power analyses.

She argued that women's low self-esteem is closely tied to childhood socialization and experiences of oppression within patriarchal culture, and encouraged readers to reclaim their agency by re-examining themselves and understanding their past experiences.


The book received mixed reviews: some readers found courage in her personal stories to face their own struggles, while some critics argued that emphasizing personal psychology and self-change might re-privatize structural political issues.


Revolution From Within: A Book of Self-Esteem, Bloomsbury, 1992

Photo source: internet


In the 1994 book "Moving Beyond Words" Gloria once again demonstrated her sharp journalistic vision and writing talent. This collection spanned psychology, the body, media, wealth, and economics, with half the essays previously published in Ms. magazine and the other half newly written.


In "What If Freud Were Phyllis; or, The Watergate of the Western World" an essay, she again used gender-reversal satire to expose the male-centered framework of Freudian psychoanalysis. In "Revaluing Economics" she turned her critique toward traditional economics, challenging how it ignores the value of unpaid housework, caregiving, and women's labor.


Moving Beyond Words: Age, Rage, Sex, Power, Money, Muscles: Breaking the Boundries of Gender, Touchstone, 1995.

Photo source: internet


In 2006, at age 72, she published "Doing Sixty and Seventy" a book just over sixty pages long, expanded from the essay "Doing Sixty" in Moving Beyond Words. In it, drawing from her personal experience of entering old age, Gloria reflected on how age functions as a social category and power relation.


She wrote that as women age, they seem to become "invisible" in the public eye, yet this state also means a certain liberation: when women no longer need to conform to gender expectations, they gain more space to live and act according to their own desires. Therefore, she advocated transforming "old age" from a private condition to be hidden into a socially recognized and collectively organized identity. Perhaps for this reason, she always opened her public speeches by stating her age.


Doing Sixty & Seventy, Elders Academy Press, 2006

Photo source: internet


After turning 80, Gloria continued writing, traveling, and giving speeches. In 2015, she published her first autobiography, "My Life on the Road" Two decades earlier, she had realized she should write this book, but had continually postponed it due to her constant travels. In it, she shared for the first time her childhood and youth, devoting extensive space to the people and events she encountered while traveling as an activist.


At the end of the book, she dedicated it to the London doctor who had helped her obtain an illegal abortion when she was 22. As the title suggests, "on the road" became the defining lifestyle and political practice of Gloria's life: meeting others on the journey, listening to their stories, and then turning them into political action.


My Life on the Road, Random House, 2015

Photo source: internet


In 2019, she published the essay collection "The Truth Will Set You Free, But First, It Will Piss You Off! Thoughts on Life, Love, and Rebellion" which includes six short essays and a selection of quotes and aphorisms drawn from decades of speeches, writings, and interviews. Each chapter also features quotations from feminist friends such as Audre Lorde, Bell Hooks, and Florynce Kennedy.


The Truth Will Set You Free, But First It Will Piss You Off!: Thoughts on Life, Love, and Rebellion, Random House, 2019. Photo source: internet


Three weeks after Gloria's death, her posthumous memoir "An Unexpected Life: A Memoir" (2026) was published by Random House. As her final memoir written in later life, the book revisits her childhood and youth, recounting her travels with her father, caring for her mother who suffered from mental illness, and her first participation in a social movement in India. It reveals how these life experiences shaped her subsequent thinking and actions.


She re-examined over sixty years of progress and setbacks in the American feminist movement, turning her gaze toward the younger generation and leaving them her experiences, lessons, and hopes. Until the final stage of her life, she continued to engage in social activism in various ways—hosting activism workshops for a new generation of organizers, joining protests on the streets of Manhattan, and using social media to speak out for transgender rights.


An Unexpected Life: A Memoir, Random House, 2026

Photo source: internet


From childhood, Gloria had longed for a stable home; yet most of her life was spent on the road. Only after gradually building her own living space and forming interpersonal connections across the globe through long journeys and activism did she realize that home and travel were no longer mutually exclusive ways of life. What she left for the world was a space always open to new companions.


Gloria in her New York home. "I have a kitchen that really works, a real desk where I can spread out my papers, and a room where visiting friends can stay—a place people want to come into. This is exactly what I always wanted when I was a child, living with my mother in places so bleak we couldn't invite anyone in." —Gloria Steinem, My Life on the Road, 2015. Photo source: Architectural Digest


Gloria Steinem's life constitutes a microcosm of the post-World War II American feminist movement. As a descendant of suffragists, she strove to travel, study, and work like men, only to discover that her efforts could not free her from the constraints imposed by gender.


She devoted her life to realizing a political ideal: building a women's political alliance across racial, class, religious, and geographical divides, united by shared gender experiences. Yet this vision, once capable of mobilizing immense political energy, has lost its persuasive power today.


Remembering Gloria Steinem is not because we need a feminist to be held up as a model. On the contrary, her life—shaped equally by acclaim and controversy—reminds us that women cannot and need not conform to any predetermined image. Precisely for this reason, we can still draw inspiration and courage for continued action by re-examining, questioning, and reinterpreting her rich legacy from diverse historical perspectives.
