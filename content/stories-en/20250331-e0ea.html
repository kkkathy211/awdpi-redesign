Introduction:

      The survival challenges faced by Asian American women in academia constitute a complex and pressing issue in contemporary higher education and research. Although Asian communities are often labeled as the "model minority," extensive research shows that these women encounter systemic barriers in academic advancement, resource access, and identity formation. From evaluation biases caused by implicit prejudice, to professional isolation stemming from cultural identity conflicts, to the difficulty of balancing family responsibilities with academic aspirations, the struggles of Asian American women scholars reflect deep-seated structural discrimination and a lack of cultural inclusivity within the academic system.


I

Challenges



1.1 Structural Wage Gap

Economic security is the foundation of personal freedom and a key determinant of whether women can live independently in society, directly affecting their social status and quality of life. For Asian American women in academia, economic factors are not only crucial for personal independence but also closely tied to the opportunities and challenges they face in their careers.

      Asian American women have long experienced structural inequality in compensation. Due to the intersection of race and gender, they are paid less than White individuals or Asian men in comparable positions. A 2021 study by Tukwila found that for every dollar earned by a White man, an Asian American woman earns only 85 cents. Over time, this gap accumulates: over the course of their careers, Asian American women earn approximately $400,000 less than their male counterparts in the same roles (McGee et al., 2024). This wage disadvantage extends beyond base salaries to include bonuses, promotion-related pay increases, and other supplemental income. In high-paying fields such as law and medicine, the wage gap for Asian American women is particularly pronounced. For example, Asian American women in the legal profession earn less on average than their White female and male peers. These data collectively demonstrate how the intersection of race and gender intensifies wage inequity for Asian American women. In higher education, despite Asian faculty members generally holding higher levels of education—78.5% of Asian faculty possess a doctoral or professional degree compared to 64.3% of White faculty—their rate of salary growth remains lower than that of White colleagues. Asian American women face even greater disparities compared to Asian men. Their salary returns are significantly affected at the associate and assistant professor levels, and their salary increases upon promotion to full professor remain lower than those of both White women and Asian men (Lee & Wong, 2018). Wage inequity hinders women's advancement in higher education and implicitly signals their undervaluation and diminished voice within the academic system. If fair compensation cannot be ensured, Asian American women may hesitate to enter or remain in academia, opting instead for higher-paying industries, thereby further weakening their representation and influence in the field and perpetuating a vicious cycle.



1.2 Lack of Social Support

      As members of the so-called "model minority," Asian Americans are often perceived as "passive, obedient, apolitical, and socially assumed to lack charisma." At the same time, they are portrayed as perpetual foreigners, seen under white supremacist ideologies as outsiders who cannot fully integrate into American society (Lee, 2015; Museus, 2014). These stereotypes lead to social isolation and create significant barriers when seeking collaboration or building professional networks. A lack of social capital translates into greater difficulty in securing promotions and publishing academic work, making it harder for them to enter core academic circles. Moreover, interviews conducted by Castro & Collins (2021) reveal that some Asian American women, after enduring long-term discrimination, internalize external denials and develop self-loathing. They not only frequently doubt their own abilities but may also develop alienation toward the Asian community itself. This mindset makes them more cautious in social choices—for example, avoiding Asian community gatherings—further distancing themselves from networks that could otherwise offer mutual support.



1.3 Unequal Distribution of Service Responsibilities

      Previous research has shown that Asian American women faculty face institutional barriers in pursuing academic success. A particularly salient issue is the unequal distribution of service duties, which are often neither properly recognized nor rewarded (Llorens et al., 2021; O'Meara, 2016). Survey data indicate that women are twice as likely as men to participate in service activities within the same department (Domingo et al., 2022). Notably, due to institutional diversity requirements, women of color are often assigned a "cultural tax"—they are frequently asked to join multiple committees, especially those focused on race-specific issues (Castro & Collins, 2019), and to perform related service work. Despite carrying this heavy additional burden, the role of service work in retention, tenure, and promotion (RTP) processes lacks clear and consistent definition. Compared to comprehensive and teaching-intensive institutions, such service is often excluded from official promotion guidelines and thus treated as secondary and undervalued (Chou, 2016). This creates a false appearance of diversity. Under the guise of promoting inclusivity, the excessive service burden placed on Asian American women—particularly in supporting underrepresented students—is rationalized, leading them to feel "used" or "overlooked" (Hanasono et al., 2019).



1.4 Dual Stereotypes

     Asian American women in U.S. STEM fields (science, technology, engineering, and mathematics) face dehumanization at the intersection of race and gender. Due to their Asian identity, they are stereotyped as nerds—socially awkward, mechanically rigid, and over-evolved human machines. This stereotype denies their emotional expressiveness, rendering them as subhuman (McGee et al., 2017; Shah, 2019). Furthermore, this image leads to their independent thinking and creativity being undervalued; their achievements are often reduced to rote test performance. Leslie et al. (2015) found that the more theoretical a field is, the less likely Asian American women are to enter it, as success in such fields is believed to require innate talent rather than diligence. On the other hand, although there is a widespread positive stereotype that Asians are "good at math," this favorable image does not translate into meaningful support for Asian American women. Instead, they are more often affected by negative stereotypes that women perform poorly in science or are less technically competent than men (Williams et al., 2014). Asian American women must provide significantly more proof of ability to be seen as equally intellectually capable as members of other racial groups.



1.5 Normalization of Implicit Discrimination and Harassment

      Due to the dual factors of race and gender, Asian American women are more vulnerable to harassment and exclusion in environments dominated by White men. Research on STEM fields shows that in academic and laboratory settings, women are frequently subjected to malicious jokes or demeaning comments from male colleagues. Over time, such harassment becomes normalized or dismissed as "just jokes." Even when many women feel uncomfortable, few speak up to challenge it. These behaviors erode women's confidence in their careers and may even lead them to change career paths or exit academia altogether to avoid long-term discomfort and more serious psychological consequences. Microaggressions are defined as "subtle, everyday insults and offenses directed at socially devalued groups" (Castro, 2020).

     These behaviors can be intentional or unintentional, but often take the form of subtle language or actions—such as ignoring Asian individuals or making jokes about Asian American women at work. Microaggressions are more invisible and routine than overt harassment, yet they are more damaging to the self-esteem and confidence of Asian American women. Accumulated over time, these microaggressions make Asian American women feel undervalued at work, leading them to question their worth and even internalize negative messages, further weakening their willingness to participate actively in academic environments and limiting their professional growth (Castro, 2020).



1.6 Credibility Challenges Due to Language and Background

      The dominance of mainstream English culture profoundly affects the credibility of Asian American women in academia. As many Asian American women are non-native English speakers and were raised in Asian cultural contexts, they are often seen as not fitting the traditional image of a scholar—particularly the prototypical White male scientist in STEM fields. The image of successful academics is frequently shaped by Western-centric, White-dominated, and male-dominated value systems, forcing Asian American women to work harder to prove their competence and credibility within this system. They are often expected to act "more assertive" to gain recognition, yet such behavior is simultaneously seen as violating societal gender expectations for women. This intersection of linguistic, cultural, and gender-based oppression makes it more difficult for Asian American women to gain recognition and advance in U.S. academia (Castro, 2020).


II

 Relevant Laws and Government Policies


     Expanding the scope of Title IX of the Civil Rights Act is an important policy reform aimed at more comprehensively protecting multiply marginalized groups, including Asian American women, from discrimination based on gender, race, and other intersecting identities. The background and significance of this reform have been deeply explored in the Boston University Law Review; for example, the Biden administration's proposed new rules particularly emphasize applying an intersectional lens to Title IX implementation, ensuring it covers a broader range of discriminatory practices (Boston University Law Review, 2023a). Specific measures include extending Title IX protections to cover gender identity, gender expression, and intersectional discrimination, while establishing new federal rules requiring educational institutions to monitor and address intersectional discrimination (Boston University Law Review, 2023b). Additionally, training for educators and administrators is being implemented to enhance their ability to identify implicit biases and systemic barriers, alongside the provision of supportive resources such as mental health counseling and reporting mechanisms. The implementation of these measures will further strengthen equity and inclusivity in educational environments, offering Asian American women and other minority groups a safer academic and social space.

      This reform will bring structural transformation to the entire education system, fostering a fairer and more inclusive academic environment. Through this framework, career pathways for Asian American women will become smoother, and the growing number of success stories and increased visibility will inspire and serve as role models for future scholars. This not only promotes diversity and equality within academia but also provides reference points and momentum for social change in other fields.

      However, the effectiveness of this policy reform also requires further critical examination. First, the implementation of expanded policies demands strong cooperation from educational institutions, which may lack sufficient resources or motivation to carry out complex intersectional protection measures. Moreover, implicit biases and systemic discrimination are often deeply rooted in culture and structure, making them difficult to eliminate through legal protections alone. Second, it is worth noting whether expanding the scope of Title IX might trigger new legal controversies—particularly in the context of conservative opposition to diversity policies, such reforms may face legal challenges or even the risk of policy reversal.



2.1 Federal and State Policies

      The National Science Foundation (NSF) and academic publishers such as Cell Press have already taken some measures to promote diversity and inclusion, providing greater support for Asian American women scholars. For example, the NSF, through its "Creating Opportunities Everywhere" (COE) and "Centers of Research Excellence in Science and Technology" (CREST) programs, prioritizes funding for underrepresented groups, including Asian American women scholars, helping them secure research funding and participate in cutting-edge STEM research (Wang, 2023). These programs also enhance academic resources and development opportunities for Asian American women scholars by supporting research capacity building at minority-serving institutions. Meanwhile, Cell Press has appointed officers for inclusion and diversity to promote equitable academic evaluation standards and encourage submissions from underrepresented scholars, thereby increasing the influence of Asian American women scholars in academia (Pengpai News, 2021).

      These measures have, to some extent, promoted fairer resource distribution, provided better career development platforms for Asian American women scholars, and advanced diversity and inclusion in academia. However, these policies also have shortcomings. First, their implementation is largely concentrated in STEM fields, offering insufficient support for Asian American women in the humanities or other academic disciplines (Espino, 2024). Additionally, most existing policies focus on single dimensions of race or gender, overlooking the complex challenges Asian American women face at the intersection of race and gender, potentially limiting the comprehensiveness of policy impacts. Furthermore, in the context of competition for limited resources, these measures may trigger tensions among different minority groups. More importantly, these initiatives lack systematic evaluation and oversight mechanisms, making it difficult to accurately assess their real-world outcomes and potentially leading to formalistic resource allocation or implementation that fails to genuinely benefit Asian American women scholars.



2.2 Institutional Policies

      Reducing unconscious bias is crucial for the advancement of Asian American women in U.S. academia, and integrating relevant measures with institutional policies is key to achieving fairness and inclusion. First, unconscious bias training is an important tool for raising awareness among faculty and staff (Herbert, 2013). Many universities have begun implementing such training, for example, using Implicit Association Tests (IAT) and reflective thinking exercises to help identify and reduce bias (Spragg, 2023). However, to ensure that training outcomes translate into actual behavior, institutions can incorporate it into regular professional development programs and mandate through policy that all hiring committee members complete bias training. This measure can directly improve fairness in hiring and promotion processes.

      Second, structured hiring and promotion procedures play a significant role in reducing implicit bias. Some academic institutions have already adopted methods such as anonymized screening, standardized scoring, and diverse hiring committees to enhance fairness. Institutions can use policy to set clear diversity goals, such as ensuring a certain proportion of Asian American women applicants in final selection stages. Additionally, cross-departmental diversity review mechanisms can be established to increase transparency in these processes. These measures will effectively reduce the implicit barriers Asian American women face when competing for senior positions.

      Third, data monitoring and feedback mechanisms are essential for identifying bias patterns and formulating targeted policies. While many institutions have begun collecting diversity data, there is a need to further link data analysis results with resource allocation and strategic planning. For example, policies can require each department to regularly report its diversity metrics, with underperforming units required to submit improvement plans.

      Furthermore, cultural change policies are fundamental to transforming the academic environment. Academic institutions can enhance the visibility of Asian American women by establishing research funds for Asian American women, hosting thematic academic conferences, and promoting women's leadership programs. At the same time, institutions should implement family-friendly policies, such as offering flexible working hours, extending parental leave, and providing on-campus childcare services, to help Asian American women scholars balance professional and family responsibilities.

      Mentorship programs and supportive networks play a critical role in reducing unconscious bias and advancing the careers of Asian American women in U.S. academia. Research shows that mentors not only provide concrete guidance in skill development, time management, and coping with research stress, but also play a vital role in psychological support and career planning (Bhatti and Ali, 2021). Meanwhile, professional networks offer Asian American women scholars channels to access academic opportunities and build interdisciplinary collaborations through resource sharing and relationship expansion. These mechanisms not only help Asian American women overcome feelings of isolation and burnout but also enhance their confidence and capacity to compete for senior positions. Therefore, institutions can establish dedicated mentorship programs and professional development networks to provide systematic support for Asian American women scholars, such as offering women's leadership training, mentor matching programs, and organizing academic forums (Lin and Kennette, 2022). These initiatives can not only mitigate the impact of implicit bias but also provide long-term support for the academic growth and career advancement of Asian American women, further promoting a culture of diversity and inclusion within academic institutions.

      Although these policies and measures can enhance the representation of Asian American women in academia in the short term, their implementation still faces numerous challenges. On one hand, policy effectiveness may be limited by resource constraints or insufficient motivation in certain departments; on the other hand, long-term cultural change is inherently complex, and policies alone may not be sufficient to fundamentally shift traditional attitudes and behaviors. Therefore, reducing unconscious bias requires sustained resource investment, rigorous oversight mechanisms, and the collective participation of the entire academic community to drive institutions toward greater fairness and inclusivity.


III

Social Movements


     The development of Asian American women in U.S. academia is constrained by dual biases of race and gender, and through self-organized social movements, they continue to advocate for their rights and push for a fairer academic environment. For example, in the Students for Fair Admissions (SFFA) v. Harvard University and University of North Carolina cases, Asian American communities challenged race-conscious admissions policies through legal means, pushing universities to eliminate race-based considerations in admissions (Supreme Court of the United States, 2022). This policy change has provided Asian American women with fairer educational opportunities and laid a more equitable competitive foundation for the next generation of Asian American women scholars. In addition, scholar Erika Lee's research highlights the complexity and challenges in Asian American identity formation, calling for the elimination of stereotypes about Asian American women through cultural diversity research and public education initiatives—offering important reference for academia to understand the struggles of Asian American women (Sina News, 2019). At the same time, grassroots social movements, such as the creation of supportive community networks, have provided platforms for career development and mutual support among Asian American women scholars, including participation in academic conferences, mentorship programs, and interdisciplinary collaborations. These efforts have significantly enhanced their visibility and voice within academia.

      However, the implementation of these policies and movements also faces numerous challenges. While the elimination of race-conscious admissions policies safeguards educational opportunities for Asian women, it may trigger disputes over the rights of other minority groups, particularly in balancing equity and diversity, which still requires further exploration. Grassroots social movements also suffer from insufficient resource allocation and sustainability, with many initiatives struggling to continue long-term due to lack of funding or public attention. Moreover, Asian women may still encounter limitations from cultural stereotypes in their career development, such as the "model minority" label that positions them as expected performers rather than leadership talents. Therefore, academic institutions should combine grassroots social movements with institutionalized policies by strengthening oversight of diversity policies, providing targeted supportive resources for Asian women, and advocating more actively for cultural inclusivity, ensuring these efforts enhance equity in the short term and promote academia toward a more diverse and inclusive direction in the long term.



3.1 Social Media and Cultural Movements

      Social media and cultural movements have played a significant role in advancing the rights and voices of Asian women in the U.S. academic sphere. These platforms and movements provide a critical channel for Asian women to showcase themselves, express their demands, and mobilize social support. For example, the "#StopAsianHate" movement rapidly spread across social media, with Asian women sharing personal experiences to expose hidden discrimination within academia and the complexity of intersecting gender and racial oppression (Yan, 2021). This movement not only raised public awareness of the barriers Asian women face in professional and academic development but also prompted educational institutions to re-examine their diversity policies. By amplifying the voices of Asian women, these hashtags and campaigns have attracted broader allyship and exerted pressure on academic institutions to improve their internal cultures and policies.

      The impact of cultural movements is also evident in academic publishing and public speaking. Asian women scholars such as Erika Lee have advanced public understanding of Asian American history and contemporary realities through publications and public discussions, thereby increasing the visibility of Asian women in both society and academia. In addition, these cultural movements have fostered the creation of supportive networks, such as online academic communities and women's alliances, which provide professional support and psychological encouragement to Asian women through shared resources and mutual aid.

      Despite their significant role in advancing the rights of Asian women, the limitations of social media and cultural movements cannot be overlooked. First, information dissemination on social media often relies on trending effects, and the attention generated by movements may fade over time, making it difficult to drive sustained policy change. Second, overreliance on online platforms may result in insufficient real-world action, and cultural movements lacking offline support and resources struggle to create lasting impact. Furthermore, public expression on social media may expose participants to additional social and professional pressures; in academia, scholars who openly oppose racial and gender discrimination may face subtle retaliation or isolation. Therefore, future efforts must integrate the promotional power of social media with institutional policy reforms, leveraging online-offline collaboration to drive deeper cultural transformation.



3.2 Building Supportive Communities and Support Networks

      Asian women face unique challenges in the U.S. academic sphere due to the intersection of racial and gender oppression, and combining supportive communities with diversity policies is a crucial strategy for improving this situation (Chun, Lipsitz, and Shin, 2013). By utilizing platforms such as the "Women of Color in Academia" Facebook group (Women of Color in Academia., n.d.) and the Asian American Scholar Forum (AASF) (Asian American Scholar Forum., n.d.), academic institutions can encourage Asian women to build supportive communities to share resources, exchange experiences, and help them cope with professional isolation. At the same time, the National Center for Faculty Development & Diversity (NCFDD)'s "Faculty Success Program" offers practical career development support for Asian women, including time management, writing productivity enhancement, and psychological support. Such mentorship programs not only strengthen scholars' professional confidence but also provide tangible assistance for their academic advancement (NCFDD, n.d.).

      However, the implementation of these measures still faces many limitations and challenges. While supportive communities can alleviate the sense of isolation among Asian women, their effectiveness depends on participants' initiative and resource availability, potentially leading to insufficient coverage and failure to reach all groups. Moreover, mentor-mentee relationships may be difficult to sustain, and some scholars may be unable to benefit from these programs due to resource scarcity. Therefore, although existing measures provide important support for Asian women's development in academia, achieving profound cultural change will require sustained resource investment, rigorous oversight mechanisms, and collective participation from the entire academic community.

Conclusion:

     Addressing the academic survival challenges faced by Asian women requires coordinated institutional reform and cultural paradigm transformation. Academic institutions should reconstruct their evaluation systems by combining quantitative and qualitative assessments, adopting a diversified evaluation framework to counteract the systemic neglect of marginalized groups under traditional assessment models. At the same time, society should foster a diverse and inclusive academic culture that acknowledges and respects differences, for instance by implementing anti-bias training to reduce micro-discrimination. More importantly, Asian women scholars must shift from being the "silent model minority" to becoming advocates for change, empowering themselves and breaking isolation through collective advocacy and the establishment of mutual support networks.
