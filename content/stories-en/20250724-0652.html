[Australia]Are You or Someone You Care About Experiencing Domestic Violence?


1. [Legal Definition]

  • According to Section 4AB of the Family Law Amendment (Family Violence and Other Measures) Act 2011 (Cth), family violence refers to violent, threatening or other behaviour by a person that coerces or controls a family member or causes the family member to be fearful.


2. [Current Situation of Domestic Violence in Australia]

  • In Australia, research data shows that one-third (31.1%) of women have experienced physical and/or sexual violence by someone they know. Two in every five women (39%) have experienced violence since the age of 15; one in every four women (27%) have experienced violence, emotional abuse or economic abuse by an intimate partner since the age of 15. One in every three women (31%) have experienced physical violence since the age of 15.

  • One in five women (22%) have experienced sexual violence since the age of 15. (https://www.ourwatch.org.au/quick-facts/)


3. [Common Types]

  • According to Section 4AB of the Family Law Amendment (Family Violence and Other Measures) Act 2011 (Cth), behaviours that may constitute family violence include (but are not limited to):

    • Assault;

    • Sexual assault or other forms of sexual abuse;

    • Stalking;

    • Repeated belittling or mocking;

    • Intentional damage or destruction of property;

    • Intentional killing or harming of animals;

    • Unreasonably depriving a family member of their rightful financial autonomy;

    • Unreasonably withholding payment of reasonable living expenses or necessary financial support for a family member or their children when the family member is entirely or primarily dependent on the person for economic support;

    • Preventing a family member from establishing or maintaining contact with their family, friends or culture;

    • Illegally depriving a family member, or any family member of the family member, of their liberty.

2. [Personal Protection Orders]

2.1 Introduction

  • In New South Wales, there are two types of AVOs: Apprehended Domestic Violence Orders (ADVOs) and Apprehended Personal Violence Orders (APVOs). Apprehended Domestic Violence Orders (ADVOs) protect people from violence by someone with whom they have a family relationship.


2.2 Eligibility Criteria

New South Wales: Section 16 of the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007 states that a court may issue an Apprehended Domestic Violence Order if, after weighing up the probabilities, the court is satisfied that a person who has or has had a family relationship with another person has reasonable grounds to fear, and actually fears, that:

  1. the other person will commit a domestic violence offence against them, or

    • the other person will engage in conduct that:

    • intimidates them or a person with whom they have a family relationship, or

      • stalks them,

      • and the court considers that such conduct justifies the need for the order.

    • Despite subsection (1), the court does not need to be satisfied that the person for whom the protection order is sought actually fears the commission of such an offence or such conduct, provided that:

  2. the person is a child, or

    • the court considers that the person's general intellectual functioning is significantly below average, or

    • the court considers that:

    • the person has been affected more than once by conduct of the defendant that constitutes a personal violence offence, and

      • there is a reasonable likelihood that the defendant will commit a personal violence offence against the person, and

      • in such circumstances, it is necessary to issue the order to protect the person from further violence, or

      • the court, after weighing up the probabilities, considers that the person has reasonable grounds to fear the commission of a domestic violence offence against them.

    • After weighing various possibilities, the court found that the person had reasonable grounds to fear the commission of domestic violence.


2.3 Application Process

  • Applicants: Section 48(2) of the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007 (NSW) states that the following persons may apply for an Apprehended Violence Order (AVO):

    • The victim: the person for whom the order is to provide protection; or

    • The guardian or legal representative of the victim: the guardian of the person for whom the order is to provide protection; or

    • Police.

  • Regardless of how an AVO application is initiated, they have the same legal effect. The defendant in an AVO application may also file an AVO application against the protected person. This is known as a cross-application.

  • Application procedures:

    • Police apply for a temporary AVO at the scene of domestic violence: A temporary apprehended violence order (AVO) is an interim order applied for by police to protect a person until the court has an opportunity to hear the application. Like all AVOs, the order is only enforceable once the defendant has been served with the application. Section 25 of the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007 (NSW) provides that police may apply for a temporary AVO by telephone to a senior police officer.

    • Direct application by the applicant: Section 52 of the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007 (NSW) allows a protected person to apply for an AVO by submitting an application signed by the Chief Magistrate of the Local Court. Individuals may seek assistance from the local court registry to file a personal AVO application. Some courts arrange for applicants to meet in person with a judicial officer to explain the grounds for the AVO and the orders sought. Other courts may arrange a telephone appointment with a judicial officer to discuss the matter prior to an in-person appointment.


2.4 Legal effects

  • An AVO is a civil order issued by a court. It is not a criminal charge and will not appear on the defendant's criminal record. However, if the defendant continues to engage in prohibited conduct, they may be criminally charged with breaching the AVO.

  • Section 14(1) of the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007 (NSW) states that intentionally breaching a prohibition or restriction in an AVO is a criminal offence, punishable by a maximum of 50 penalty units or imprisonment for 2 years, or both. Section 14(7) provides that a protected person does not commit an offence by assisting, inciting, counselling, or procuring a breach of the order. Under section 14(4), unless the court orders otherwise, a person convicted of breaching an AVO due to violence against another must be sentenced to imprisonment. Section 14(8) requires a police officer who decides not to commence criminal proceedings or not to charge a person with an alleged breach to record the reasons in writing.


2.5 Legal basis

  • Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007 (NSW), Section 5 (definition of family relationship), Section 16 (conditions for applying for an apprehended violence order), Section 17 (court rules for granting apprehended violence orders), Section 48 (applying for a family violence arrest warrant), see

    https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/whole/html/inforce/current/act-2007-080


3. [Shelter]

3.1 Introduction

  • In New South Wales, Australia, referrals to shelters can be made through the domestic violence hotline (1800 656 463). Below are the main shelters in New South Wales:

    • Staying Home Leaving Violence: This program assists women aged 18 and over (and their children) who are separating from a violent partner or family member to safely remain in their own home or another address of their choice, while removing the perpetrator. It also helps the woman find new employment and new schools for her children.

    • Start Safely: The Start Safely program is funded by the NSW Housing department and includes rental subsidies to provide short- to medium-term financial assistance to victims of domestic violence, enabling them to live safely.

    • Housing NSW: New South Wales can provide emergency housing for victims of domestic violence. This may include temporary stays in motels or shelters.


3.2 Eligibility criteria

  • Staying Home Leaving Violence: This program assists women aged 18 and over (and their children) who are separating from a violent partner or family member to safely remain in their own home or another address of their choice, while removing the perpetrator.

  • Start Safely: The Start Safely program is funded by the NSW Housing department and includes rental subsidies to provide short- to medium-term financial assistance to victims of domestic violence, enabling them to live safely. To be eligible, applicants must:

    • have experienced domestic violence;

    • be homeless or at risk of homelessness;

    • meet social housing eligibility criteria;

    • be able to demonstrate the ability to afford private market rent after the subsidy period; and

    • be willing to accept relevant support services.

  • Housing NSW: Victims of domestic violence may be eligible for priority housing through the NSW Housing Authority.


3.3 Application procedures and links

  • Staying Home Leaving Violence: Victims can apply themselves or be referred through local government or relevant non-governmental organisations:

    https://dcj.nsw.gov.au/service-providers/supporting-family-domestic-sexual-violence-services/dfv-programs-funding/staying-home-leaving-violence.html and Justice (nsw.gov.au)

  • Rent Choice Start Safely: Victims generally need to demonstrate in their application that they are homeless or at risk of homelessness, have no assets or have assets so low that they qualify for social housing, and that they can afford private market rent after the subsidy period. Some domestic violence victims may have certain assets but, due to domestic violence or other uncontrollable circumstances, cannot access those assets in the short to medium term, and may still qualify for the subsidy (for example, a domestic violence victim jointly owns a property with the perpetrator, but the Family Court has ordered that the property cannot be sold during ongoing legal proceedings). The Start Safely subsidy is income-tested. Applicants receiving the subsidy must pay their full Commonwealth Rent Assistance (CRA) plus 25% of their remaining income as rent. The balance is paid directly by FACS as a subsidy to the real estate agent or landlord. The Start Safely subsidy is only approved for privately owned rental properties that are affordable. An affordable property means one where rent does not exceed 50% of your income including CRA. The rental subsidy is initially for three months and can be provided for up to 36 months. https://www.facs.nsw.gov.au/housing/factsheets/start-safelyices (nsw.gov.au)

  • Housing NSW: Applicants eligible for priority housing are placed ahead of others on the waiting list who are not on the priority list. If your client currently lives in public housing and has experienced domestic violence, they may apply to the NSW Housing Authority for a transfer to another property, temporary accommodation, or assistance in taking over the existing lease (if their name is not already on the lease).

    https://www.facs.nsw.gov.au/housing/help/applying-assistance/assistance

5. [Criminal Prosecution of the Perpetrator]

5.1 Crimes the perpetrator may be charged with

The Crimes Act 1900 (NSW) (the "Crimes Act") outlines a range of relevant sexual offences, including but not limited to:

  1. Common assault: Section 61 of the Crimes Act provides that intentionally or recklessly assaulting another person, causing the victim to fear immediate unlawful violence or unconsented physical contact, is punishable by a maximum of two years' imprisonment.

  2. Assault occasioning actual bodily harm: Section 59 of the Crimes Act provides that assaulting another person where the victim suffers injury beyond mere transient or trifling harm, or where there is a medium- to long-term impact, carries a maximum penalty of 5 years' imprisonment, or 7 years if committed by a group.

  3. Assault occasioning grievous bodily harm: Sections such as Section 33 of the Crimes Act provide that assaulting another person and causing truly serious injury—including broken bones, serious burns, disability, severe disfigurement, miscarriage or fetal deformity, or any serious disease—carries a maximum penalty of 25 years' imprisonment. 

  4. Sexual assault: Section 61I of the Crimes Act provides that sexual assault occurs when a person engages in sexual intercourse with another person without their consent, knowing that the other person does not consent. The maximum penalty for this offence is 14 years' imprisonment.

  5. Aggravated sexual assault: Section 61J of the Crimes Act provides that aggravated sexual assault occurs when a person engages in sexual intercourse with another person without their consent under "aggravating circumstances." The maximum penalty is 20 years' imprisonment.

  6. Sexual assault by forced self-manipulation: Section 80A provides that this offence occurs when a person, through threats, forces another person to perform self-manipulation—where an object manipulated by the person is inserted into the vagina or anus. The maximum penalty is 14 years' imprisonment, or 20 years if aggravated circumstances apply, same

as:

https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/act-1900-040


5.2 Prosecution

(1) Prosecution standards:

  1. Indictable offences Section 3 of the Criminal Procedure Act 1986 (NSW) defines "indictable offences" as offences that may be prosecuted on indictment, including common law offences

    (https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/act-1986-209#sec.3). In simple terms, these offences are relatively serious, with maximum penalties ranging widely from up to two years to life imprisonment. Indictable offences are divided into two types: those that may be dealt with summarily (i.e., in the Local Court), and those that cannot, known as strictly indictable offences. For the former, if the summary procedure is used and police do not elect to proceed by indictment, the legal process is the same as described above.

    • Indictable offences are generally heard by a judge and jury in the District Court or Supreme Court and prosecuted by the Office of the Director of Public Prosecutions.

  2. Summary offences: As the name suggests, these are less serious offences, with maximum penalties generally not exceeding two years' imprisonment. For more information, refer to the Summary Offences Act 1988 (NSW).

    (Summary Offences Act 1988) https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/act-1988-025

    • Summary offences are the most common type of criminal cases, prosecuted by police prosecutors and heard by magistrates in the Local Court.

(2) Prosecution process:

  1. Police and prosecutors conduct an investigation;

  2. Preliminary assessment/decision to charge;

  3. Commencement of proceedings or filing of charges;

  4. Committal proceedings;

  5. Hearing;

  6. Trial;

  7. Sentencing.

A person deciding to report domestic violence to police will be required to provide a full and detailed statement. This statement forms the basis of the prosecution and is used to support the charges.


6. [Divorce]

6.1 Types of divorce

  • Irretrievable breakdown of marriage

  • Divorce after separation

  • Sole application for divorce


6.2 Grounds/conditions for divorce

Section 48 of Australia's federal Family Law Act (1975) states: the Federal Circuit Court will grant a divorce order where:

  • the marriage has broken down irretrievably (section 48(1)); and

  • the parties have separated and, thereafter, lived separately and apart for a continuous period of not less than 12 months immediately preceding the application for a divorce order (section 48(2) of the Divorce Act); and

  • if there are children of the marriage, appropriate arrangements have been made for the care, welfare and development of those children in all the circumstances (section 55A). Consent of both spouses is not required for divorce. Under section 49(1), the court may make a divorce order if one spouse has informed the other of their intention to separate.


6.3 Divorce Procedure

  • Chapter VI of Australia's federal Family Law Act (1975), see:

    https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00275/latest/text

  • Application requirements: In Australia, you may apply for divorce if you or your spouse meets any of the following criteria:

    • you regard Australia as your home and intend to live in Australia indefinitely, or

    • you are an Australian citizen by birth, descent, or grant of citizenship, or

    • you must usually have lived in Australia for at least 12 months before applying for divorce. You must satisfy the court that you and your spouse have lived separately for at least 12 months and that there is no reasonable likelihood of resuming married life. It is possible to live under the same roof and still be considered separated (this is known as "separation under one roof").

    • If you were married overseas and your foreign marriage is recognized in Australia (under Part VA of the Marriage Act 1961), you must provide the court with a copy of your marriage certificate. If your marriage certificate is not in English, you must upload an English translation of the certificate, together with a sworn affidavit of translation provided by a qualified translator.

  • Application process: Divorce applications must be submitted online using the online form available through the Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia's portal. This allows you to access your court documents within the court's secure website and view court orders at any time. You may prepare the divorce application yourself or engage a lawyer to assist you.

    • Application link:

      https://www.fcfcoa.gov.au/fl/hdi/apply-for-divorce#:~:text=Applications%20for%20divorce%20should%20be,to%20do%20it%20for%20you.


6.4 Child Custody and Maintenance

  • Chapter VII of Australia's federal Family Law Act (1975), see:

     https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00275/latest/text

  • Fundamental principle: In deciding whether to make a particular parenting order in relation to a child, the court must regard the best interests of the child as the paramount consideration.


6.5 Spousal Maintenance

  • Chapter VI of Australia's federal Family Law Act (1975), see:

     https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00275/latest/text

  • In Australia, the court's approval of divorce does not determine your financial, property, maintenance, or child custody arrangements. To resolve these matters, you may:

    • participate in dispute resolution to settle or narrow the issues,

    • reach an agreement with your spouse and file it with the court as a consent order, or create a parenting plan or financial agreement, or

    • if you and your spouse cannot reach an agreement (and time limits may apply), apply to the court for orders.

    • The time limit (statute of limitations) for filing applications with the court regarding most financial matters begins once the divorce is finalized. Except where otherwise agreed with the other party, most financial/property proceedings arising from the breakdown of marriage must be initiated within 12 months of the divorce order becoming effective.

[Australia]Visa Issues


  • Schedule 2 of Australia's federal Migration Regulations states that the following temporary visa holders may still apply for permanent residency if they have experienced family violence after being with their sponsor for more than two years:

    (https://immi.homeaffairs.gov.au/visas/domestic-family-violence-and-your-visa/family-violence-provisions) :

    1. Partner visa category: Subclass 309, 100, 820, 801;

    2. Fiancé(e): Subclass 300 (in specific circumstances);

    3. Dependent child under 18: Subclass 445; and

    4. Distinguished Talent: Subclass 858.

  • If victims of domestic violence do not meet the eligibility criteria for permanent visas under the family violence provisions, other visa options may also be considered.

    (https://immi.homeaffairs.gov.au/visas/getting-a-visa/visa-listing) :

    1. They may qualify as a "refugee" if they have a well-founded fear of persecution due to race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group, or political opinion, and cannot or will not seek protection from their home country. They may also be eligible for complementary protection if they have credible reasons to believe that, as a necessary and foreseeable consequence of being returned from Australia to the receiving country, they would face a real risk of significant harm.

    2. Alternatively, they may be eligible for a Witness Protection (Trafficking) visa, a skilled visa, or a student visa. For further information, please contact the Immigration Advice and Rights Centre.
