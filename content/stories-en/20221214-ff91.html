Introduction

According to United Nations data, women and children are 14 times more likely than men to die in natural disasters. Women are particularly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change, especially because they often bear the social roles of providing water, food, and fuel for their families and caring for others. This inequality is also linked to the injustices they face, including gender-based violence and lack of access to information.


Women are the first to be affected by climate change, and they are also the group with the most knowledge and power to mobilize against it. Unfortunately, current efforts to include women in climate governance remain fragmented and biased, constituting a major barrier to achieving climate justice.


Original source: UN Women UN Women places the fundamental issue of women's equality at the forefront of its priority work areas.


The following article was translated by members of the French team of Avoice's language support team, hoping to raise awareness about the critical importance of indigenous women's participation in climate action.



Part

1

Why must women be at the core of climate action?



Those most affected by climate change today—women, girls, and members of marginalized communities—must be involved in the planning and implementation of climate response actions to equitably share in the benefits.


Because women possess unique knowledge and experience, especially at the grassroots level, their participation in decision-making processes is crucial for effective climate action. A 2019 study showed that increasing the number of women representatives in national legislative bodies leads to stronger climate change policies and reduced emissions. At the grassroots level, women's participation in natural resource management is closely linked to improved resource governance and conservation.


Increasing women's access to productive resources can enhance agricultural productivity and food security, while also reducing carbon dioxide emissions. If all women smallholder farmers had equal access to productive resources, their agricultural output would increase by 20–30%, and 100 to 150 million people would be lifted out of hunger. Increased agricultural yields can alleviate pressure to clear forests for farmland, thereby reducing additional emissions.


* Senka Barudanovic is an ecology professor in the Faculty of Science at the University of Sarajevo, Bosnia and Herzegovina

Photo credit: UN Women/Rena Effendi


In the corporate sector, women's leadership is associated with greater transparency regarding climate impacts. A higher proportion of women on company boards is positively correlated with the disclosure of carbon emissions information.


Effective collective action to address climate change requires a significant transformation in how we produce and measure economic value. Phasing out fossil fuel-based extractive industries and economies can create new employment and skills development opportunities for women. Expanding investment in the healthcare sector is an effective way to highlight collective well-being and strengthen economies without increasing emissions.


Finally, sexual and reproductive health and rights are key to gender equality in climate action. By strengthening healthcare systems to meet current needs for sexual and reproductive health services, countries can safeguard recent progress on climate change in areas such as environmental and biodiversity conservation, adaptation to change, and improvements in health, education, and gender equality.



Part

2

What does it mean

to place gender equality at the heart of climate change solutions?


To place gender equality at the heart of climate change solutions, we must integrate diverse gender perspectives into comprehensive and sustainable policies and programs that reduce climate, environmental, and disaster risks.


Ensuring women and girls' full and equal participation in decision-making processes is a top priority in addressing climate change. Without gender equality today, a sustainable and more equitable future remains out of reach.


Climate change solutions must also include improved investment in gender-sensitive statistics and data to amplify the relationship between gender and climate, ensure land rights are advanced and protected, and promote women-led and women-centered sustainable solutions, particularly indigenous and local nature-based solutions, resource management, and food production activities.


In the páramo ecosystem of the Andes Mountains in Ecuador, local women are using sustainable agricultural production and landscape management to restore ecosystems weakened by years of desertification and overgrazing—phenomena that have rendered large tracts of land barren. The actions taken by women also help eliminate gender bias and empower women to contribute to decision-making processes in their communities. Bibiana Aido, UN Women Representative in Ecuador, stated: "The páramo project shows us that without women, it is impossible to talk about solutions to climate change and sustainable development."


* Norah Magero with Vaccibox, an innovative solar-powered refrigerator made by her organization Drop Access using locally available materials

Photo credit: RAEng/GGImages/Alissa Everetti


Finally, climate change solutions must adopt financing approaches that are gender-equal. Norah Magero, a mechanical engineer and renewable energy expert from Kenya, advocates: "Women-led initiatives must be fully and fairly funded to achieve a more equitable and ecological social transformation."


To ensure a more sustainable future, we need to invest in improving renewable and clean energy technologies and promote women's participation in their development and use. Norah Magero encourages everyone: "Recognize, celebrate, highlight, and encourage the brave women who consistently advocate for climate action that promotes equity, is gender-responsive, and just."



Part

3

In the words of Kamala Thapa: Place indigenous women and our communities at the center of climate action.


Kamala Thapa is a 39-year-old indigenous Magar woman and head of Indigenous Peoples and Local Communities at the Center for Indigenous Peoples Research and Development, a non-governmental organization based in Kathmandu, Nepal.


Kamala Thapa: "If we want to build a just, fair, and sustainable future, we must place indigenous women and our communities at the center of climate action.


Whether they live in the highlands or lowlands, indigenous communities in Nepal have lived sustainably for generations. Our way of life in harmony with nature is rooted in our indigenous cultural and spiritual values and our collective decision-making. For a long time, we have used our traditional knowledge and practices to maintain the biodiversity of Nepal's forests, grasslands, and marine environments.


* Kamala Thapa in Bhaktapur, Nepal

Photo credit: UN Women/Uma Bista


Nevertheless, indigenous women are rarely included in decision-making processes related to sustainable development and climate action. This has negative consequences and serious impacts on indigenous women and indigenous knowledge.


For example, in response to climate change, the government has progressively designated 'protected areas,' which now cover more than a quarter of Nepal's territory. When a 'protected area' is established, indigenous communities are expelled from their ancestral lands. However, because indigenous peoples' livelihoods and cultural survival depend on natural resources, indigenous women continue to return to their lands to gather wild vegetables, firewood, fodder, and medicinal plants. As a result, indigenous women face threats, harassment, and violence from security forces deployed in protected areas.


This example shows that climate change policies and laws that do not center indigenous women and our communities may do more harm than good. The ecosystems that constitute our ancestral lands do not need 'protection'—we have already preserved and maintained them for generations.


Environmental protection policies and laws will be more effective if they promote the use of indigenous knowledge and cultures in addressing climate change. If they fail to do so, they may further marginalize indigenous communities, who have the lowest resource consumption and greenhouse gas emissions.


After decades of activism by indigenous advocates and natural resource experts, we are finally beginning to see progress. My organization has conducted genuine prior consultations with our communities regarding projects affecting our lands. But according to the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, we demand full, free, prior, and informed consent from indigenous communities before any legislative or administrative measures are adopted.


Indigenous peoples are the traditional stewards of the land, and our way of life is founded on preserving biodiversity for future generations. This is a vital component of successful climate action. Indigenous women in Nepal are repositories of knowledge about their lands and environments. Only by recognizing, respecting, and utilizing this knowledge can we collectively achieve climate justice and sustainable development for all. "
