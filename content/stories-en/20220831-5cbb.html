 Introduction 

The original interview article was sourced from the Latin American newspaper *Volcán*. *Volcán* is one of the most influential media outlets in Latin America, primarily publishing articles related to women's issues with the aim of spreading feminism, advancing women's empowerment in the region, and raising public awareness about women's rights protection.


Avoice officially established a partnership with *Volcán* in July 2022 and obtained the newspaper's official translation authorization.


The following article was translated by the Spanish-language team of Avoice's language support team, hoping to help more people understand the values of this minority female leader.


Defender of Women's Rights: Francia Márquez


Today, violations of women's and Black people's rights remain common worldwide, and this phenomenon is particularly severe in underdeveloped regions such as Latin America. On August 7, 2022, the Vice President of Colombia— Francia Márquez (Francia Márquez) was officially sworn in, becoming the first Afro-Colombian woman Vice President in Colombia's history. She is a social leader, feminist, environmental activist, human rights defender, lawyer, and politician in Colombia.


Image source: Francia Márquez's Facebook account


She actively participates in various social, environmental protection, and human rights activities, hoping to change Colombia's current situation through political means. She holds unique perspectives on feminism, women's empowerment, the relationship between humanity and nature, transforming patriarchal political systems, euthanasia, and preventing environmental crises.



"Let us rise, hand in hand, and move forward together."


As Francia Márquez (Francia Márquez) [1]has said many times, becoming president is not her ultimate goal; it is merely a milestone on the path of striving to make Colombia a country that places life and care at the forefront. Márquez refers to Mother Earth as the "Great Womb." She is striving to make Colombia a country that recognizes the "[2]importance of the "Great Womb" and protects it. Additionally, she advocates for the nation to redress the historical debt owed to youth, Black people, women, farmers, diversity, and the oppressed.


[1] Francia Elena Márquez Mina, current Vice President of the Republic of Colombia, was elected on August 7, 2022. She is Colombia's first Afro-Colombian female Vice President. Hereinafter referred to as F.M.

[2] Útero mayor, literally meaning "Great Womb," is actually Márquez's metaphor for Mother Earth. In her election manifesto, she stated: "Soy cuidadora de la casa grande: el útero mayor, la madre tierra." (I am the caretaker of the great house: the Great Womb, Mother Earth.)

Image source: Francia Márquez's Facebook account


39 years ago, Francia Márquez was born in Yolombó, Suárez, Cauca Department, Colombia. She is a human rights defender, women's rights and environmental activist, and in 2018 she was awarded the Goldman Environmental Prize (Premio Medioambiental Goldman)—often referred to as the "Nobel Prize of environmentalism." She has been widely recognized worldwide as "the woman who challenged illegal mining and dam projects in Colombia."


Today, in addition to facing the 2022 presidential election, she has also become a pre-candidate for the political coalition "Historic Pact" (Pacto Histórico) through her "Soy porque somos" (I am because we are) movement. Francia Márquez is a humble woman: although her words are full of wisdom and inspiration, providing fertile ground for nurturing hope, she still embraces the principle of knowing what one knows and acknowledging what one does not know, never pretending to be learned.



01 

*Volcán*: "Soy porque somos" (I am because we are) is the name of your political movement. How would you explain this name to someone unfamiliar with the spirit of Ubuntu?


F.M: I believe Ubuntu is a commitment to life and humanity. This philosophy is not new; it is a philosophy cherished by African-descendant peoples, one that enables us to think collectively, to see all people as one big family, and to understand that if nature does not exist, neither will we, because we are part of nature. I believe it is this philosophy—seeing ourselves as part of a whole, part of nature—that has enabled us, as ordinary people, to successfully resist so much historical oppression and injustice. This is not merely collective thinking, but solidarity among the people. It is precisely this sense of belonging to a large family that guided our ancestors in resisting slavery and leads us, as descendants, to resist colonialism, racism, social exclusion, state violence, armed violence, and the countless forms of structural violence we have experienced.


And as a mother, I feel that "Soy porque somos" is an instinct, a care, a maternal love that begins forming a connection through the umbilical cord from the moment a mother becomes pregnant. To me, this movement means planting the "navel," creating a bond between us and this land. In my view, "Soy porque somos" is a collective concern for life.


Image source: Francia Márquez's Facebook account



02 

*Volcán*: Starting from this collective, people-united philosophy, what is your strategy? How do you plan to run for president in a country like Colombia, which is rife with gender, racial, and class discrimination?


F.M: We are not experts in representative politics, but regarding these strategies, what we want to do is start from the community work we are familiar with, using organizational strength and listening to dissenting voices to build a political system. Today, we want to place these community-based movement methods into politics. I believe it is important to travel across the country and campaign, because if we unite, the places silenced by this politics of death can finally find their voice.[3] This is a challenge for a Black woman, meaning I will be contending with all those who have long held power, with privileged white men. What we can do is build our team from the grassroots, conducting campaign outreach door-to-door, person-to-person, in communities, streets, and suburbs.


[3]  The Black Eagles (Las Aguilas Negras), an armed group, issued three death threats against Francia Márquez, attempting to prevent her from holding campaign events in southwestern Colombia.


We do not have the funds to conduct a presidential campaign across Colombia, because in this country, corruption is widespread, and the powerful gain political benefits by forcing people to suffer hunger, violence, displacement from their homes, and by ignoring their needs. We are not interested in that kind of politics. We want the people—and women in particular—to realize that power is in our hands, and we must take up this responsibility to achieve the change and transformation we need. We must dare to envision possibilities in a world full of impossibilities and negations.



03 

*Volcán*: Traditional political parties, whether right-wing or left-wing, have long used patriarchy as the standard for their actions and behaviors, dominating the political arena. How do they accept women's power in electoral competition? What is this struggle for power with men like within the political arena itself?


F.M: The struggle exists both externally and internally. First, we must ensure that our presence on the political stage is legitimate, because while many people are interested in our existence, many others look down on it. They ignore you, belittle you, and try to tell you where you should be. Even progressive figures have told me: "Francia, don't think about becoming president—run for the Senate instead." But why? What gives you the right to tell me what position I should hold? I am the one who decides which position we will run for, and whether I should occupy it should be decided by society. This is not only sexism, but also racism. Because I am a Black woman, we Black women are always being told where our place is in this society. Now is the time for Black women to stop passively accepting the destinies assigned to us. The moment has come for us—ordinary people, women who constitute society—to decide our own place.


In the past, we women were always relegated to secretarial roles in politics, accompanying men to political events; we stood behind tables to straighten candidates' coats to make them look like good politicians. Now it is time for us to directly refuse male politicians: "I will no longer polish your coat. I stand before you, because I am just as capable as you are of participating in national politics." And the final outcome will be decided by the nation.


Image source: Francia Márquez's Facebook account


In this country, Black people have never received respect from all sectors of society. The world believes they have the authority to command us. It is time for us to reclaim the dignity we deserve as citizens and create a political landscape of racial equality that this country needs. This is exactly what we are doing. Beyond results, politics must align with voters' expectations to place life at the center of all political activity. Everything else is irrelevant. Politics is politicians' speeches and words, their fraud and lies. And the people, having been deceived by politicians so many times, are already tired of it.


I've heard many people say, "We must go to the local areas, we must carry out activities beyond the big cities," "We must go deep into the Colombian people." But I don't need to say "we must go to the local areas," because I already come from a small place. I don't need to "go back" to understand these communities, because I am one of the Colombian local people. Although I dislike the word "go," because it carries a derogatory tone—using this word means denying the value of those local communities, most of whom have suffered exclusion and oppression. Using the phrase "the broad masses of Colombia" sounds as if I previously couldn't see them from a higher position, and only now, because I'm running for office, have I turned my gaze toward them—and then, luckily, received their votes. That's wrong. Power lies with the people. What I do is help these marginalized grassroots communities feel their own strength, help them understand that if the people see themselves as the focus of politics, then change becomes possible, real transformation becomes possible. Only the people can save the people, and the people must make the effort to build an equal political structure for Colombia, because the mestizo privileged class[4]will not come to save us.


[4] Mestizo privileged class: people of mixed white and Indigenous American ancestry.


For women and diverse groups, this will be an opportunity to build our movement from the ground up. We have long been oppressed by socially privileged groups, forced to our knees, and this will be an opportunity for us to rise again and pave our own path.



04 

Volcán: Do you think your platform is a feminist politics?


F.M: Not all women see themselves as feminists, and feminist identity cannot be the sole criterion to judge whether their actions are effective. They are part of this struggle, and they have long been holding up this country. In my community, most women don't see themselves as feminists. Even I have only just begun to understand this concept, and I don't consider myself a feminist either. I simply see myself as a woman fighting for justice, for equality, for dignity, for humanity—including women and LGBT+ communities—and also fighting to protect Earth, the home of humanity. I believe we can expand the possibilities of building our movement by respecting differences. Maintaining differences is crucial, because diversity brings rich and varied cultures to society and can bring real change to the nation. The politics we pursue is different from the patriarchal politics dominated by the white male privileged class, which has long erased differences.


Image source: unsplash


Therefore, we have this strategy of creating a new political landscape together with diverse groups: because it is necessary to bring in the voices of other communities; because we want to live in a world where everyone is equal. I believe this is the meaning of Ubuntu philosophy. It is possible to build a diverse government, a collective government, in this country; and at the same time, we must believe that we can help public officials understand that they are servants of the people, not the masters of the people.


This is not easy, because it means mobilizing the people, encouraging change, and breaking existing political structures—and people find it hard to get used to such an unconventional way of governing. We have operated under traditional political forms for 200 years; change means breaking many long-standing norms and rules. This is a huge challenge. We believe we must give meaning to democracy, because in this country, democracy means nothing to many people.


This is also a challenge for us, because when a woman begins political activity under her own name, people interpret her through the lens of how they see traditional politicians. So how can we make people see that we are different? Unlike traditional politicians, we are building democracy from their opposite and from alternative spaces.



05 

Volcán: How did you encounter feminism?


F.M: I don't know if I've already encountered feminism. Perhaps feminism is calling me. I gave birth to my child at 16 and experienced a lot of violence. I lived with my mother and watched her suffer abuse from her partner. I also personally experienced violence. And some female scholars believe feminism only comes from academia—this idea is shocking.


I was afraid to talk about feminism, afraid to enter feminist circles, because I'm not a feminist. I am a peasant woman from northern Cauca, working alongside other women to help defend our living space. Of course, this is recognized as feminism, but at the time I told Ochy Curiel [5], "I'm not a feminist." Now I understand feminism better, and I've gradually realized that my actions do indeed carry feminist characteristics. Feminist consciousness is woven thread by thread; people need to maintain a mindset of continuous learning toward feminism.


[5]  Rosa Ynés Curiel Pichardo (March 15, 1963, Dominican Republic) is a Dominican activist, feminist theorist in Latin America and the Caribbean, social anthropologist, and singer-songwriter.


That's why I say, I think it's feminism that is calling me, not that I have found feminism. Because I've always been learning and understanding different perspectives. For example, abortion is unthinkable for my family. My grandmother has her story—she says, "How can we have abortions? We must cherish life." So I gave birth to my two children and raised them with this worldview. But I also understand what this means for women. Therefore, I can understand both the idea of women fighting for the collective and the idea of women fighting for life and their bodies. I also agree: if a woman doesn't want to have a child, why force her? Why impose motherhood on her? I keep learning, and I hope to continue learning. Running for office has given me an opportunity to deeply explore this issue at the national level.



06 

Volcán: Regarding the autonomy over our bodies and lives, what is your stance on euthanasia?


F.M: I am certain that if a person decides on euthanasia, it's because they feel their life has no meaning, no value, and they have already suffered too much pain. I believe we should respect that person's decision and allow them to choose euthanasia. I think our laws should take this step. We must bring this issue into public discussion and advance relevant legislation—no one has more authority than the individual themselves to decide whether they want to live.



07 

Volcán: The environmental crisis is imminent, and every country bears great responsibility for environmental protection. What political measures can we take to prevent it, so that when the crisis hits, it's not too late?


F.M: I believe we must change the model of economic development. This is certainly not easy, and it won't become easy just because I'm elected president, but I believe that if people realize society needs to continuously carry out these changes, we can at least bring this issue into national and public discourse. I will consult with the entire nation, just as the people have always consulted on issues like mining, fracking, and oil extraction. Of course, we must respect the people's autonomy in these matters. Economic reform is not easy, because Colombia is a country highly dependent on oil extraction and an "extractivist economy."


Achieving this goal requires very high awareness, so I hope society's collective consciousness can rise: to recognize that humanity is in a dead end, and we must at least transition away from an economy based on extractive energy, to contribute to protecting the Earth. If we cannot reduce greenhouse gas emissions to zero, we have no way out. If we want future generations to continue to survive and thrive, one of our major tasks today is to change consumer culture. This means we must re-examine many existing habits—habits that make people feel happiness through material consumption, but are also destroying our Earth home.


Image source: unsplash


For me, this is why we are taking action: we must change this deadly policy. But realistically, it's not easy, and it won't change just because I become president in the future. But I do have this political vision, and I firmly believe it will be changed—and we are already moving forward.


Another key element is social consensus. And to change social consensus, "people" are indispensable. Everyone has their own responsibilities, and we must take up these responsibilities. Of course, while the United Nations advocates that governments and economic sectors should be the direct bearers of responsibility on this issue, we too bear responsibility, because we have accepted these measures.



08 

"Volcano": How can we break away from the racism, sexism, and classism represented by many individuals who enjoy widespread visibility in the media? And how can we make those who believe "oppression is justified" acknowledge the existence of oppression and realize that in fact they are part of the problem?


F.M: I am not responsible for finding ways to eliminate racism for the racists in this country. I believe that living in a society filled with racial discrimination, xenophobia, and patriarchy, we have already shouldered many burdens. The burden of eliminating racism should no longer fall on those who are discriminated against, but should be shared by the entire society, which must continuously reflect on whether the privileges it grants are justified. Dismantling patriarchy and gender-based violence is not the responsibility of women and sexual minorities alone, but a shared responsibility of all humanity.


For example, being called "Black" does not make me feel offended or disgusted, but the speaker must realize that for me, and for many others, this term carries a heavy history of oppression and violence. Violence not only strips us of our human dignity, but may also cost the lives of many Black people like me, or George Floyd, Anderson Arboleda, and countless young people in this country. In the slums, Black youth are discriminated against simply because of their skin color, based on the belief that such racial characteristics are markers of criminality. Dorothea Roberts once said, "Black people are born guilty," meaning that simply being Black is already seen as a criminal trait. This is why in the slums, it is Black youth who are beaten and abused the most. Of course, all impoverished youth live under violence, but the cruelty directed toward Black youth is especially horrific.


I often wonder about the reasons behind this, for instance, from the perspective of environmental racism, the areas in Colombia with the highest levels of mercury contamination today are often those inhabited by racially marked populations—Black people, impoverished Indigenous people, peasants, and slum dwellers. And no one cares whether these people live or die.


Image source: freepik



09 

"Volcano": What issues interest you the most and move you the most right now?


F.M: There are currently six issues that interest me:


1) Ending the civil war. We have already caused multiple bloodshed incidents; now we must use maternal love and human compassion to stop the war. Men support war because war can serve as a political bargaining chip, helping them win elections and gain power. Women do not need war to maintain our positions at the center of power. To uphold the principle of life above all, we can give up war as a bargaining chip.


2) Addressing the drug issue: Frankly speaking, considering many factors, we must now accelerate the legislative process related to drugs:


a. Gender justice. Women are simultaneously harassed by drug traffickers and anti-drug forces, so to resolve patriarchal violence, we must stop the drug trade;


b. Racial justice. Drug policies based on racial profiling were originally proposed by racial figures in the United States, and this standard has now influenced the entire world. Today, historical reparations for African descendants, Indigenous peoples, and peasants mean ending the war, and ending the war means resolving the drug issue;


c. Economic justice. We must consider how to use plants previously used for illegal purposes in medical and food fields, and create income for these communities. This is exactly what some people are already doing with cannabis. The legalization process of cannabis has begun, but the related trade has not brought economic benefits to Black people, Indigenous peoples, women, and victims. We need to ensure this economic activity reaches those affected by these policies.


3) Environmental issues. We must contribute to addressing the environmental crisis, which means shifting from an extractive model to a more sustainable one, placing life safety at the center. I believe it is necessary to consider distributive economies, women's economies, and diverse popular economies. People are striving to develop these economic forms, but the state does not provide them with strong corporate, financial, or industrial support. The state must offer such support to ensure they have a place and can in turn nourish the entire nation.


4) Historical reparations. We must compensate for the ongoing negative impacts of slavery, racism, and colonialism that many people still suffer from today, and this requires fair distribution. The state must increase social investment in areas that have been marginalized and oppressed.


5) Feminism and diversity. We believe feminist, diverse, and anti-patriarchal policies must be promoted. We always talk about land, yet we continue to discuss it in patriarchal ways. Men often shout slogans like "land revolution," but I think we women must also focus the debate on equal land distribution. A feminist, ecologically protective land reform can care for and guarantee women—the caretakers of life—so that we can have our own land to live well. I come from a rural area, so I often think about the living conditions of rural women, because they are often the most vulnerable group.


6) Prison issues. On this topic, I have learned a great deal from Angela Davis [6]: prison systems in Colombia and many other parts of the world are a continuation of slavery. The lives of those who enter these prisons are considered worthless, because most inmates are poor; the rich do not go to prison—they stay at home as if imprisoned—only the poor, Black people, Indigenous people, peasants, and impoverished women end up in prison. But prisons do not rehabilitate anyone, and the state fails to fulfill its role, so I believe we must seek other methods focused on prevention rather than punishment.


[6] Angela Davis (born January 26, 1944) is an American political activist, scholar, and writer. Davis was a nationally prominent activist and radical figure in the 1960s, a leader in the Communist Party USA and the Black Panther Party. She is now a retired professor at the University of California, Santa Cruz. Her research focuses on feminism, African American studies, critical theory, Marxism, popular music and social consciousness, and the philosophy and history of punishment and prisons.


Image source: Francia Márquez's Facebook account


These policy outlines are the work we are currently engaged in, and realizing them may well take our entire lives. But I believe these are part of the structural issues we face, alongside education. However, as is well known, the coverage of free education is not broad. how many young people from rural areas, or even from the slums of this city, actually have access to free education? I have personally seen children from the frontlines who didn't even finish elementary or secondary school. For them, "learning" means learning how to fill their stomachs, not how to receive an education. We must also pay attention to how knowledge serves life, which means we should reform the education system, changing the current model that serves an uncivilized, predatory neoliberal capitalism.



10 

"Volcano": Perhaps in such a social context, it is difficult to remain optimistic. How should we maintain hope?


F.M: I don't know if I should be the one to persuade people to believe in this country, where: death permeates our daily lives; famine torments young people; women suffer violence; and "dismemberment houses" exist—a little boy goes to steal, and when found again, he has already been brutally killed—this is exactly what happened recently in Tibú.


I don't know if I can tell my people, "You must believe." People have the right to "disbelieve," and this right deserves respect. People have the right not to believe in a society that strips them of their rights, crushes their dreams, until they completely lose hope in it. As for myself, it is hard for me to believe: I once fled this country with my children—how can I convince my children to believe in a country that made us wander and live in insecurity? Because this is an unsafe country that offers nothing to young people.


However, what I want to say is, despite all of this, we can still rebuild amidst pain, rebuild amidst despair, and sow the seeds of hope. As long as we are alive, it is possible to change many things, and these changes are in the hands of each and every one of us.


In the last election, we made wrong decisions, and the consequences have been heartbreaking—we lost many vibrant lives: 84 young people were killed, and many others went missing or were imprisoned; some were forced to move to other regions, thinking they could live in peace, but this was merely our own self-silencing in the face of persecution. Many were compelled to leave the country altogether, as famine continued to worsen. What do we have left? We must rise from the ashes of the charcoal fire and keep moving forward—there is no other choice. Every person, every individual who makes up this society, especially those who have suffered harm and exclusion: let us stand up, hand in hand, and walk forward together.
