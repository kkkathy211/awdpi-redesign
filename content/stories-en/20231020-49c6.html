Introduction:

Recently, several countries and regions have made progress in advancing gender equality. Mexico's Supreme Court ruled to legalize abortion, marking another historic milestone for abortion rights in Latin America; the bankruptcy of Birmingham's local government in the UK highlights the cost of gender discrimination, reminding people to pay attention to equality; World Sexual Health Day reminds us to value personal rights and the importance of consent. However, UN reports also point out that the global momentum toward gender equality remains slow, with large numbers of women still living in violence and poverty. Achieving gender equality remains a long and challenging journey. We need to further promote gender equality both institutionally and culturally.


At the same time, racial discrimination and hate against Asian communities in the United States have not diminished, with anti-Asian hate crimes continuing to occur. An Asian museum in Seattle, USA, was vandalized, and multiple anti-Asian attacks in Portland have repeatedly raised concerns about the safety of Asian communities. This reminds us that vigilance is necessary, and greater communication and understanding among different ethnic groups are needed to overcome prejudice. Local governments and law enforcement agencies must also provide stronger protection so that Asian people can live without fear. To better protect Asian communities, a Central Valley organization in California has launched a new project to track hate incidents, "Stop AAPI Hate" has initiated a campaign to monitor and prevent the use of inflammatory anti-Asian political rhetoric, and Asian voters in Virginia are prioritizing gun control. Against the backdrop of Asian communities calling for equal treatment, American society is gradually increasing its attention to Asians. Initiatives such as designating Lunar New Year as a holiday, recognizing Asian laborers who previously suffered injustice, and Asians attaining leadership roles demonstrate the growing influence of Asian communities in American society. We encourage more positive responses to Asian demands, so that Asians can truly become respected and recognized members of society.



I. Gender Equality

1. "Progress on the Sustainable Development Goals: The Gender Snapshot 2023" Released


On September 7, UN Women and the United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs jointly released the "Progress on the Sustainable Development Goals: The Gender Snapshot 2023," analyzing the current state of gender equality across all 17 Sustainable Development Goals. According to the report, if current trends continue, an estimated 110 million girls and young women will be out of school globally by 2030, and more than 340 million women and girls will live in extreme poverty, nearly a quarter of whom will face moderate or severe food insecurity. The report also shows that no country has been able to eliminate intimate partner violence, and violence against older women is particularly concerning. Globally, the proportion of older women experiencing intimate partner violence ranges from 16.5% to 54.5%.

2. Celebrating World Sexual Health Day


September 4 is World Sexual Health Day. This year's theme is "consent." Consent means "allowing something to happen or agreeing to do something," and is central to any healthy sexual relationship or contact. In 2010, the World Association for Sexual Health began celebrating World Sexual Health Day to raise global awareness of sexual health. Regardless of gender, sexual orientation, or any other factor, educating ourselves on how to give consent, knowing how to express consent, and properly obtaining consent are key elements of positive sexual health and relationships. The United Nations Population Fund published an article on the day stating that this year's focus on "consent" highlights its importance: everyone has the right to make their own choices about their lives and bodies, including whether and when to have children, meaning everyone must have access to comprehensive sexual and reproductive health services and information to realize these rights. The article emphasizes that sexual health rights are often denied, especially for women and girls, sexual minorities, people with disabilities, and those living in poverty or belonging to discriminated groups. In 2021, about 22 million people lived in forced marriages, most of them women. To this day, bride kidnapping and forced marriage persist in many regions worldwide, with many girls forcibly abducted and raped by their future "husbands" and forced to marry their abductors. This phenomenon is most severe in countries such as Ethiopia, Kazakhstan, and Kyrgyzstan, where data shows that 16% to 23% of marriages in Kyrgyzstan are caused by abductions.

3. Mexico's Supreme Court Rules Abortion Legal Nationwide


Latin America sees a major new development in the trend toward legalizing abortion, as Mexico's Supreme Court ruled on September 6, 2023, that the criminalization of abortion in the Federal Penal Code of Mexico is unconstitutional because it "violates the rights of women and people with reproductive capacity to make decisions." This ruling marks a significant victory for the long-standing campaign by Mexican women's rights organization GIRE to legalize abortion. Mexico joins Argentina, Colombia, and other Latin American countries in recent years that have legalized abortion, contrasting with legislative practices in the United States that restrict abortion. This is an important advancement for Mexican feminists and will also help reduce deaths caused by unsafe, illegal abortions. Volker Türk, the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights, welcomed the decision and called for immediate action to implement the ruling. He also urged countries around the world that still criminalize abortion to follow Mexico's example, reform their laws, and ensure safe and legal abortion in line with their obligations under international human rights law.

4.  Birmingham, UK, Bankruptcy: Paying for Discrimination Against Women 11 Years Ago


On September 5, Birmingham City Council in the UK released a statement on its official website announcing that, due to facing severe debt and a fiscal deficit of up to £87 million, the city government would halt all non-essential spending and maintain only basic public services to protect vulnerable groups. This means that Birmingham's local government is effectively bankrupt. Birmingham's economy is actually performing reasonably well; as the UK's second-largest city, Birmingham had a regional GDP of £31.9 billion in 2021, second only to London, and its economic growth rate reached 7.4%, ranking among the top in major UK cities. Birmingham's financial crisis stems more from the local government paying for past mistakes. As early as 2012, courts ruled that Birmingham City Council had a problem of unequal pay for equal work and needed to compensate hundreds of female employees. To date, the city council has paid about £1.1 billion in compensation, but the compensation issue remains unresolved, with related debts continuing to accumulate at a rate of millions to tens of millions of pounds per month. In the Tuesday statement, Birmingham City Council admitted it is now unable to repay these debts.

5. Co-founder of Rolling Stone magazine removed from Rock & Roll Hall of Fame board over discriminatory remarks against Black people and women


Jann Wenner, co-founder of Rolling Stone magazine and the Rock & Roll Hall of Fame, was removed from the Hall of Fame's board after making derogatory remarks about Black and female musicians. Wenner sparked controversy while promoting his new book "The Masters," which features interviews with musicians such as Bob Dylan, John Lennon, and Bono of U2—all white men. During the book promotion, Wenner commented that female and Black musicians lacked depth. The Rock & Roll Hall of Fame subsequently announced Wenner's removal from the board. Wenner immediately apologized, stating that his remarks had hurt Black and female artists.

Prosecution documents show that the man was previously charged in 2013 with assaulting an Asian man, constituting a hate crime. 3. Portland police in the United States classify stabbing of two teenagers at transit station as racist incident


The suspect accused of stabbing two people on September 2 at a MAX station in southeast Portland, Oregon, was arrested on charges of first-degree assault, second-degree assault, two counts of first-degree bias crime, and first-degree robbery. Under Oregon state law, a bias crime is any offense committed against a victim based on the suspect's perception of the victim's race, color, religion, sexual orientation, disability, gender identity, or national origin. The suspect is also suspected of involvement in a convenience store robbery that night. The stabbing occurred around 5:45 p.m. at the TriMet platform at the Southeast Flavel Street Station in the Lents neighborhood. Police said both victims sustained serious injuries but were not in life-threatening condition. One victim was transported to the hospital by ambulance, while the other received treatment at the scene.

4. Hate incident tracking project launched in Stockton, California, United States


According to ABC 10 on September 1, a new project has launched in the Stockton area of California's Central Valley. The website of the 209 Asian and Pacific Islander Coalition (APIC) will track anti-Asian hate incidents in Stockton and the Central Valley. Influenced by efforts across U.S. states to monitor hate incidents, community leaders in Stockton believe a localized approach is needed to collect information on hate events affecting diverse communities in the Central Valley. Inspired by the nonprofit Stop AAPI Hate, the 209 APIC launched the new website to raise awareness of its "Stop Hate" reporting tool. The tool requests demographic information, descriptions of hate incidents, and identifies what resources could help survivors "heal trauma, find closure, and seek justice" after experiencing hate. The organization plans to provide support for hate incident survivors, including mental health services, financial assistance, and self-defense workshops.


The 209 APIC is funded by California's Department of Social Services through its "Stop Hate Program," which aims to curb the rising trend of hate targeting the AAPI community. 209 APIC staff said they hope their ongoing work in the community will help people feel safe using the reporting tool when they need support in response to any violent act, whether it be microaggressions, racial slurs, or discrimination based on one's identity. The 209 APIC plans to use the collected data to help shape policy, identify public education needs, and provide better protection for diverse communities in Stockton and the Central Valley. Information shared through the hate incident reporting tool will be kept confidential.

III. Asian Voices

1. "Stop the Blame" campaign opposes scapegoating of Asians


On September 21, the "Stop the Blame" campaign was launched by the nonprofit Stop AAPI Hate and Chinese for Affirmative Action. The campaign's website includes information on the impacts of racist language and policies. It was initiated amid escalating U.S.-China tensions and in response to discriminatory rhetoric and legislation targeting Asians. As the 2024 U.S. presidential election cycle progresses, the campaign will monitor major debates and election events, publicly condemning candidates who use racist language. The campaign website will also document state-level legislation banning Chinese individuals from purchasing land, as well as state-level anti-discrimination initiatives. The campaign also calls on the U.S. Congress to reform the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA), due to risks of its abuse for racial discrimination.

2. Gun control a key concern for AAPI voters in Virginia, United States


A poll of Asian American and Pacific Islander (AAPI) voters in Virginia, United States, shows that gun control has become a major issue in the November state elections, second only to rising cost of living. Seventy percent of AAPI respondents said they are more likely to support candidates who advocate for universal background checks. Experts analyze that this concern stems from AAPI communities' direct exposure to gun violence threats, as well as their familiarity with strict gun control policies in Asian countries. Experts note that AAPI voters are becoming increasingly important in Virginia, where the Asian population has grown by 112% since 2000; candidates who ignore issues like gun control risk losing AAPI voter support.

3. Thai garment workers once enslaved in a California sweatshop in the U.S. inducted into Labor Hall of Honor


In September, a group of Thai garment workers who helped expose labor abuse by their former employer in 1995 was inducted into the U.S. Department of Labor's Hall of Honor. The case is considered the first recognized modern slavery case in the United States since the abolition of slavery in the Constitution, and led to landmark legal protections for low-wage workers. It serves as a warning to remain vigilant against such abuses. Acting U.S. Secretary of Labor Julie Su said the case reflects the long-standing neglect of workers' needs in the global economy, especially the struggles of women workers; honoring these workers celebrates progress while reminding us that more work remains. "This case became a national model and inspired workers across the United States," said Kent Wong, director of the UCLA Labor Center. "At the heart of this case was a group of mostly female immigrant seamstresses who spoke up and united to fight for justice. Today, we honor their courage, resilience, and the power of collective action." The honored Thai workers said receiving this recognition filled them and their families with pride, marking that the significance of their struggle has finally been acknowledged by history.

4. Lisa Takeuchi Cullen becomes first person of color elected president of Writers Guild of America East


Japanese American writer and producer Lisa Takeuchi Cullen made history by becoming the first person of color elected president of the Writers Guild of America East (WGA East). The announcement was made by WGA East on September 21. Takeuchi Cullen is also the third woman to hold the position, succeeding outgoing president Michael Winship. Upon assuming leadership, she expressed deep commitment to the welfare of all WGA East members. In her official statement, Takeuchi Cullen said: "Even as we negotiate a fair deal with the Hollywood studios, I know this fight is not over. The fight to secure compensation commensurate with the value of our vital work—for all our members, including television writers, film writers, journalists, and podcast creators—continues. The fight for all workers to win a life of dignity continues. The fight for all labor to place human rights above corporate profits continues."

5. New York State in the United States declares Lunar New Year a public school holiday


On September 9 local time, New York Governor Kathy Hochul signed a bill designating Lunar New Year as a holiday for all public schools in New York State. Hochul said the bill reflects recognition of Asian American community culture. In a press release, she said: "This is not just a day off from school, but an opportunity for our children to learn about and celebrate their own or different cultures and traditions." A 2021 report by the Pew Research Center noted that New York is home to the second-largest Asian American population in the United States. Previously, in 2015, then-New York City Mayor Bill de Blasio announced that New York City public schools would begin observing a Lunar New Year holiday starting in 2016.
