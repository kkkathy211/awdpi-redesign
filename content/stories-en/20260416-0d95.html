From the first Athens Olympics to the recently concluded Milan Winter Olympics, the Olympic Games have left a 130-year footprint in human history. These more than one hundred years have witnessed top athletes from around the world striving for the same dream—"higher," "faster," "stronger"—and also witnessed female athletes moving from being excluded from the Olympic arena to breaking records and standing on the gold medal podium..


At the recently concluded Milan-Cortina Winter Olympics, female participation once again set a new historical record for the Winter Olympics. According to event structure calculations, women participated in 53.4%the competition events; in terms of event composition, among 16 disciplines, 12 achieved complete gender balance in athlete numbers.


However, changes in the Olympic gender structure did not happen overnight. Even today, female athletes continue to respond to various doubts and prejudices through relentless effort, rewriting rules with every jump, every slide, and every defiant struggle.


From Beijing to Milan, the number of women's events has reached a new record.



In its early days, the modern Olympic movement almost completely excluded female participation.


For a long time, competitive sports were seen as symbols of strength, speed, and confrontation—qualities traditionally associated with men. In 1896, the first modern Olympics were held in Athens, with all participants being male; women were not only unable to compete, but were even considered unsuitable to watch intense athletic competitions.


However, over the following century, with the development of women's rights movements and changes in societal gender perceptions, women gradually moved from the margins to the center of the Olympic stage.


In 1900, the Paris Olympics allowed women to compete for the first time. At that time, women could only participate in a few events such as tennis and golf, and the number of female participants was extremely small. Over the following decades, although the number of events and participants for women steadily increased, it remained far below that of men.


It was not until the 21st century that the International Olympic Committee (IOC) began explicitly including "gender equality" in the Olympic reform agenda, gradually narrowing the gender gap among athletes by increasing women's events, establishing mixed-gender events, and encouraging countries to send female athletes.


Against this backdrop, the Beijing Winter Olympics achieved a 44.7% female participation rate, setting a new historical high for the Winter Olympics.


Building on the Beijing Winter Olympics, where 10 disciplines achieved full gender equality, the 2026 Milan-Cortina Winter Olympics took another step forward: among all 116 events, 50 were women's events, setting a new historical high in the number of women's events, with an additional 12 mixed-gender events. According to event structure calculations, women participated in 53.4% of the events, a proportion that broke the Winter Olympics' historical record. In terms of event structure, among 16 disciplines, 12 achieved complete gender balance in athlete numbers, a ratio that also set a new Winter Olympics record.


The Milan Winter Olympics also introduced four new women's events for the first time: freestyle skiing dual moguls, women's doubles luge, ski jumping individual large hill, and ski mountaineering sprint, further expanding women's participation in winter sports.


Additionally, in cross-country skiing, male and female athletes competed for the first time on the same-distance course, a change marking another step forward for the Olympic movement toward gender equality at the rule level.


Breaking traditional narratives: sports are about more than competition.


If event design and participation ratios reflect institutional-level gender equality, then the opening ceremony of the Milan Winter Olympics transformed this concept into a more emotionally powerful expression through visual art.


As the first female president in IOC history, Kirsty Coventry, mentioned an African concept in her speech: Ubuntu—"I am because we are." This concept emphasizes interconnectedness and mutual achievement, setting a tone for the entire ceremony that differs from traditional competitive narratives:


sports are not just about competition and winning or losing,

but also about imagining community and care.


At the opening ceremony, Italy's first female astronaut to enter the International Space Station held a little girl's hand while gazing at the stars, symbolizing the intergenerational transmission of women's experiences and dreams; in the torch relay, both the starting and ending points were completed by women, showing that women are no longer bystanders but bearers of "sports glory"; during the fashion segment, female models wore oversized tailored suits from the Italian luxury brand Armani, their sharp lines and confident, composed demeanor visually breaking long-standing narrow perceptions of femininity.


Photo source: Xinhua News Agency


Moreover, the silver down ceremonial gowns worn by the ceremony guides were both functional for warmth and retained the texture and elegance expected of formal attire. This design conveyed a clear message: women do not need to reveal skin to showcase charm; on the cold winter sports field, warmth and sophistication, elegance and strength, can coexist.


Together, these images formed a female narrative: strength does not belong only to intense confrontation, but also exists in gentleness, empathy, and mutual support.


When the Olympic stage—a symbol of competition and victory—tells stories of women and community in this way, the spirit of sport gains new meaning: respect, equality, and mutual connection.


The "Gender Equality Observer" mechanism brings focus back to sport itself.



At the opening ceremony, the female narrative completed the abstract expression of "gender equality," while on the competition field, a series of safeguarding mechanisms turned gender equality from concept into reality.


In recent years, the IOC has continuously emphasized that true equality is not only reflected in the increasing number of women on the field, but also in systemic changes across multiple levels, including sports governance, media representation, and grassroots participation.


This Winter Olympics also explored various aspects in event organization and oversight mechanisms.


In media communication, this Winter Olympics introduced a gender-sensitive "Gender Equality Observer" mechanism to continuously monitor and evaluate event broadcasts and news coverage. For a long time, female athletes in media coverage were often excessively focused on appearance, clothing, or private lives, while their athletic performance was downplayed. The new monitoring mechanism aims to bring the lens and discourse back to the competition itself—strength, technique, speed, and breakthroughs.


At the same time, event organizational structures are also changing. More and more women are entering event management and decision-making systems, from organizing committee staff to senior management, to the large number of volunteers involved in event services—women's presence can be seen throughout every aspect of the Olympics.


More significant changes are happening away from the spotlight.


Host country Italy has long relied on community sports networks to encourage more girls to engage with winter sports. Many local ski clubs offer low-barrier access to children, allowing girls from ordinary families to easily step onto the snowfield and stand on skis. For some children, this may be just a weekend sports experience; but for the sports system, such grassroots support is the soil for female athletes' growth.


The expansion of event offerings, institutional safeguards, and grassroots sports support work together, enabling more and more women to gain access to competitive sports.


They are no longer rare "exceptions," but are gradually becoming a stable and important part of the competition field. Gender equality is no longer just an empty Olympic slogan, but a concrete goal slowly taking root through countless institutional operations and daily interactions.


Breakthroughs, injuries, and regrets: the presence of women on the field.


If institutional reforms created the conditions for women to enter the Olympics, it is still the female athletes standing on the field who truly break records and change Winter Olympic history.


With every jump, every slide, and every defiant struggle, they are gradually shifting the narrative of the Winter Olympics from "women's participation" to "women's leadership.".


In China's snow sports, Xu Mengtao is undoubtedly the most symbolic figure.


Photo source: instagram: taotaoski


From the 2010 Vancouver Games to the 2026 Milan Games, she has never missed a single one of five consecutive Winter Olympics, becoming China's first "five-time Olympian" in snow sports. At the 2014 Sochi Winter Olympics, she won a silver medal in women's aerials; at the 2022 Beijing Winter Olympics, she claimed the gold with a high-difficulty move, achieving China's first-ever Olympic gold medal in this event—a "breakthrough from zero.".


Throughout her career, Xu Mengtao has won over 40 individual World Cup gold medals and completed the rare "Grand Slam" by collecting Olympic, World Championship, and World Cup titles. More movingly, she has undergone four major knee surgeries and has seven steel pins implanted in her legs, yet she still stepped onto the Olympic field for the fifth time at age 36. Her presence has led people to re-examine "resilience" and "time" in competitive sports.


On the ice, Chinese short track speed skater Fan Kexin is the only female athlete from Team China to have competed in four consecutive Winter Olympics. At the 2014 Sochi Winter Olympics, she won a silver medal in the women's 1000m; at the 2022 Beijing Winter Olympics, she claimed the gold medal in the mixed team relay alongside her teammates, securing the first gold medal for the Chinese delegation at those Games.


Fan Kexin's career has also been full of challenges: she was forced to suspend training due to anemia, underwent knee surgery, and even fractured her teeth from clenching them too hard shortly before departing for Milan. Although she ultimately did not stand on the podium in Milan, the 32-year-old reached the final round of the women's 500m for the first time and calmly said: "The feeling of striving is wonderful."


On the international stage, female athletes continue to make history at the Winter Olympics.


Italian short track legend Arianna Fontana made history by competing in a record six consecutive Winter Olympics, winning a medal at each one. Over her career, she has accumulated 13 Olympic medals (3 gold, 6 silver, 4 bronze), becoming the most decorated athlete in short track speed skating history. At the 2026 Milan Winter Olympics, the 36-year-old won silver in the women's 500m on home soil, then led her team to gold in the mixed team relay, adding further brilliance to her legendary career.


American ice hockey star Hilary Knight has also appeared at the Winter Olympics five times. As a core forward and spiritual leader of the U.S. team, she led her team to gold at the 2018 PyeongChang Winter Olympics and has won medals across multiple Olympic Games. At the 2026 Milan Winter Olympics, the 36-year-old equaled the U.S. women's ice hockey record for most Olympic goals (14), continuing to write her own history.


In alpine skiing, Mikaela Shiffrin has become one of the most dominant skiers of the modern era thanks to her remarkable consistency. At just 18, she won gold in the slalom at the 2014 Sochi Winter Olympics, becoming the youngest Olympic champion in the event; at the 2018 PyeongChang Winter Olympics, she added another gold in the giant slalom. Though she faced a downturn at the Beijing Winter Olympics, she quickly rebounded. By the end of 2025, she had secured 104 World Cup victories, becoming the most successful athlete in FIS history in terms of wins.


Equally unforgettable is American snowboard legend Lindsey Jacobellis. Her Olympic journey has been full of drama: at the 2006 Turin Winter Olympics, she lost the gold medal in the final after showboating while leading; for many years afterward, she failed to reach the podium again. It wasn't until the 2022 Beijing Winter Olympics that the 36-year-old completed a redemption 16 years in the making—first winning gold in women's snowboard cross, then adding another gold in the mixed team event with her teammates.


Sometimes, it's not gold medals that change the Winter Olympics.


At the 2026 Milan Winter Olympics, German freestyle skier Muriel Mohr missed the final by just 0.4 points. When the camera turned to her, viewers might have expected a look of disappointment or a regretful explanation, but she simply pulled out her phone, which displayed one sentence: "for every girl with a dream."


Photo source: Yangshipin


In that moment, the Winter Olympics seemed no longer just a stage about medals and rankings. Stories that transcend injury, age, and failure reveal another meaning of sport. As more and more women step onto snow trails, ice rinks, and center stages, the spirit, character, and history of the Winter Olympics are being rewritten.


Long-standing accumulated bias and stark gender disparities in economic rewards



Yet, as we celebrate the growing number of women taking the Winter Olympic stage, we must also recognize that gender equality in the world of sports remains far from complete.


Even at the 2026 Milan Winter Olympics, hailed as the "historically most gender-balanced" Games, there remains one glaring exception—Nordic combined. This traditional event, combining ski jumping and cross-country skiing, still features only men's events and is the only discipline at these Winter Olympics without a women's category.


This seemingly technical event structure actually reflects long-standing gender bias in skiing. Ski jumping has been an official Winter Olympic event since 1924, but women's ski jumping was not included until the 2014 Sochi Winter Olympics—a gap of exactly ninety years.


More poignantly, it was not until the 2026 Milan Winter Olympics that women's ski jumping finally included an individual large hill event comparable to the men's. For a long time, female athletes could only compete in smaller-scale normal hill events with shorter inrun tracks. This institutional disparity directly limited female athletes' competitive space and visibility.


Behind these delayed systems lie stark prejudices.


Gian Franco Kasper, former president of the International Ski Federation, once publicly stated that women are "physiologically unsuited for ski jumping," even going so far as to say he "didn't want to see female bodies carried off the Olympic field in a coffin." Such remarks seem almost absurd today, but they reflect the real ideological barriers women's skiing has long faced.


Beyond disparities in event offerings, unequal economic rewards present another major challenge for female athletes.


At a ski jumping World Cup qualifying event held in Garmisch-Partenkirchen, Germany, in late 2024, the male champion received 3,000 Swiss francs in prize money, while the female champion received only a package of shampoo and towels. This scene quickly sparked widespread discussion in the sports world. In the subsequent official competition, the prize gap remained stark: the male champion earned 13,000 Swiss francs, while the female champion received only 4,300—more than three times less.


Similar cases are not isolated incidents.


At a skiing event in Norway in 2023, the total prize money for the women's category was less than one-fifth of that for the men's; at a cross-country skiing World Cup event in Austria, the female champion once received a sponsorship package that included kitchen cleaning supplies. Event organizers often justify such disparities by citing "lower viewership for women's events," yet they ignore a key fact:


It is precisely the long-term lack of resource investment and limited exposure opportunities that have created this gap in the first place.


Freestyle skier Eileen Gu once recalled that when she joined a professional ski team at age 8, she was the only girl, and it took her three full years to gradually change people's stereotypes about female skiers. She once said: "In today's world, men have more opportunities than women to pursue sports-related careers. Many people believe this is simply because men's muscles are naturally larger and stronger than women's."


In more "male-dominated" disciplines like ski mountaineering, Chinese athlete Tang Zi yi has expressed similar views:


"

"I don't want to be given special treatment just because I'm a woman, nor do I want to miss out on routes I love due to perceived physical inadequacy."

"


Even in the face of ongoing gender inequality, female athletes continue to choose constant departures and relentless forward motion. Because only action can bring hope of breaking down prejudice, and every track left on the field is a powerful response to the status quo.
