INTRODUCTION

Introduction


What if more and more people feel that their lives are constantly "arriving late"?


Careers can be repeatedly chosen, cities can be constantly changed, marriage is no longer a must for everyone, and the once-planned life path gets overturned through repeated trial and error.


Confusion, fluidity, and constant self-reconstruction—this phase is called the "Odyssey Years."


Odysseus meeting the sorceress Circe.

Image source: John William Waterhouse – "Circe Offering the Cup to Ulysses"


In Homer's epic, the hero Odysseus sets sail for home after the fall of Troy. He drifts at sea for decades, only setting foot on Ithaca ten years later. Thus, "Odyssey" has become synonymous with a "long and perilous journey." In 2007, David Brooks, a columnist for The New York Times, published an article titled "The Odyssey Years," first introducing this concept. But perhaps what truly defines this journey is not the wandering itself, but the act of finding direction again after repeatedly veering off course.


Today, many women are experiencing their own "Odyssey." What they face is not just choices about career, city, and self, but also multiple expectations tied to age, marriage, childbearing, family, and the social clock.


We interviewed four women at different stages of life, hoping to re-examine through their experiences—when life has no standard answer, how exactly should one keep moving forward?


DRIFTING

When "drifting" becomes the norm


Image source: Korean drama "When Life Gives You Tangerines" (Pokssak Sogatseuda)


At 30, Cheche is now pursuing a master's degree in Korean language education in South Korea. Since childhood, she has been a classic "J-type" personality—someone who likes to plan life meticulously. During her sophomore year, she took a leave of absence to travel the world. During that time, she moved across different countries, yearning for the courage to discover the truth of life. But at 30, for the first time, she realized that those once-certain life plans suddenly failed—"I don't know what I should do, what I can do, who I am, or who I can become." When confronted with questions from others about her "marriage," "childbearing," and "studies," she admitted feeling "anxious." At one point, she even believed she was nothing, and that all her experiences so far felt like bubbles. She said, "Before, I could list many of my strengths, but after going through the Odyssey Years, I can't quite name them anymore."


 Linyu, on the other hand, once actively pursued society's "standard answers." As a freelance journalist, she believed in her student years that academic performance would earn her a chance to leave her hometown, and that hard work would bring rewards. But after graduation, she realized that the adult world has no standard answers—"If I cling to that model, I'll always feel defeated, so I had to let go." When we discussed her past, she admitted: "Until last month, I hadn't gone home for three years." That was the most recent moment she felt the weight of "drifting" and "losing control."


Drifting may not be a free choice; it is more like a strategy one must adopt under multiple constraints.


Psychologist Bernice Neugarten once proposed the "Social Clock" theory: society silently dictates what a person should accomplish at each age. Graduation, employment, marriage, childbirth, promotion—there seems to be an invisible timetable.


Overview of the social clock. Image source: The Paper


Cheche is constantly reminded, "Returning to China at your age means being considered older, and childbirth will be harder on your body"; when Linyu returns home, she is naturally expected to take on more household labor. Standing at the sink, facing the same exhausting chores her mother's generation endured, the unfair division of domestic work makes her deeply uncomfortable. She bravely says "no," but only triggers fiercer family conflict.


For women, this timetable seems even stricter.


CHOICE

Facing "drifting," their choices


If the Odyssey Years mean life has deviated from the planned course, then the real question is not "how to choose," but what one can rely on to keep moving forward when familiar coordinates gradually lose their meaning.


Image source: Stephen Daldry's film "The Hours"


"Life is like a walking process." During drifting, Cheche feels like a piece of floating wood.


But now she says: "I place great importance on mental fulfillment and richness. If my spirit isn't satisfied, I feel my life withers. So at 30, I'm unmarried, without a house or a job, because I long for the freest, most liberated, and most comfortable state for myself. I've gained many things others my age don't have—more freedom and more time, which I can use to broaden my horizons." In the face of drifting, she has become more certain about her choices.


Another young woman, Ting'er, describes herself as "confused," "anxious," and "timid." A friend once planned to quit their jobs together and travel, but their shared future suddenly changed. The friend chose marriage—"She might return to her hometown, stop working, have children. She's certain, but I'm left facing uncertainty."


Ting'er says: "Once I achieve financial freedom, the first thing I'll do is quit. I want to learn everything I've ever wanted to learn and make my life different." Facing the unknown alone, she chooses to hold onto more hope.


 Linyu, describes her life as "wandering." At work, she feels each article is like a temporary anchor—once reached, she drifts toward the next unknown. She once tried to fit into the workplace, only to realize her identity as a writer was merely a "tool" in her boss's hands. The power dynamics and deep control in the workplace eventually led her to leave.


After quitting and becoming independent, she regained the freedom to write: "I'll spend one or two weeks deeply engaging with a person, entering their life, and then presenting their story. That's what I want to do now." In the face of drifting, she chooses to hold the anchor in her own hands.


Among the interviewees, the only one who has already passed through the "Odyssey Years" and is now at the beginning of age 48, Sister Xiaoyin, also offers her answer.


At 24, she faced a period of intense upheaval. From running a stall to starting her own business, she often couldn't sleep through the night, repeatedly questioning whether this challenge of fate would give her a chance to survive. She says: "I had no way back; many of my choices were forced. I once wanted to go to Beijing, but later compromised because of family. I had some dissatisfaction with my life, doubting whether this was the life I truly wanted. But at the time, I didn't have many choices. Looking back now, perhaps my stability was sometimes forced—marriage, or anything else. It wasn't until around age 35, when one gradually matures, that one begins to reflect on one's marriage."


Sister Xiaoyin says: "When I was young, I had no way back—once I took on a responsibility, I could only move forward. I needed to earn money, survive, support my family."


But now she says: "My past experiences shaped me, whether good or bad. Without the past, I wouldn't be me." In the face of drifting, she chooses reconciliation with her former self, and chooses contemplation as a way to ease the deepest blows of fate.


Perhaps the four women's ways of finding direction in life are not the same. Some confirm themselves through repeated choices, some seek direction through hopes for the future, and others only slowly understand the meaning of their experiences after having lived through them.


When life has no standard answer, they begin to re-examine what truly matters. Like Teacher Cai Lei's words, may we all take them to heart—"Every direction is forward."


Image source: Chen Luyu's Slow Talk (Teacher Cai Lei)


VOYAGE

When we set sail again


On this long Odyssey voyage, people inevitably ask: "Why are we so afraid of falling behind?" The answer may not lie entirely within the individual. Perhaps it's because the social clock implanted anxiety earliest: follow the routine, do certain things at certain ages. It seems we all carry an invisible measuring stick in our minds; once we fall behind the pace, we're seen as "falling behind."


The discipline and constraints of our families of origin, the comparison and competition among peers in society, and opening social media to see only others' "highlight reels"—our own trials and pauses appear especially glaring.


Wanting to escape, we open social media, only to find algorithms have already curated a "perfect life exhibition," pushing the most idealized templates to our eyes: beauty, romance, career advancement, discipline check-ins—each post quietly draws boundaries, then labels lives outside those boundaries as "lacking."


Without noticing, these forces internalize into an invisible yet omnipresent judge, constantly putting us on trial—translating "drifting" into "instability," and "trial and error" into "unreliability."


The real predicament is never the act of losing one's way itself, but rather the social conditioning that forces women to believe that losing their way signifies failure. But life should be steered by you.

Q

 Linyu's question 

Do you think the Odyssey Years will pass, or is this just the norm of life?

 Ting'er answers 

I don't think feeling lost should be the norm in life—it will pass. Because this phase itself is a process of self-exploration and self-identity, it's inevitable to feel confused and unsettled. People won't stay lost forever, though everyone has their own standard for when they've "moved past" it. For me, when I can find a job one day that I truly enjoy, that doesn't stress me out, and that provides steady income, I'll know this period is over.

A

Q

 Ting'er asks: 

During your Odyssey period, have you ever regretted your choices? Do you think things might have turned out better if you'd taken a different path? Have you felt loneliness during this time, and how do you view it?

 Cheche answers: 

I really enjoy being alone, and I've never regretted my choices. Because once I choose a path, I never look back or wonder about the other one—I just keep walking forward on this one. Even if I reach a point where the road ends in darkness, I'll dig out a new path. That's my firm decision—I must make sure there's only one road for me, no alternatives. As long as there are multiple choices, people hesitate, and I don't allow myself to hesitate. Would things have been better if I'd chosen another path? I don't think so. It's all the same. Every path has its thorns—I don't see much difference.


But people inevitably fall into emotional whirlpools. Sometimes I realize I'm caught in one, so I just enjoy it, like lying down and sleeping inside the whirlpool. But I won't stay immersed in any emotion for too long, because I know if I do, I won't be able to climb out. After a brief moment, I find ways to pull myself out—go for a walk, stroll by the seaside, listen to a sad song, shed a tear or two—and then I'm okay. Then I check if there's a new flavor of ice cream to try—taste it—and I'm okay again. I heal myself in my own way: if I feel like eating, I eat something joyful. It's that simple.

A

Q

 Cheche asks: 

In the process of searching for oneself, is the biggest enemy confusion or comparison? Are the choices you're making now truly what you want, or are they just because you don't want to fall behind others? Will the Odyssey period ever end, or is this just the norm of life?

 Sister Xiao Yin answers: 

Based on my experience of having lived half a lifetime, the biggest enemy is comparison.


Once you start constantly comparing yourself to others, you may stop caring about what kind of life you're actually living. That's terrible—you lose yourself, lose your agency, and lose your own understanding of the world. As for whether today's choices are truly authentic, maybe about half of them are. The other half aren't genuine; that half of inauthenticity is actually compromise or sacrifice made for the sake of the other half that is true. You need to understand that in life, even if you strive hard, there are many things you still can't attain. But that doesn't mean fate is invincible. Confusion is a normal part of being human. You're still young—there's no need to rush. Let's encourage each other.
