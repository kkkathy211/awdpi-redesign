Part.1

Overview of Complex Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (CPTSD)

In life, certain events can make us feel bad—such as accidentally breaking a bowl, being barked at by a strange dog, or being scolded by a family member. While these incidents may worsen our mood, most of us can regulate our emotional state independently. Other events, however, can have negative impacts—such as being deceived, humiliated, or harassed—and may even leave psychological trauma. Recalling such events can trigger intense sadness, anger, or frustration, and negatively affect our future lives—such as believing that an entire group of people is untrustworthy, being unable to trust others again, or becoming unusually sensitive to certain situations…


In other words, our connection with the world becomes disrupted. Most connections are related to interpersonal relationships. In addition to events that can cause PTSD (Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder), such as car accidents, plane crashes, or sexual assault, the prolonged presence of many seemingly less severe events can also lead to CPTSD (Complex Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder). CPTSD is a more hidden trauma syndrome, caused by events that are less severe than those causing PTSD, yet it can have even more serious impacts and consequences than PTSD. Traumatic events can be divided into two types. Type II trauma, also known as "complex trauma," was formally introduced by Judith Herman in Trauma and Recovery. This type of trauma is often triggered by chronic, ongoing stressors in daily life, including persistent and repeated abuse and violence, or long-term entrapment in dangerous relationships that one cannot escape—such as enduring prolonged domestic violence, childhood neglect, or being a refugee living in a war-torn country. The characteristics of Type II trauma are: long duration of trauma formation, profound impact on the individual, generally difficult to recover from independently, and complex and varied symptom presentations.



Part.2

Impaired connection and emotional regulation disorders

Experiences of connection with others fulfill our desire to feel alive. Impairment in the ability to connect with the self and others, along with the resulting loss of vitality, is a dimension underlying many psychological and physical problems. Unfortunately, we often fail to recognize that this underlying blockage prevents us from experiencing the connection and vitality we long for.


The Neuro Affective Relational Model (NARM) aims to address these dysregulations, disruptions, and distortions, while continuously supporting the development of healthy connections and sustained vitality. NARM identifies five biologically based core needs, including connection, attunement, trust, autonomy, and love-sexuality, which are essential for our physiological and emotional well-being. When these biologically based core needs are not met, predictable psychological and physiological symptoms arise: self-regulation, self-awareness, and self-esteem are all impaired. As adults, the more our lives are dominated by the five adaptive survival styles, the more disconnected we become from our bodies, the more distorted our sense of identity becomes, and the less able we are to self-regulate. Although we may partially recognize the limitations of these survival styles and their physiological patterns, we fear moving beyond them. When we identify with a survival style, we tend to remain trapped in learned limitations and self-imposed constraints, losing our capacity for connection and vitality.


In short, self-regulation refers to our ability to sleep when tired, and to release stress in healthy ways when tense. Emotional regulation involves how we manage our rich range of emotions: sadness, joy, anger, excitement, challenge, and fear. When we cannot feel our emotions, when we are overwhelmed by them, or when emotions cannot be processed effectively, emotional dysregulation symptoms arise. For our well-being, being able to control the intensity of both positive and negative emotions is crucial. When we cannot manage intense or distressing emotions, or when we feel anxious or depressed, we are in a state of emotional dysregulation. Disrupted sleep or eating patterns, anxiety, panic attacks, compulsive behaviors, depression, and addiction are some common symptoms of emotional dysregulation. Under CPTSD-induced emotional dysregulation, people experience intense, unmanageable pain, which significantly negatively impacts their lives.


Part.3

Three-stage intervention model for CPTSD

In CPTSD intervention, in addition to helping individuals alleviate PTSD symptoms, it is also necessary to help them rebuild a sense of autonomy and control, learn emotional regulation skills, and establish healthy, positive interpersonal patterns. Herman (1992b), based on the definition and symptoms of CPTSD, argues that the core of psychological trauma is the loss of autonomy and the disruption of emotional connections with others. Based on this, she proposes that trauma recovery mainly consists of three stages: establishing safety, remembrance and mourning, and reconnecting with ordinary life, guiding trauma-affected individuals back to a "normal" state through these three stages (Herman, 1992b).


Herman proposed a three-stage model: The first stage is the stabilization phase, primarily focused on helping individuals establish a sense of safety, while training them in relevant skills (for example: emotion identification and expression, interpersonal communication skills). This phase helps individuals gain a sense of safety and control, equipping them with specific skills and self-efficacy to face potential discomfort in later trauma-processing stages, while also making subsequent stages more effective; The second stage is the trauma processing phase, during which work is done on trauma-related memories, helping individuals adapt to traumatic experiences in a safe environment through exposure, narrative techniques, and others, reconstructing their cognition of the trauma and integrating the traumatic experience into their life story; The third stage is the consolidation and transformation phase, in which therapists primarily help individuals consolidate emotional and social skills learned during treatment and apply them to broader real-life situations to handle and respond to new challenges. At the same time, the third stage also serves as a supplementary phase, allowing for screening of other psychological issues that may have been overlooked during CPTSD intervention.



Part.4

Pathways and recommendations for psychological support

When experiencing trauma, if the impact on mind and body is extremely severe—such as leading to depression—individuals may need to visit a psychiatric hospital for diagnosis and treatment, such as taking psychiatric medication or participating in psychotherapy. If, after assessment, symptoms can be alleviated through counseling or similar means, individuals can seek a qualified counselor for consultation to relieve mild to moderate psychological distress. If assessment indicates no need for counseling, individuals can talk to friends, exercise more, or keep an emotional journal (I think… I feel… My emotions are…) to alleviate negative emotions. When the situation exceeds your own capacity to cope, actively seek psychological support (call a psychological hotline, seek help from psychiatrists, psychotherapists, or counselors, etc.).
