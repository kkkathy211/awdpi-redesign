"Domestic violence only happens zero times or infinite times."


This truth is widely known, yet very few people dare to confront and resist domestic violence—and even fewer are willing to speak out about it.


At the same time, in this age of information overload, platforms that allow survivors of domestic violence to speak are rare.


How they face their own situations, how they step out of the shadows, and what mindset they adopt to start a new future—these are things that deserve our attention.


Therefore, we interviewed several women who have experienced domestic violence, attempting to present their real stories and inner worlds through words, while also passing on their courage to others in similar situations.


The protagonist of our story is Ms. Sally, a Chinese woman living in the Netherlands. Educated and self-reliant, she achieved both financial and emotional independence, yet still could not escape the shadow of domestic violence.



It has been nearly two years since her official divorce, but Sally still remembers vividly the image of her son, not yet seven years old, staring at her with bright eyes and quietly asking, "Mom, why does Dad say you're a thief?"


For Sally, of course, this was completely baseless. She had a stable job and a decent income—why would she ever steal from him?


Yet despite never having done such a thing, both her ex-husband and mother-in-law repeatedly twisted the facts, branding her with this false accusation in front of their child.


"At that time, my son was under seven—a crucial age for a child—so I protected him very carefully."


"But after what he (my ex-husband) did, I felt my son became more sensitive and less willing to trust people."


Regarding her ex-husband, Sally told us he had grown up in a divorced family, living only with his mother, and deeply understood the impact of a missing father figure on a child.


He once told Sally he never wanted to be irresponsible like his own father, and instead wanted to always treat his family well and be present in his child's life.


However, when their child was just four years old, he had already abandoned the family, going to Hong Kong to pursue a new relationship.


"In the end, he still became the kind of person he once said he'd never be—that's the irony," Sally said.



She also mentioned that her ex-husband was someone who craved absolute authority and control. To him, maintaining this dominance mattered far more than being a responsible husband or father.


So whenever this status or power was challenged, he would react with extreme actions.


"If he faced setbacks outside—like work stress or low income—he would likely transfer his anger and violent behavior onto his wife."


"If you're someone with stable income and respected at work, why would you still pick fights at home?"


Undoubtedly, Sally was forced to "experience" her ex-husband's violence firsthand.


But after it happened once or twice, she realized she needed to cut her losses and leave immediately. One incident occurred when she was in labor and he hit her on the head; another erupted into domestic violence after he delayed applying for her Chinese visa, causing a heated argument.

  

Sally understood that physical violence inevitably causes psychological trauma, which may prevent a person from bravely confronting or resisting abuse, trapping them in a vicious cycle they cannot escape.


This phenomenon is even more pronounced among women with immigrant backgrounds. She referenced a study from New York, showing that in households where the wife is an immigrant, the rate of domestic violence is 2–3 times higher than among local women..


Immigrant communities are not all as we might imagine—wealthy and living comfortably abroad. Sally, living in the Netherlands, knows: "I believe many immigrants come from less privileged backgrounds."


"This may mean they lack financial resources, educational qualifications, or stable jobs. Their social circle often revolves solely around their husband and children—living entirely for them. They endure domestic violence but are unwilling or unable to leave, sinking deeper and deeper."

For Sally, women with similar experiences are easier to connect with. She shared the story of a female friend, also a Chinese woman in the Netherlands, whose ex-husband was Dutch.


She was a full-time homemaker—though not highly educated, she took excellent care of her child and husband.


Even so, she could not escape the fate of domestic violence.


She was beaten until her body was covered in bruises, and her husband forbade her from leaving the Netherlands, hiding her passport and confiscating her phone, effectively imprisoning her at home.


For years, she endured the abuse and suffering for the sake of her child, as the child was her entire life.


When the child was two or three, she could no longer bear the violence and wanted to leave.


But due to language barriers, she couldn't seek timely and effective help, and ultimately escaped only with the assistance of Chinese and child protection organizations.


Because of financial hardship, educational limitations, and language barriers, after being verbally torn apart by her ex-husband—accused of even drinking water incorrectly—and under constant pressure from child protection services, she was forced to give up primary custody of her child.


Trapped in the vortex of domestic violence, her body and mind already shattered, she was like a drowning person—powerless to resist or fight back—ending up with nothing.


This friend's experience is precisely the reality for countless overseas Chinese women suffering domestic violence.


Therefore, Sally firmly believes: "When you want to break free from this vicious cycle, you must leave without hesitation—even if it comes at a cost."


After being lost in darkness for too long, one loses courage and hope, so she had to go in search of that ray of light.


Thus, despite the many unknown difficulties ahead, she resolutely embarked on the long road of seeking justice.



The first obstacle on this journey was her ex-husband and mother-in-law. Because they constantly provoked conflict, Sally had no choice but to resort to legal action and communicate through lawyers.


In 2017, Sally filed for divorce, but it took nearly two years from filing to finalization. Whether during or after the divorce process, her ex-husband and mother-in-law continuously harassed her with text messages and verbal abuse.


No matter how she explained or presented evidence, "he would always twist it to make me feel like I was the one at fault," she recalled.


Her ex-husband distorted and fabricated facts, defaming her in front of their young son and falsely accusing her in court. Her mother-in-law even sent WeChat messages to Sally's mother in China, inventing baseless incidents.


The most serious incident occurred when Sally planned to take her child to visit Shanghai. She had fulfilled her duty to inform the child's father, who initially agreed.


But on the day she and her son were about to depart, they were stopped at the airport by border police, told she was suspected of child abduction.


It turned out her ex-husband had called the police, fabricating a story that Sally intended to kidnap their son and take him to China.


"By provoking me like this and constantly obstructing my ability to live well after divorce, I feel this is a form of deep discrimination against women."


In the Netherlands, even after divorce, as long as one parent does not use drugs or commit crimes, they retain custody rights—unlike in China, where custody is often awarded solely to one parent.


Sally holds primary custody, while her ex-husband has visitation rights, seeing the child every two weeks. Due to joint custody, major decisions in the child's life—such as changing schools or international travel—require mutual agreement from both parents.


This means Sally must continue to interact with her ex-husband after divorce for the sake of the child. He uses every possible excuse to create conflict, yet despite his behavior, Sally must maintain a surface-level peace for the child's sake.


"A child is a mother's greatest vulnerability—there is no other. So he can always use the child to hurt you."


"If I didn't have a child, everything would be much simpler. I could face him with a much stronger attitude."


In resisting domestic violence, it is because of her child that she has this one gentle hesitation and compromise.

In addition, another intractable challenge is that Dutch society has a natural sense of exclusion toward "colored persons," much like how big cities treat outsiders.


As a Chinese woman in the Netherlands, Sally knows that educated Dutch people generally view Chinese as hardworking and intelligent, and they respect such immigrants.


However, things cannot be generalized—you might encounter people with no prejudice at all, but you might also meet those who judge by skin color, seeing some as superior and others as inferior.


Even Sally, who reminds us to "analyze each case specifically," sighs: "As long as you're connected to the Chinese community, as long as your skin is colored, you will inevitably face some degree of discrimination."


Therefore, discrimination is unavoidable during the process of seeking justice.


The Dutch have a stereotype toward "foreigners," and taking legal action only makes them think, "What are you making a fuss about? Can't you just stay quiet?"


Despite being fluent in English, Sally faced discrimination from her first lawyer (of Indian descent), who treated her with impatience and arrogance in the name of "white superiority," offering little substantive help.


This forced Sally to switch to another lawyer who could communicate in English. However, such externally hired lawyers charge very high fees—nearly 250 euros per hour (approximately 1,981 RMB per hour).


"Fortunately, my income can cover these costs. If I didn't have this job, I'd have no choice but to rely on the first lawyer, and the consequences are unimaginable."


At the same time, communication with child protection services was also difficult. Knowing Sally was a Chinese immigrant, they treated her differently. "Sometimes the things they say are really harsh," Sally told us personally. "For example, no matter how many years the father has been gone, since you're an immigrant, you can't help your child integrate into Dutch society. You two can fight all you want, but you can't deprive the child's father of his rights."


In court, the situation was similar. "The judge reads the documents, doesn't verify truth or falsehood, meets you for just an hour, and then makes a judgment about you. Of course, having an East Asian face and not speaking fluent Dutch undoubtedly counts against you."


At this point, Sally fell silent for a moment, then said: "At times like this, I wonder—what exactly did I do wrong? Just because I'm not Dutch, does that mean I'm wrong?"



Throughout her journey of "speaking out," as a Chinese woman survivor of domestic violence, being in a vulnerable position, Sally faced various accusations and misunderstandings, but she never chose to give up.


Because she knew that being part of the "silent majority" would only sink her deeper into the quagmire of domestic violence.


"Even if this struggle might last for years, don't give up. Stay strong, don't be defeated by difficulties or discrimination. Don't dwell too much on the past—look toward the future."


During those difficult days, Sally reminded herself of this countless times. Now, it has become her most sincere encouragement to women facing similar situations.


In the Netherlands, the climate is mild year-round, pleasant for living. Moist, fresh monsoon winds blow in from the sea, and things are moving in a better direction.


Sally not only won custody of her eldest son, but the current judge is also putting increasing pressure on her ex-husband. She told us her current husband is a good man who supports and helps her in fighting for her rights, and her younger son is growing up healthily.


During the interview, Sally mentioned that her younger son was sleeping, and if he woke up crying, she would need to step away from the computer to care for him.


Even through the screen, we could sense that compared to the hardships of the past, her life now is filled with much more peace and warmth.


"Actually, I've been fighting for my rights for nearly one or two years, all on my own, figuring things out as I went. When I first went to court, I was trembling all over, because I had no idea what kind of situation I was walking into." "


Recalling the time when she fought alone, she still feels shaken: "I was just enduring everything by myself—truly, it could drive a person to illness. So you must find companions, find an organization—don't endure everything alone. "


If I had known back then that an organization like this existed, I definitely would have sought help."


Fortunately, we met Sally, and she was willing to share her story with us.

In fact, Sally does not fit the image of a so-called "typical victim."


Because in a society where domestic violence is often oversimplified, many people still equate it only with physical violence, and assume victims are mostly women from lower socioeconomic backgrounds.


So we often treat domestic violence survivors as a small, disadvantaged group, lacking sufficient empathy and attention. Like some of Sally's friends, who attributed her experience of domestic violence to "poor judgment in choosing men."


"Even the most successful woman can stumble when it comes to choosing a partner. Because no man is going to tell you during dating, 'Sorry, I'm abusive,'" Sally said.


People who have never experienced domestic violence, in a sense, are less like "ordinary people" and more like "survivors."


Being unaware of the pain of domestic violence is itself a privilege, and one should never casually make insensitive remarks.


Intimate relationships are inherently uncertain—you must never fully depend on them. Before it turns into a time bomb, you must build up the resources to walk away.


So Sally tells us: "Never underestimate the importance of education and employment. No matter what, always maintain the awareness and ability to be independent and strong."


Finally, when asked, "What would you like to say to women in similar situations?" her tone was sincere and firm:


"If you're a Chinese woman being beaten at home, you must stand up. Don't let your own weakness reinforce the stereotype that Chinese women are weak. We are a community—this only makes fighting for our rights even harder."


Resisting domestic violence has never been an individual's responsibility alone—it requires collective struggle.


Sally was willing to openly share these scarred memories with us, to encourage more women in similar situations to stand up and speak for themselves.


If domestic violence is treated as a private family matter and resolved by sweeping it under the rug, it will only lead to longer and more severe violence.


This will only worsen your relationship with your ex-husband, and both you and your children will suffer greater harm over time.


"You absolutely must stand up. Never, ever give up fighting for your rights and speaking out."


Even if the wounds left by domestic violence can never be completely erased, even if stories shadowed by trauma rarely reach a "happy ending,"


What we can do now is take action to stop the loss in time and work to minimize the harm.

  

"This world should have no victims—only when the weak become strong will others hold back. This world should have no gods—only when you have your own voice and strength will you no longer be afraid!"  


These words carry the gentle yet powerful strength of women, filled with belief and hope for a better future for women, and are recorded here today.


Don't be afraid to speak about and express your experience of domestic violence—bravely and promptly end this vicious cycle.


Every time someone speaks out, it may only be a fleeting glimmer in the cold night, but it will always bring warmth to those who are lost.


One more voice means one more source of gentle yet powerful strength—women supporting each other, becoming light for one another.


Though she has lived through her darkest moments, she has ultimately found that light that belongs to her.

At the same time, Sally has become a volunteer with Avoice, offering one-on-one counseling in our online Chinese women's community and sharing her experience and energy with other Chinese women in need.
