 Introduction:


The Chinese community is a significant group within Malaysian society, and paying attention to the living conditions and legitimate rights and interests of Chinese Malaysian women is of great importance. This report focuses on the current situation of domestic violence against Chinese Malaysian women, aims to popularize legal and policy knowledge for Chinese Malaysian women, and provides self-help methods as well as pathways to seek social assistance.

I

Chinese Malaysian Women and Domestic Violence

Situation Analysis and Victim Profile



According to data from the World Health Organization, 35% of women worldwide have experienced some form of domestic violence by an intimate partner during their lifetime. A survey on Malaysia found that 9% of married women in the country have experienced different types of domestic violence (WTO, 2013). In 2020, Malaysia's total population was approximately 32.45 million, of which 29.8 million were Malaysian citizens, and ethnic Chinese Malaysians accounted for 23.2% (Xinhua News Agency, 2022). Ethnic Chinese women are one of the groups that cannot be overlooked when discussing domestic violence in Malaysian society. Like their counterparts in Singapore, Indonesia, Thailand, and Vietnam, most Malaysian Chinese originate from Fujian Province, having migrated to Malaysia during waves of immigration and settled there. Similar to the social status of Chinese Indonesians, Malaysian Chinese are also well known for their business acumen.


weforum.org


According to the Global Gender Gap Report 2021 released by the World Economic Forum (WEF), Malaysia ranked 112th out of 156 countries in terms of gender equality index (2021). As a country where over 60% of the population practices Islam, victims' perceptions of domestic violence are also influenced by religion. Muslim women's discourse on submission to fate, and Buddhist references to karma and retribution, are religious factors that lead them to tolerate domestic violence (Rahman & Ismail, 2019). In addition, reports by relevant organizations on domestic violence in Malaysian society point out: "Malaysians tend to underestimate the complexity of abuse, with 37.1% of the surveyed population believing that leaving an abusive relationship is not difficult, and 44.9% believing that women who stay with abusers are also responsible for the ongoing abuse." (LOHESWAR, 2021). Research examining the causes of domestic violence in Malaysia from a family perspective has found that older men and families with more children are more likely to experience domestic violence. (Awang & Hariharan, 2011).


During the outbreak of the coronavirus, the overall incidence of domestic violence in Malaysia did not decrease but increased. Between January and December 2020, a total of 2,287 domestic violence cases were recorded, and after the Malaysian government implemented the Movement Control Order (MCO) on March 18, 2020, the number of domestic violence incidents clearly increased (KPWKM, 2020). Due to restricted movement and social isolation, abused women were forced to stay under the same roof as their abusers. Coupled with limited personal capacity, they are often economically dependent on their families and abusers, enduring both physical and psychological suffering during the pandemic lockdown.


A study on intimate partner violence (IPV) among Malaysian women indicated that, compared to local Malaysians, Chinese Malaysian women were less likely to have experienced IPV in the past year (Chan et al., 2019), which may stem from cultural values in Chinese families that emphasize protecting family reputation (Chan, 2006). In fact, domestic violence is only one aspect of Malaysia's broader social agenda to promote gender equality. Child marriage and gender inequality in employment remain urgent issues that need to be addressed to improve women's social well-being. Under the framework of the Belt and Road Initiative, the Chinese government actively promotes the concept of a community with a shared future for humanity, encouraging emerging countries to adopt Belt and Road international human rights standards and gender equality policies. In addition, China has pledged a donation of 10 million USD to UN Women to support the implementation of the Beijing Declaration and Platform for Action and the achievement of the UN Sustainable Development Goals (Phnom Penh Post, 2022). As a Belt and Road ASEAN partner country, China, while fostering friendly economic cooperation with Malaysia, is also creating more employment opportunities for local women, further promoting social gender equality.



II.

Domestic Violence in Malaysia Related

Legal Provisions and Assistance Policies



Malaysia was the first country in Asia to legislate protection for victims of domestic violence. The Malaysian Domestic Violence Act 1994 was revised and published on February 9, 2012, further revised and gazetted on September 21, 2017, and officially implemented on January 1, 2018. The new amendments to the Domestic Violence Act incorporated many key issues, such as an expanded definition of domestic violence, allowing the Act to operate alongside the Penal Code or any written law dealing with offenses related to domestic violence, and enabling the issuance of interim protection orders through applications to the court (Siraj, 1994).


Prior to the amendment of the Domestic Violence Act 1994, Section 2 of the Act defined domestic violence as committing any of the following acts:

  1. attempting to cause the victim to fear bodily harm,

  2. causing bodily harm,

  3. using force or threats to compel sexual acts,

  4. detaining or confining the victim against their will,

  5. intentionally damaging or destroying the victim's property to cause distress or suffering,

(Section 2 of DVA 1994; Rahman & Ismail, 2019).


The scope of domestic violence as defined by the law primarily includes physical harm, such as threats of harm, causing bodily injury, forced sexual acts, restricting or detaining the victim, and property damage, among others.


After the amendment of the Domestic Violence Act 1994, its definition of domestic violence was expanded to include psychological, emotional abuse, and economic loss (Siraj, 1994). According to Section 2 of the Domestic Violence Act, domestic violence includes:

  1. misappropriating the victim's property, causing the victim economic loss;

  2. intending to threaten the victim, causing them to fear for their own, their property's, or a third party's safety, resulting in psychological suffering;

  3. communicating with the victim through any means, or communicating information about the victim to a third party, with the intention to humiliate the victim;

  4. causing psychological abuse, including mental harm to the victim;

  5. using any intoxicating substance or other substance without the victim's consent, or with illegally obtained consent, to cause the victim to hallucinate;

  6. in cases where the victim is a child, using any hallucinogenic substance or other substance to cause the victim to hallucinate.


These amendments to the Act have broadened the definition of domestic violence to include certain forms of psychological, emotional abuse, and economic loss. However, the definition does not recognize broader forms of psychological/emotional abuse, verbal abuse, and economic abuse as acts of domestic violence. (Rahman, 2019).



Under the Domestic Violence Act 1994, victims may file complaints with the police or officers, bring criminal charges against their husbands, and seek protection from the court. In addition, the Act also provides victims with the right to claim compensation for injuries caused by violence, and the right to receive counseling to promote reconciliation and rehabilitation, thereby enhancing legal protection for the family.


The Domestic Violence Act 1994 also provides for Long-Term Protection Orders (LPO) and Interim Protection Orders (IPO), aimed at protecting the safety of victims. Victims may apply for an Interim Protection Order during police investigation of a domestic violence case (Section 4, DVA 1994). In 2012, the amendment allowed protection orders to establish and maintain a specified safe distance between the victim and the abuser, including restricting the offender from entering shelters (Section 6, DVA 1994). Anyone who intentionally violates a protection order and uses violence against the protected person may be sentenced to imprisonment for up to one year and fined up to 4,000 Malaysian Ringgit (Section 8(2) of the Domestic Violence Act 1994). Malaysian laws related to the prevention of domestic violence also stipulate that police, when investigating and gathering evidence on domestic violence based on relevant information, may apply for an Interim Protection Order on behalf of the victim (Fan Yu, 2017). In addition to applying to the court for an Interim Protection Order, victims may also apply to the local Social Welfare Department for an Emergency Protection Order (EPO)..


The Domestic Violence Act 1994 also provides that victims have the right to compensation for property damage or other losses caused by violent acts (Section 10 of the Women's Rights Act 1994). Before deciding on compensation, the court may consider the following:

  1. the victim's pain and suffering;

  2. medical expenses;

  3. any loss of income;

  4. the value of damaged property;

  5. any other relevant costs.


To protect victims, the court may refer them to the Ministry of Social Welfare, or, if the victim is a Muslim, to the Islamic Religious Department, for counseling services. In addition to the remedies provided under the Domestic Violence Act 1994, victims may also find remedies under several other written laws. For example, offenders may be prosecuted under any offense against the person in the Penal Code. This may include causing hurt or grievous hurt, criminal force, assault, or even murder. Although Malaysian law does not recognize marital rape, victims may invoke Section 375A of the Penal Code, which states that a husband who causes fear of death or hurt to his wife for the purpose of sexual intercourse commits an offense. If convicted, he may be imprisoned for up to five years. This provides additional protection for victims of domestic violence (Rahman, 2019).


Although the Domestic Violence Act 1994 has been amended, it still contains loopholes. (Rahman, 2019):

  1. Under this law, domestic violence is not recognized as a specific criminal offense, but rather defined as a series of abusive acts involving threats of harm, causing harm, use of force, or detention against a spouse, former spouse, child, incapacitated adult, or other family member. According to the 1994 DVA, in order for abusive behavior to be classified as a criminal offense, the victim must invoke provisions in the Penal Code related to crimes against personal safety. However, offenses defined under the Penal Code fail to reflect the serious, ongoing, and frequently repetitive nature of domestic violence.

  2. The definition of domestic violence urgently needs improvement. For example, stalking and intimidation—common forms of domestic violence—are not included in the definition of domestic violence under the Domestic Violence Act 1994. (2013). Although the Act includes sexual and psychological abuse within its definition, the Domestic Violence Act 1994, when implemented in law enforcement, still focuses more on physical harm rather than non-physical forms of violence, and police arrest powers remain based on bodily injury caused by the perpetrator. Section 7(1) of the Act states, "If the court is satisfied that the person against whom a protection order or interim protection order is sought is likely to cause actual bodily harm to the protected person, the court may, as it deems fit, attach a power of arrest to that protection order." This indicates that courts primarily consider evidence of physical harm. Therefore, victims suffering from non-physical abuse still find it difficult to prove the perpetrator's offense.

  3. The Domestic Violence Act 1994 does not recognize domestic violence occurring between unmarried couples or in dating relationships. This is because Malaysia does not legally recognize any intimate relationship outside of marriage. Therefore, if unmarried couples report cases of domestic violence, they will be handled under Chapter 15 of the Penal Code as criminal acts involving violence, assault, or causing bodily harm. In reality, there are many unmarried cohabiting couples in intimate relationships in Malaysia who are not protected by this law (Kumaralingan Amirthalingam, 2003).

  4. Section 11(1) of the Domestic Violence Act 1994 authorizes courts to order the referral of victims and perpetrators to reconciliation agencies. However, orders requiring victims to attempt reconciliation with the offender may very likely place the victim in danger.. (2015).



III.

Malaysian Chinese Women Self-Help Measures Against Domestic Violence



Based on available literature, support policies, and legal provisions related to domestic violence in Malaysia, this report provides the following possible self-help measures, ordered chronologically, for victims to adopt.


1

 Seek Assistance 


  1. Call Malaysia's emergency hotline 999 or 112 and report the incident to the police promptly.

  2. File a request with the court for an interim protection order together with the police (if the interim protection order is not successfully granted, apply for an Emergency Protection Order (EPO) through the local Social Welfare Department).

  3. Call the Malaysian Chinese Association Legal Advisory and Women's Aid Center hotline. 603-2716 5955

The Malaysian Chinese Association Women's Wing provides assistance to Chinese Malaysian women who are victims of domestic violence, including legal consultation, counseling on family, marriage, and youth issues, helping victims apply for Emergency Protection Orders (EPO) from the Social Welfare Department, applying for Interim Protection Orders (IPO) from the court, arranging shelter, obtaining aid from the Social Welfare Department, and other consultations, as well as providing psychological counseling for victims.



2

 Evidence Collection 


Evidence of domestic violence plays a crucial role in proving the occurrence or imminent danger of abuse and can strongly support a victim seeking legal aid after experiencing violence. Collecting and preserving evidence immediately after an incident of domestic violence can maximize the integrity and effectiveness of the evidence, increasing the likelihood that women will receive the legal assistance they deserve. Given that Malaysia's legal provisions on domestic violence do not provide a sufficiently clear definition and do not include some common forms of domestic violence—such as broader psychological/emotional abuse, verbal abuse, and economic abuse—the evidence collection methods listed below aim to help victims increase their chances of success when using the law to protect their rights.


Evidence collection may include:

  1. Police reports, police interview records, and injury assessment results.

  2. Audio-visual materials of domestic violence incidents, including recordings or videos of physical harm inflicted on the victim, forced sexual acts, unlawful confinement, or intentional property destruction; photographs or videos of bodily injuries and damaged scenes; recordings or videos of threats to the victim or a third party (such as family members or children) involving threats to life safety or verbal violence.

  3. During a domestic violence incident, victims should, without escalating the abuse or endangering their lives, try to record the entire process via audio or video, and immediately after the incident, when safe, use photos or videos to document the scene and their injuries.

  4. Medical records or photos, proof of physical and psychological injuries, medical expense receipts, proof of income loss and property damage.

  5. The Domestic Violence Act 1994 also stipulates that victims have the right to compensation for property damage or other losses caused by violent acts. When awarding compensation, the court will consider the victim's suffering and injuries, medical expenses, any loss of income, the value of damaged property, and any other related costs.

  6. Witness testimonies, statements from minor children, and records of the victim's own statements; request neighbors, colleagues, or minor children who witnessed or heard the domestic violence to testify in court.

  7. Any audio recordings or written records by the abuser admitting to the act of domestic violence.



3

 Physical and Psychological Trauma Treatment


After domestic violence occurs, victims should promptly seek medical treatment for both physical and psychological injuries to avoid irreversible harm due to delayed care. If victims are unable to access medical or psychological counseling services due to physical or financial constraints, they may contact local Malaysian Chinese Association Women's Rescue Centers or other women's domestic violence aid organizations for assistance (see section below on domestic violence support organizations in Malaysia).

Tenaganita (Women's Force) 



Tenaganita.net


Background:

Tenaganita was established in 1991. As a human rights organization born from the struggles of Malaysian women and migrant workers, it is committed to helping, building, advocating for, and protecting migrants, refugees, women, and children from exploitation, abuse, discrimination, slavery, and human trafficking. It promotes and protects the rights of marginalized and vulnerable groups who have no voice in this globalized world.


Mission:

To seek and strive for the promotion and protection of the rights and dignity of all women, migrants, and refugees, while collectively creating spaces for their full potential and liberation in a globalized world.


Service Areas:

  1. Shelters; currently operating shelters in Selangor and Penang, providing temporary accommodation, food, skills training, and enrichment activities. It also offers legal support, psychological intervention, restoration of rights, and empowerment for shelter residents.

  2. Tanma Community; provides a space for refugee women's groups to communicate, enhance skills, and empower themselves. Through formal and informal "peer support groups," it conducts training on gender-based violence and exercises in women's leadership, creating safe spaces and support for women who may experience violence.

Women's Aid Organisation (WAO) 



wao.org.my


Background:

The Women's Aid Organisation (WAO) was established in 1982. It is a non-profit, public welfare organization advocating for women's rights, particularly against violence towards women. It is committed to ending violence against women and promoting gender equality in Malaysia, while focusing on raising awareness and changing mindsets to end violence against women and building a society that respects women's rights and dignity.


Mission:

WAO provides free and confidential services to survivors of domestic violence, rape, and other forms of violence. Its services are guided by the belief that every woman has the right to live free from violence and to make decisions about her own life.


Service Areas:

  1. Provides shelters for survivors of domestic violence in Malaysia.

  2. Accepts hotline inquiries and promotes women's rights.

  3. Provides social worker accompaniment, walking alongside victimized women throughout the judicial process, offering emotional support, and helping them access services from the police, hospitals, welfare departments, and other agencies.

  4. Women and Children Programs. Offers recreational, psychosocial, and skills development programs to help domestic violence survivors recover and rebuild their lives. Also provides educational support for children of domestic violence victims.

  5. Childcare Center. Provides childcare services meeting the needs of food, housing, education, and recreation for children of victims returning to society.

  6. Regularly organizes exhibitions or workshops to promote women's rights and empower women.

Joint Action Group for Gender Equality (JAG) 


Background:

JAG is a coalition of civil society organizations (CSOs) committed to achieving gender equality. Established in 1985, JAG initially focused on combating violence against women. However, as more gender-related issues and research emerged, the organization evolved to adopt a more comprehensive approach to sexual and gender-based violence (SGBV).


Vision:

To build a just, democratic, and equitable society in which all people, especially women, are respected and free from all forms of violence and discrimination.

Action: Over the years, the many organizations comprising JAG have successfully carried out advocacy, public awareness, education, and legal reform efforts. JAG played a pivotal role in the drafting of the 1994 Domestic Violence Act and made landmark contributions to the inclusion of "gender" in Article 8(2) of the Federal Constitution in 2001.


Service Areas:

  1. Policy Advocacy. Lobbying ministers, politicians, and the public to enact, amend, or repeal laws and policies, aiming to end gender-based violence and promote women's rights.

  2. Public Education and Training. Raising awareness about gender-based violence and women's rights across all sectors of society through lectures, seminars, forums, workshops, training-of-trainers (TOT), and other outreach activities.

  3. Support Services. Providing free counseling and legal information services via the Telenita helpline to all individuals in crisis due to gender-based violence, regardless of gender, race, religion, socioeconomic status, or other intersecting identities.

  4. Community Engagement. Conducting outreach and intervention campaigns against gender-based violence in diverse communities, including marginalized communities. 1. Public Education and Training.

Women's Legal Aid and Counseling Bureau (LAWA) 


Background:

LAWA is a legal advisory and women's aid center established by the Malaysian Chinese Association (MCA) Women's Wing on December 16, 2014. It is Malaysia's first one-stop assistance center for female victims of all ethnicities established by a political party, aiming to deliver justice and protect the legitimate rights and interests of women victims of domestic violence, sexual assault, and similar issues.


Partnering Organizations:

LAWA collaborates with multiple government agencies and non-governmental organizations (NGOs) to provide comprehensive support for vulnerable female victims, helping them resolve problems and encouraging them to move forward and rebuild their lives. Government agencies involved include the Legal Aid Bureau under the Prime Minister's Department, Royal Malaysia Police, Social Welfare Department under the Ministry of Women, Ministry of Health, and Ministry of Human Resources. NGOs include the Malaysian Bar Council, Women Lawyers Association, Malaysian MAHA Cultural and Educational Association, and the Xinxin Jishi Public Welfare and Charity Organization.


Service Areas:

Providing free legal consultation, counseling, and assistance to female victims of all ethnicities, handling issues such as marriage, domestic violence, sexual assault, and workplace sexual harassment through walk-in consultations, phone calls, and emails, regardless of race, religion, or background.

Recommendations for Assistance and Protection Measures


As experience in providing assistance accumulates and laws continue to improve, and with sustained efforts by both government and non-governmental organizations, support and protection for women in Malaysia are steadily advancing. It is important not only to recognize the direct impact of assistance methods on victims but also to fully mobilize active collaboration among institutions. In terms of assistance methods, further optimization can be achieved through:

  1. Combining legal aid with psychological counseling

  2. Categorized and regionalized assistance based on age, religion, and other factors

  3. Promoting temporary shelters for domestic violence victims

  4. Establishing victim case files and conducting follow-up tracking


Regarding cooperation between government and non-governmental organizations, the following measures can be taken:

  1. Establishing an integrated anti-domestic violence mechanism to organically coordinate collaboration among police, courts, prosecutors, government agencies, and NGOs. This includes, but is not limited to, holding regular inter-agency meetings, increasing policy transparency, and establishing shared databases.

  2. Mobilizing social capital to strengthen financial support for assistance programs and ensure stable funding

  3. Encouraging greater participation of women in providing feedback on needs and in the formulation of related gender rights policies
