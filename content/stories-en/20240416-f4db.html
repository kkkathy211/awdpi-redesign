One

The Workplace Challenges of Asian American Women:

The Story Behind the Data


As economic globalization deepens and women's educational attainment improves worldwide, Asian American women are increasingly playing a significant role in the U.S. labor market. However, they still face numerous challenges in the workplace. This article will conduct an in-depth analysis of the situation of Asian American women in the U.S. workforce, revealing the inequalities they encounter.



Asian American women demonstrate strong labor force participation rates, yet still confront a rigid "bamboo ceiling."


The labor force participation rate of Asian American women in the United States has reached remarkably high levels. According to data from the U.S. Census Bureau, Asian American women accounted for approximately 6.3% of the total U.S. population in 2021, and this figure is projected to rise to 9.5% by 2060 (U.S. Census Bureau, 2020). As of 2020, the labor force participation rate for Asian American women aged 25 to 54 reached an impressive 70.4%, far exceeding the national female average of 56.2% for the same age group (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2020). In specific sectors, data from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics in 2021 show that Asian American women made up as much as 20.0% of all employees in management, business, and financial occupations, compared to 14.5% for Black women, 11.5% for Hispanic or Latina women, and 18.0% for White women (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2020). In addition, Asian American women have strong educational outcomes: data from the U.S. Census Bureau indicate that in 2018, the four-year college graduation rate for Asian American women was 78.5%, higher than the national average of 60.9% (U.S. Census Bureau, 2021).


Despite their strong performance in the labor market, Asian American women still face a rigid "bamboo ceiling." This ceiling means that even with outstanding education and professional qualifications, they are often seen as "doers" rather than "leaders," severely limiting their career advancement and demonstrating a complex pattern of "high employment rates in high-paying industries, but limited upward mobility." of complex characteristics.


According to McKinsey's 2022 report, *Asian Americans in the Workplace: Diverse Outcomes and Potential Challenges*, only a small number of Asian Americans advance to the highest executive levels, and the proportion of Asian American women is even lower—less than 1%. More shockingly, there are no Asian American women serving as CEOs in either S&P 100 index companies or Fortune 500 corporations. These figures reflect the many challenges Asian American women continue to face in their career development.

Within the Asian American female population, significant income disparities exist across different industries and ethnic subgroups.


Representation of Asians in high-tech industries:

With changes in immigration policy in the 1990s and the rapid development of the technology sector, a large number of high-skilled Asian immigrants have entered the United States, increasing Asian representation in fields such as computer science, mathematics, and engineering. For example, Asian Americans make up 23.3% of workers in computer and mathematical occupations (USAFacts Team, 2023). This shift not only highlights the contributions of Asian Americans to the U.S. high-tech industry but also reflects the profound impact of immigration policy on occupational representation. A Pew Research Center report shows that Asian Americans have seen significant gains in representation in computer science, mathematics, and engineering—fields typically associated with higher income levels—demonstrating their considerable influence in these high-tech industries (Pew Research Center, 2018).


Differences among Asian ethnic subgroups:

Asian workers in the U.S. primarily come from three regions: 35% from East Asia, 35% from Southeast Asia, and 27% from South Asia (McKinsey, *Asian Americans in the Workplace: Diverse Outcomes and Potential Challenges*, 2022). Among Asian Americans living in poverty, Chinese Americans account for nearly one-third. Asian American women generally earn less than men, with significant gaps, and disparities across ethnic subgroups are substantial. The chart below compares the wages of women from various Asian subgroups with those of non-Hispanic White men. Except for some groups (Taiwanese, Indian, Malaysian, and mainland Chinese women), who perform relatively well, for every dollar earned by non-Hispanic White men, women from other Asian ethnic subgroups earn between $0.52 and $0.92, with considerable variation across subgroups. Data show that from 2015 to 2019, the median annual income for full-time employed Indian American women was $70,000, while for Vietnamese American women it was only $36,500 (Source: Bleiweis, R. (2021). The Economic Status of Asian American and Pacific Islander).

Asian American women are forced to confront fragile labor relations and inadequate employment protections.


Limited employment protections: Employment protections for Asian American women in the U.S. are extremely limited. The United States is the only industrialized nation that does not guarantee paid leave for workers. Women often reduce their working hours (and thus their income) or leave their jobs entirely to fulfill unpaid caregiving responsibilities.


The U.S. Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) of 1993 guarantees eligible workers up to 12 weeks of unpaid leave, or 26 weeks to care for a service member, but only 53% of Asian American workers qualify for FMLA benefits—slightly lower than other racial groups (57% of Black workers and 58% of White workers). Nearly half of Asian American women have difficulty accessing, or cannot access, childcare services.


The massive impact of major crises (such as pandemics): The pandemic had a severe impact on Asian American women's employment, and the post-pandemic scarring effects are serious. According to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (2021), in 2020, employment levels declined sharply across all racial and ethnic groups. The employment-population ratio for White Americans dropped by 3.2 percentage points to 57.9%, while for Asian Americans it fell by 4.4 percentage points to 58.2%. In the same year, the labor force participation rate for White Americans declined by 1.6 percentage points to 61.6%, while for Asian Americans it decreased by 2.0 percentage points to 62.4%. In terms of unemployment, the rate for White Americans rose by 2.8 percentage points year-on-year to 6.0%, while for Asian Americans it increased by 4.0 percentage points to 6.7%. These figures indicate that Black, Asian, and Hispanic Americans were more vulnerable to the economic recession triggered by the pandemic than White Americans.


The impact on Asian American women was particularly pronounced. An analysis by the National Women's Law Center (2023) found that, as of December 2020, 44% of Asian American women aged 16 and older who lost their jobs during the pandemic experienced unemployment lasting at least six months—the highest proportion among all racial groups. This phenomenon is closely linked to the previously mentioned fact that Asian American women are disproportionately represented in the service sector.

Figure: Unemployment Rates (%) for Different Groups Aged 20 and Over, January 2019–January 2022 (Source: Bleiweis, R. (2021). The Economic Status of Asian American and Pacific Islander)


Asian American women face significant challenges in labor relations and employment security in the United States. During major crises such as pandemics, their employment situations become even more precarious. What if other crises, such as an economic downturn, occur in the future? These realities constantly remind us that enhancing the stability and protections for Asian American women in the labor market is imperative.



An overlooked group: The absence of Asian American women in workplace research


In our research process, we must confront a serious issue: the group of Asian American women has not received the attention it deserves and is frequently overlooked in many studies and statistical reports.


First, broad classification methods limit our understanding of the diversity among Asian American women. In many official datasets, "Asian American" or "Asian American women" is the smallest category used, but this classification fails to capture the nuanced realities of women from different ethnic backgrounds in the workplace. For example, Chinese, Korean, Japanese, and Indian women—whose education levels and cultural backgrounds differ significantly—are rarely studied as separate subgroups. In workplace data, Asian American women who do not work in tech companies, are not in mid-to-senior management, or are employed in retail, caregiving, tourism, and similar fields are easily overlooked. Compared to the so-called "elite Asian American women," these women receive far less research attention. Second, the accuracy of data remains questionable. Due to the lack of an intersectional perspective in some studies on women's workplace equality, data on Asian American women often suffer from sampling bias, insufficient sample sizes, and incomplete information.


This lack of in-depth research and detailed statistics makes it difficult to fully understand the challenges and opportunities Asian American women face in the workplace. To develop more inclusive and equitable workplace policies, we urgently call on official research institutions to conduct deeper investigations into this issue and fully recognize the diversity and complexity of Asian American women across different occupational fields. Only then can we better support the professional development of Asian American women and ensure they receive fair opportunities and equal treatment. Next, we will examine the reasons behind the workplace inequality experienced by Asian American women in the United States.


Two

Reasons for workplace inequality

faced by Asian American women in the United States


Asian women in the U.S. workplace face multiple challenges, the root causes of which include cultural differences and unfair treatment within the work environment.



Cultural Differences: Asian women are caught under a "double bind"

In U.S. workplace culture, Asian women often struggle with this double bind. This is because they must not only adapt to American workplace norms but also confront labor market stereotypes about Asian cultures. Cultural conflicts encountered at work frequently place them under immense pressure.


First, let us explain what stereotypes about Asian women are.


Asian women are often assigned a stereotype of being "obedient," hardworking, and "well-behaved." While these impressions may sometimes help them secure professional opportunities, they also force them to maintain traits of being "polite, intelligent, and compliant" at work. This not only severely limits their freedom but also deprives them of their individual diversity and potential. If they attempt to break away from or deviate from this "model minority" image, they risk losing career opportunities or facing greater discrimination. When Asian women try to assert greater influence, stronger professional competitiveness, and higher leadership potential, they are in fact challenging the hierarchical structure shaped by gender and racial stereotypes.


In McKinsey's report *Asian American Employees: Diverse Outcomes and Hidden Challenges*, a 30-year-old Southeast Asian woman working in the U.S. said: "The workplace environment is difficult because people here always assume that Asian women should be excellent sales managers. I've been constantly fighting invisible biases—I've done everything I can to grow, yet I'm still held back because of my identity." Another South Asian woman, who immigrated to the U.S. as a child and now works as a software engineer, said: "I truly believe my cultural background makes it harder to prove myself. It's an issue of confidence—I worry about being fired." (McKinsey & Company. (2022). *Asian American workers: Diverse outcomes and hidden challenges.*)


A study published in the *Journal of Asian American Psychology* (Mukkamala & Suyemoto, 2018) revealed specific stereotypes and discrimination, involving 107 participants and demonstrating that Asian women face discrimination in both professional and personal life. The main forms include: (1) Exoticization—linked to the historical stereotype of Asian women as "geisha girls"; (2) Ineligibility for leadership—perceived as lacking ability, too quiet, and deficient in leadership qualities; (3) Submissiveness—seen as easily controlled and unlikely to advocate for themselves; (4) Cuteness and smallness—assumed to naturally be a certain way; (5) Invisibility—lacking sufficient power to speak up; (6) Service roles—associated with low-level, gender-specific jobs, often linked to domestic work or nail salons; (7) Other forms of discrimination: assumptions that all Asians are the same; being mistaken for another Asian ethnicity; being seen as foreigners; perceived as naturally intelligent and successful; poor driving skills; and various misunderstandings about cultural beliefs and customs. Figure 7 shows data on various forms of discrimination and inequality experienced by women of different races in the U.S. workplace. Compared to the average across other races and genders, Asian women are more likely to be interrupted during workplace meetings or subjected to cultural insults.

Second, the dominant cultural influence of white adjacency (Kim, 2023).


White adjacency is a workplace cultural phenomenon that tends to reward those perceived as conforming more closely to white norms while penalizing those seen as distant from them. White adjacency treats white culture and values as the dominant standard, positioning other cultures as secondary or foreign. This culture places additional pressure on Asian women, as the values and social expectations of Asian cultures often conflict with those of white culture.


For example, Asian cultures emphasize avoiding conflict and maintaining group harmony, expressed in Chinese as "it's better to have one less problem than one more" (duō yī shì bù rú shǎo yī shì), while the Japanese popular phrase "kuuki o yomu" (literally "reading the air," equivalent to "being perceptive to social cues") conveys a similar meaning. This leads Asian women to avoid overt resistance in the workplace, instead prioritizing interpersonal relationships and personal image to prevent conflict and safeguard their development opportunities. This cultural mindset is often perceived as deviant by white cultural norms and values, and such implicit discrimination places pressure and blame on individuals, framing experienced injustices as personal psychological issues, causing many Asian women to fall into endless self-doubt.


At the same time, this often leads to the occurrence of microaggressions. First, microaggressions specifically refer to racial slights that create barriers to professional advancement (Sue et al., 2007). In the workplace, microaggressions experienced by Asian women include derogatory terms targeting their race and gender—such as "exotic spice" (Mukkamala & Suyemoto, 2018), "mail-order brides" (China doll, mail-order brides) (Perez, 2003), or "Asian Dragon Lady" (Mukkamala & Suyemoto, 2018). "Exotic spice" refers to the exoticization of Asian women, while "Asian Dragon Lady" portrays them as controlling and untrustworthy (Mukkamala & Suyemoto, 2018). "Mail-order brides" originated from the "mail-order bride industry" in the 1970s, when American men sought to marry Filipino women; these brides were often perceived as gentle and submissive Asian women (Perez, 2003).


Gendered racism to some extent prevents Asian women from being seen as leadership figures, rendering their professional identities invisible and silent. These stereotypes have profound negative impacts on Asian women's self-efficacy, outcome expectations, and career interests in the workplace, ultimately preventing them from achieving the same level of professional success as other racial groups and genders.



Workplace Environment: Intense insecurity and psychological suppression caused by the need to "constantly prove oneself"


In the workplace, Asian women often have to repeatedly prove their competence and value—a phenomenon known as "proving-it-again bias." This means they must continually re-establish their presence and authority to receive the same respect and recognition as their colleagues.


For example, being asked in meetings to restate their qualifications; having their ideas ignored until repeated by a white man or woman; or being denied promotions despite exceeding job requirements. According to a study by the Society of Women Engineers, 61% of women of color reported having to repeatedly prove themselves to gain the same respect and recognition as their peers, compared to only 35% of white men (Tulshyan, 2016).


This situation causes significant psychological stress for Asian women. Compared to their white peers, they must exert far greater effort to gain recognition and promotion opportunities. Yet, even when they work hard, they are often seen as task executors rather than leaders, especially in male-dominated industries. This makes them hesitant to take assertive actions, fearing damage to their professional reputation and advancement prospects.


Moreover, Asian women also struggle to achieve high psychological safety in such workplace environments. Psychological safety refers to employees' belief that they can speak openly and raise concerns without fear of retaliation. When Asian women share their experiences of gender and racial discrimination at work, they are often met with backlash, making them feel even less safe. Yet, constructive ideas often need to be voiced at critical moments—precisely when individuals who speak up demonstrate both honesty and vulnerability, exposing themselves to potential threats. The current U.S. social environment still prioritizes white emotional comfort, resulting in Asian women lacking psychological safety when sharing their experiences of injustice and pain, creating significant barriers to their resistance.


During the pandemic, this sense of workplace insecurity increased sharply and continues to have lasting effects. Ruiz, Im, and Tian (2023) found that during the COVID-19 pandemic, Asian American adults experienced various forms of discrimination. These experiences were often not isolated incidents but occurred in multiple, overlapping forms. The survey revealed that most Asian Americans experienced discrimination in three broad areas: being treated as foreigners (even if born in the U.S.), being perceived as model minorities, and encountering other race- or ethnicity-based discriminatory incidents in daily life. The survey also found that 78% of Asian adults were treated as foreigners to some extent, including being told to "go back to your country," assumed not to speak English, criticized for speaking non-English in public, or having their names mispronounced. Additionally, 63% of Asian adults experienced being stereotyped as model minorities, including assumptions that they are good at math and science or not creative thinkers (Ruiz, N.G., Im, C. & Tian, Z., 2023. *Asian Americans and discrimination during the COVID-19 pandemic*. Pew Research Center). During the COVID-19 pandemic, reported incidents of discrimination against Asian Americans increased, including a rise in hate crimes targeting Asians. Within the Asian American community, a significant proportion believe that discrimination against Asians is a major issue and that the racial challenges faced by Asian Americans receive too little attention.


In summary, the inequality faced by Asian American women in the U.S. workplace is a multifaceted issue resulting from a combination of factors. Cultural differences and unfair treatment within the work environment place significant challenges on these women, requiring proactive measures from both society and employers to support their development and foster a more equitable and inclusive workplace.


Next, we provide a guide to protecting rights for women experiencing workplace inequality.


Three

A Guide for Asian American Women in the United States

Workplace Rights Protection Guide



Five steps you can take when facing workplace inequality:


1

Carefully read your employment contract and employee handbook.

If you are currently employed, we recommend first carefully reviewing the clauses in your employment contract (contract) regarding working hours, salary, work location, contract duration, termination conditions, as well as your employee handbook (handbook/manual), which typically includes your employer's anti-discrimination policies and complaint procedures.


2

Communicate with your employer.

Next, you may report discriminatory behavior experienced by yourself or others to the human resources department or your supervisor, preferably via email or other written form, providing a detailed and as objective as possible account of the incident, including the time, location, individuals involved, evidence, and witnesses, and keep a copy; if you choose to report verbally in person, we recommend sending a follow-up email afterward to confirm the content and accuracy of the discussion, and retain any written response you receive.


3

Seek help from a labor union.

If you are a union member, you may contact your union representative to file a grievance regarding the terms and conditions in the Collective Bargaining Agreement (CBA) related to discrimination. If the agreement covers your claims, this may be a viable channel for resolution.


4

File an administrative charge.

If the discrimination is not adequately addressed or compensated, or if you face retaliatory actions from your employer due to your anti-discrimination efforts, you may turn to government agencies to file a Charge of Discrimination. The responsible agencies include your state's anti-discrimination or civil rights office—typically the Fair Employment Practices Agency (FEPA)—or the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC). Either may be used, though the deadlines for filing charges may differ. FEPA and EEOC will first attempt mediation; if no voluntary agreement is reached, they will initiate an investigation. If discrimination is confirmed, they will begin conciliation procedures; if unsuccessful, the agency has the authority to decide whether to file a lawsuit (Discrimination Lawsuit). If the investigation fails to confirm a violation or the agency ultimately decides not to sue, you will receive a Notice of Right-to-Sue..


5

Pursue judicial litigation.

Generally, administrative channels through government agencies take precedence. If you intend to file a personal lawsuit in court under federal law, you must go through the above steps, meaning you must first file a charge with the EEOC or FEPA and obtain a Notice of Right-to-Sue before you can file in court. Lawsuits based on the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA) and the Equal Pay Act (EPA) are two exceptions: for ADEA, a charge must be filed within a certain timeframe before litigation, but a Notice of Right-to-Sue is not required; for EPA, neither a charge nor a Notice of Right-to-Sue is required, though to invoke Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 in court, the administrative charge process must still be followed.


It is important to note that the laws enforced by the EEOC target employers, not other individuals; their coverage is limited by conditions such as the type of employer and the number of employees, which state laws may supplement; they apply to various employment situations, including hiring, firing, promotion, harassment, training, wages, and benefits. Therefore, if you encounter unequal treatment during your job search or face unemployment due to unequal treatment, the aforementioned rights protection process also applies to your prospective employer or former employer. Additionally, if you are an employee or job applicant of a U.S. federal government agency, the complaint procedure is different and is not covered in this article.


Finally, when discrimination occurs—especially when it involves privacy or sensitive issues—you fully retain the right to resolve the matter privately, the right to report anonymously through hotlines or nonprofit organizations, or the right to choose not to act. However, we encourage you to use your voice—as an individual, as an ally, as a witness.



Understand in detail the rights you hold and their legal basis.


Types of Discrimination

Legal Protections (partial list)

Race/Color

  • Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 Protects individuals from employment discrimination based on race, color, national origin, sex, or religion. It is illegal to discriminate against any employee or job applicant based on race or color in hiring, firing, promotion, compensation, job training, or any other term, condition, or privilege of employment. Title VII also prohibits employment decisions based on stereotypes or assumptions about the abilities, traits, or performance of individuals of certain racial groups. It prohibits both intentional discrimination and neutral employment policies that disproportionately exclude minorities and are not job-related.


  • The Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) Anti-discrimination provision 8 U.S.C. §1324b explicitly prohibits discrimination based on citizenship status. The law bans discrimination in hiring, firing, recruitment, or referral for a fee based on citizenship status or national origin; unfair documentary practices in employment eligibility verification, I-9 forms, and electronic employment verification (E-Verify); and retaliation or intimidation.

Age

  • The Age Discrimination in Employment Act of 1967 (ADEA) Protects job applicants and employees who are 40 years of age or older from employment discrimination based on age.


  • The Age Discrimination Act of 1975, enforced by the Civil Rights Center (CRC) of the U.S. Department of Labor, prohibits discrimination based on age in programs and activities receiving federal financial assistance. The Act applies to all age groups and allows certain age distinctions and factors other than age to meet its requirements.

Sex/Gender

  • Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 Protects individuals from employment discrimination based on race, color, national origin, sex, or religion; since June 2020, gender identity and sexual orientation have also been included under protection.


  • Sexual harassment is a form of sex discrimination and violates Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964. Unwelcome sexual advances, requests for sexual favors, and other verbal or physical conduct of a sexual nature constitute sexual harassment when such conduct explicitly or implicitly affects an individual's employment, unreasonably interferes with an individual's work performance, or creates an intimidating, hostile, or offensive work environment.

Compensation

  • The Equal Pay Act of 1963) Requires that men and women employees in the same establishment be given equal pay for equal work. The jobs need not be identical, but they must be substantially equal. Whether jobs are substantially equal depends on the content of the work, not merely on job titles. The law covers all forms of compensation, including salary, overtime pay, bonuses, stock options, profit sharing and bonus plans, life insurance, holiday pay, cleaning or gasoline allowances, hotel accommodations, travel expense reimbursement, and benefits.


  • Title VII of the Civil Rights Act also prohibits sex-based discrimination in the payment of wages and benefits. Therefore, individuals filing a claim under the Equal Pay Act may also file under Title VII.


  • The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), enforced by the Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division (WHD), mandates that, as of July 24, 2009, the federal minimum wage for covered non-exempt employees is $7.25 per hour. Many states also have minimum wage laws. If an employee is subject to both federal and state laws, the employee is entitled to the higher minimum wage. The law also requires that employees who work more than 40 hours in a workweek receive overtime pay at a rate not less than one and one-half times their regular rate of pay.

Pregnancy/Breastfeeding

  • The Pregnancy Discrimination Act of 1978 (PDA) is an amendment to Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, stating that discrimination based on pregnancy, childbirth, or related medical conditions constitutes unlawful sex discrimination. Women affected by pregnancy or related medical conditions must be treated the same as other applicants or employees who are similar in their ability or inability to work.


  • The Family and Medical Leave Act of 1993 (FMLA), enforced by the U.S. Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division, provides eligible parents (including foster and adoptive parents) with up to 12 weeks of unpaid leave to care for a new child.


  • The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) states that impairments related to pregnancy (e.g., gestational diabetes) may qualify as disabilities. Employers may be required to provide reasonable accommodations for employees disabled due to pregnancy, unless doing so would cause undue hardship (significant difficulty or expense).


  • The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), enforced by the U.S. Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division, requires that nursing mothers be allowed reasonable break time and a private, non-bathroom space to express breast milk at work. Employers must provide these accommodations for eligible employees for up to one year after the child's birth.

Religion

  • Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibits employers from discriminating against individuals in hiring, firing, or any other term or condition of employment based on their religious beliefs (or lack thereof). Title VII also prohibits religious segregation in the workplace, religious harassment, and requires employers to reasonably accommodate the religious beliefs and practices of applicants and employees, under certain conditions.

Disability

  • The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) and The Rehabilitation Act It is illegal to discriminate against qualified employees or job applicants on the basis of physical or mental disability in the private sector, state and local governments, and the federal government. The law requires employers to provide reasonable accommodations to employees or applicants with disabilities unless doing so would cause undue hardship or expense. The law also protects individuals from discrimination based on their relationship to a person with a disability, even if they themselves do not have a disability.
