1

Crossing the ocean to work in the United States

"

"We are called helpers. In some people they call us helpers because you know, we do the dirty job."

(We are called helpers. People call us this precisely because we do the dirty work.)


The Philippines is the world's largest exporter of caregivers. Among the 2.2 million Filipinos working overseas, about one-quarter are engaged in caregiving-related jobs. Maria is one of them.


Twenty years ago, Maria crossed the ocean to come to the United States. When asked why she chose to become a healthcare worker, Maria shares the same reason as many of her compatriots in America. "You could say it's a 'family tradition,' because my family has always worked in similar roles," says Maria. "My mother was a supervisor in hospital administration, and I have relatives who are doctors, so I spent almost my entire childhood in hospitals."


In 2023, as the first "peaceful year" arrived after the retreat of the pandemic, Maria turned 52. Twenty years earlier, when she first arrived in the U.S., she was a single mother with three children. She worked two jobs at once, supporting her family while earning a living.


After completing a period of professional training, Maria obtained her certification and began working as a licensed nursing assistant at the Veterans Affairs Hospital in San Francisco—commonly known as someone who "does the dirty work for nurses." Every day, she mechanically carried out fragmented tasks that American nurses were unwilling to do, such as "recording vital signs, assisting patients with toileting, and checking vouchers."


For Maria, although caregiving work allowed her to support her family, the profession brought more than just physical exhaustion and overwork—due to frequent overtime, lack of compensatory time off, and other job-related issues—she also endured immense psychological pressure over the long term.


Out of responsibility to her patients, working late into the night was routine. After finishing night shifts, Maria still had to care for her children's daily needs. As a single mother, she had already given her all just to survive. "Just working and taking care of my kids has left me completely drained," Maria said with regret. "I couldn't accompany them or inquire about their studies or mental well-being."



Fortunately, Maria is an outgoing and optimistic person. "My situation is not uncommon. Many Filipino caregivers working in the U.S. have gone through the same. My children have grown up safely—this makes all the hardship worthwhile."



2

They should be understood, not defined

"


Compared to Filipino caregivers, people may have a deeper perception of "Filipina domestic workers" (maids) This is how the Philippine government promotes its domestic workers abroad: highly qualified, hardworking, gentle, obedient… Without exception, as a "senior caregiver" in the U.S., Maria possesses all the so-called "qualities" of Filipino caregivers: mild-mannered, perceptive, good at problem-solving, and highly tolerant.


However, the "positive feedback" and recognition from clients and employers often mean that Maria must face these "dirty jobs in others' eyes" with extraordinary psychological resilience and efforts beyond the scope of her job.


The most challenging task is caring for patients with dementia. "Caregivers may suffer physical assaults or sexual harassment from patients, yet they are powerless to respond because the patients are mentally unwell." For Maria and her fellow Filipino caregivers, such unpredictable physical and psychological harm from clients is far more terrifying than physical exhaustion.



An ordinary American caregiver can earn at least $50 per hour, with experienced ones earning as much as $70–80. In contrast, Filipino caregivers like Maria earn no more than $30. "It's not that we're less professional than American caregivers," says Maria. On the contrary, "we aren't afraid of hard or dirty work, and we take this job—our livelihood—very seriously." What Maria cannot voice is the underestimation of Filipino workers' capabilities and the neglect of their contributions.


This unequal treatment is not due to "educational level." In fact, most Filipino nurses have received higher education. Take the 1948 "Exchange Visitor" program, for example: it offered foreign nursing graduates two-year master's programs and clinical internships in American hospitals, enabling many Filipino nurses trained in the American system to work in the U.S.


In 2009, Maria obtained U.S. citizenship through skilled immigration policy, yet she is still perceived as "having not received a good education." A simple, recurring problem is that Maria is often asked by Americans around her, "Do you speak English?"



3

Guardians born of war

"

Data shows that in the United States, 5.2% of caregivers are from Asia, nearly half of whom are from the Philippines.


During World War II (1939–1945), large numbers of American women were recruited to serve as caregivers. After the war, the U.S. government stopped incentivizing people to enter caregiving through improved pay and working conditions—a policy that directly caused a rapid decline in the number of caregivers, leading to immigration labor policies. Afterwards, the U.S. government turned overseas: hospitals introduced new temporary visitor strategies, and within a decade, over ten thousand Filipino caregivers came to work in the United States.


Then, in the 1960s, major historical changes—such as the creation of new healthcare insurance and Medicaid, and various women's rights movements—gave American women more opportunities to enter new professions. A series of policies and reforms continuously reshaped the demand for healthcare services.


In 1946, after the Philippines gained independence from American colonial rule, its economy continued to decline. During the Marcos era (1965–1986), corruption was rampant, deindustrialization occurred, and the people's living conditions worsened. At that time, the Philippine economy stagnated and wages were meager, prompting many to seek better opportunities abroad. Remittances from Filipino caregivers and other overseas workers played a significant role in revitalizing the economy. The convergence of individual and governmental interests made the export of Filipino caregivers/laborers appear to be a seemingly "win-win," even "multi-win," situation.


Yet the unjust treatment endured by Filipino caregivers in foreign lands has not received sufficient attention. After arriving in the U.S., many host hospitals treat them merely as cheap labor. Like Maria, they long endure the dirtiest and most exhausting work, yet receive salaries far below those of local caregivers, while also facing criticism and discrimination from all directions.



4

To see and understand, to respect and support

"


Maria possesses the typical gentleness of an Eastern mother. Though somewhat reserved and cautious during interviews, she becomes remarkably firm and passionate when offering advice to women working in healthcare: "Be strong, face challenges with an open mind; when caring for patients, learn to empathize and put yourself in their shoes; love your work, do it out of passion rather than economic necessity; maintain dedication/loyalty, empathy, and compassion."



Maria's advice to ethnic minority women in the U.S. is: "Be strong." As she says this, her voice trembles slightly, recalling her past days moving between patients—times when she worked diligently and conscientiously, yet were also filled with deep sorrow.


Ethnic minority caregivers like Maria and other service workers come to the United States with dreams. Despite facing unfair treatment, they still devote themselves wholeheartedly to the country's development. Both civil organizations and local governments should recognize their contributions, treat their labor equally, and pay attention to their psychological needs. "We, like American caregivers, take our work seriously," Maria tells us.
