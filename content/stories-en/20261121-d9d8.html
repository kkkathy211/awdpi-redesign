Crossing Boundaries: Blogger · Researcher · AI Developer


 Jing:  Our next question: as women move from "what should I do" to "what can I do," could you give an everyday example from Xiaohongshu?

 404:  Everyone's information feed is different. I've noticed a common example around relationships and marriage. We often see messages like, "At a certain age, you should be dating and getting married." But in the content I study, some say: "It's not that I don't want to date—I just don't want to marry just for the sake of marriage." The shift is subtle: she can still desire intimacy, but now sees marriage as an option, not an obligation. Seeing other women share different lives might help someone realize their hesitation is valid, that they don't need to constantly explain or apologize for it.

But I think algorithms have a dual role. They can repeatedly show you scattered experiences, creating a sense of companionship and support; but they can also keep pushing another set of standards—telling women they must be independent, disciplined, and career-successful. So "I should get married" becomes "I should become the perfect independent woman." I think neither extreme is quite right. What I care about is whether content expands people's choices, including the space to temporarily make no choice at all—that's also a valid option.

My platform research mainly analyzes public posts and comments, so I can't directly determine whether seeing these contents changes people's real-life decisions. That's something I may explore in later research.



 Jing:  So do you think, because of algorithms, women are moving toward their own unique narratives, or is it the algorithm that decides which trends become widely visible?

 404:  I think both are happening at the same time. When women actively share their experiences and respond to one another, algorithms determine the visibility of those expressions. And when creators see certain content gets more feedback—like higher traffic, more likes and comments—they may adjust their expression accordingly. So I see it as a dynamic relationship, a continuous interaction between user expression, platform distribution, and audience feedback.

So when thinking about this issue, we need to look not only at what women are saying, but also ask ourselves and remind ourselves:

Whose stories are more likely to be seen? Are complex, contradictory, or nuanced experiences—ones that can't easily be summarized into a catchy quote, even though quotes are especially popular online—being left out of view?

Whether it's creators' choices or platform algorithm recommendations, I think these are all aspects we need to pay attention to.

 Jing:  Do you see any connection between your observations as a blogger and your research? For instance, has being a creator helped you notice details that might be easily overlooked in desk research?

 404:  Regarding this question, first, I think the two are deeply connected; second, yes, there are indeed some details that desk research might miss.

First, my research mainly uses digital ethnography, which includes sustained platform observation. My experience as a blogger helps me better recognize that posts are curated expressions. For example, as a content creator, the choice of images, the wording of titles, which experiences to share, and which parts of those experiences to reveal—all of these shape the final presentation. Interactions in the comment section also continuously reshape the meaning of a post.

But this also makes me attentive to the silence behind the data: we see what's published, but some experiences remain unspoken or unseen. Being inside the platform helps me notice these nuances, and also reminds me to constantly reflect: my feed doesn't represent everyone, and public posts aren't the full picture of life. I can't generalize all women's experiences from them. This is something I always keep in mind when doing research.

 Jing:  On the other hand, could your research have any impact on your work as a blogger? Are there any new considerations you've developed?

 404:  Definitely. During the research process, I've had many divergent thoughts. There are some changes in the kind of content I want to produce as a blogger, both before and after conducting the research, which affects my topic selection and judgment about content output.

Because the research has made me especially cautious about presenting personal experience as a universal solution. For example, when I share something, I can explain why a certain choice works for me, but I'm more careful not to imply that all women should follow the same path. When responding to comments, I also try first to understand the other person's specific situation. Some people need encouragement, others face real-life constraints, and some just want their hesitation to be heard. I think responses should be based on specific contexts and individual circumstances—it's hard to give clear-cut or binary answers.

So when I share personal content, I hope to offer a reference that helps people reflect on what they truly want, while also leaving space for different choices, rather than suggesting that only one path is correct.

 Jing:  You mentioned that your research uses digital ethnography. Some of our audience might not be familiar with what exactly digital ethnography means—could you elaborate a bit more?

 404:  Sure. Since I'm doing platform research on Xiaohongshu, I may use data collection tools—there are many available on GitHub, and people can search for them. I spent about half a year scraping relevant posts, using specific tags to collect the data. That's part of my methodological approach.

What is digital ethnography?

In simpler terms, it means continuously engaging with an online community, and there are two main aspects to this: first, the online community itself, and second, sustained participation. It's not about briefly collecting posts under a certain tag; rather, it's about consistently monitoring the community's evolution—understanding how people live, communicate, interact there, and what these interactions signify.

For example, when I study Xiaohongshu (Little Red Book), I don't just read posts—I also observe continuously. I don't know if you've come across certain posts where people screenshot others' content. These kinds of interactions can only be noticed through long-term observation: someone might see a post, take a screenshot, and say it inspired them, which then sparks further discussion. How do people express their experiences? How do comment sections respond? What are the unwritten rules within the community? For instance, a tag I previously tracked is now unusable—it's been banned. How do these interactions shape people's understanding of themselves throughout this process? Xiaohongshu can be seen as a space for real-life experiences and interactions, albeit virtual and online. Of course, my own feed and personal experience cannot represent everyone. This is a more accessible explanation—I'm not sure if I've made it clear.

 Jing:  Let's talk more about your experience as a blogger. Your fashion style is very distinctive—just like your bio says, "Knows fashion in AI, knows AI in fashion."  404:  To answer directly: yes, I think there's a strong connection.

Suddenly I remember something: recently I attended an offline event and met several musicians who shared an interesting perspective. This might be a bit off-topic, but it's still about expression, so I'd like to share. They said that when we wear clothes, for example, in the early days we'd make a hole in a T-shirt to thread a wired earphone through—it's actually a form of wearable tech. Now, in the AI era, many people talk about AI embodied intelligent wearables—aren't these essentially the same thing? I found this idea quite interesting. Since they're musicians, at music events they hand out T-shirts with a display screen on the front that shows sound waves moving in sync with the music being played. Isn't this similar to the smart wearable devices we wear today? Isn't this also a form of expression? I was deeply moved when I heard this and strongly agreed.

Coming back to the question: for me, fashion is a daily form of self-expression—how I want to present myself today, what makes me comfortable, what feels fun to me… It's a way I express myself. Of course, these choices are also influenced by others' gaze, social contexts, and platform aesthetics.

Linking this back to my research: in one of the posts I collected, someone shared that she finally dared to go out in a tank top, but still sometimes fears others' stares. I wonder if others feel this conflict too—I definitely do. I think this tension is very real: trying something new doesn't mean I've completely overcome that pressure. So I hope women can feel freer to decide how to dress, and also free to change their minds. Whether you like dressing up or not, whether you dress boldly or modestly, it should all be your own choice. As for me, I hope fashion and technology can coexist naturally, without having to hide one part of myself for the sake of another identity..

AI Translation and Female Authority


Jing:  404 has another study titled "From Scientist to Inspiration: Tracing the Discourse Degradation of Female Authority in AI Translation Pipelines," which translates into Chinese as "Cong kexuejia dao lizhi renwu: zhuiji AI fanyi jilei chengxu zhong nuxing quanwei de huayu jiangge" (From Scientist to Inspiration: Tracing the Discourse Degradation of Female Authority in AI Translation Pipelines). Those interested can take a look.

Some viewers might not be very familiar with this article or the process of AI translation—could 404 please explain in everyday language what central question this research most wants to answer?

 404:  When you read a book, there's a cover with a blurb; when you open it, you see the translated main text; and at the front, there's an abstract. The question this research wants to answer is: when a text introducing a professional figure goes through AI translation, summarization, and is then turned into a blurb, how is that person re-narrated?

When we talk about translation bias, we often first look at whether pronouns are translated correctly. But I want to go further:

Tracing the Discourse Degradation of Female Authority in AI Translation Pipelines

When women who have achieved success in a professional field have their stories translated into other languages, is there a cumulative bias? Even if their professionalism is still mentioned in translation, does the reader ultimately perceive them as someone who creates knowledge and makes contributions, or as someone who is deeply moving and inspiring?

That's what my title means. So what I'm focusing on is: How does a person's professional authority change after going through this chain of processing—that is, a cumulative translation process? I hope I've made this clear—if not, I'm happy to add more explanation.

 Jing:  Okay, our next question is almost like a follow-up: could you use an example to illustrate exactly what AI changes during translation, and how these changes come about?

 404:  For example, suppose there's a Chinese text that you want to translate into English. The text introduces a chief scientist who, through rigorous logical reasoning, proposes a new model—and it turns out this scientist and the model have rewritten the entire industry's standards. Up to this point, the original text doesn't explicitly state the person's gender.

I would have the model perform translation, summarization, and blurb writing in sequence, and then compare the different versions. In one version, I didn't mention any gender at all—just added one sentence: "Science has no gender." The final result was that, as the processing progressed—from initial translation, to summary, to blurb—the focus gradually shifted away from logical reasoning and model breakthroughs, and instead emphasized perseverance, resilience, and inspiration. In multiple runs, this emotionalized framing consistently appeared, regardless of whether the earlier translation used "she" or "he."

So it's not necessarily the facts that change, but the narrative focus undergoes a significant shift. For instance, why do we value these people? Is it because they created certain knowledge, or because their story is particularly uplifting and touching? That's what I'm trying to study. Of course, the paper will continue to be refined.

 Jing:  May I ask—do you use many different AI platforms to run these tests?

 404:  I wrote this paper at the end of 2025 and beginning of 2026, and at that time I used ChatGPT. I didn't test across different models because I felt that would make my paper too scattered, so I chose to focus on one specific model.

 Jing:  Thank you. The shift from "scientist" to "inspiration" is very interesting—could you elaborate on whether there was a turning point that sparked your interest in this issue, and changed your view on AI and language expression?

 404:  The turning point, I think, was realizing that "bias can also hide within praise". Before, I would closely watch whether pronouns were translated correctly, but during my research, when I added a sentence saying "science knows no gender," in that version I noticed the recommendation system would prioritize perseverance and resilience, while the scientist's actual contributions were pushed further back. This made me realize that even if a statement doesn't belittle you, or even if it's praising you, it still doesn't necessarily clarify your professional capabilities.

Later, when reviewing AI outputs, beyond checking accuracy or translation errors, I began focusing on what it was praising: is this person's identity as a knowledge creator still at the core, or has it already been pushed to the margins? This has actually influenced how I understand language: It's not just about whether facts are preserved, but also how those facts are organized—what is placed at the forefront.

AI for Good: The Story of Genderly


Genderly was invited to participate in Tencent Research Institute's Creator Camp


 Jing:  We know that 404 is not only an outstanding blogger and researcher, but also the founder of Genderly. Could 404 briefly introduce to everyone what kind of project Genderly mainly is?

 404:  First of all, I'm not particularly accomplished in any of these three areas—I've just dabbled, haha. Okay, let me explain the main issue Genderly wants to address. I wouldn't say we aim to "solve" it outright, but rather respond to a very common situation:

When I read a sentence and feel uncomfortable—say, a news headline or some piece of text—I feel uneasy but can't clearly identify what's wrong or how to respond. Or when I finish writing something and am about to publish it, I want to check whether I've overlooked any gender perspectives or gender-related issues.

Let me give an example. We analyzed a recent case: when Professor Wang Hong won the Fields Medal, Xinhua News Agency used the headline "How Did This Guangxi Girl Win the Highest Honor in International Mathematics?" When we feel uncomfortable seeing such a headline, we can use Genderly's browser extension to directly unpack the underlying logic. Of course, we have two product formats: the web version is more suited for medium-to-long texts with complex narratives, which I'll elaborate on later when discussing the product.

In one sentence, Genderly helps users break down these vague feelings into concrete, discussable textual evidence. The browser extension can locate relevant text segments, provide explanations, alternative expressions, or help you respond by offering different response strategies. The web version goes further by analyzing character positioning and the overall narrative structure. We aim to provide evidence-based analysis and expressive support, helping users better understand their own feelings and then decide whether and how to respond. That choice remains with the user. Our judgments are always context-dependent and open to questioning and correction.


Genderly website interface (https://www.genderly.icu/)


 Jing:  Could you explain, what exactly is the "My List" section in Genderly? How did you come up with the idea to design it?

 404:  First, let me add some background about the product: Genderly was actually formed by merging two separate products. The first one was a browser extension we developed during a women's hackathon in January this year. Like Jing, I often had these issues—when scrolling through Xiaohongshu or Douban, I wished for a plugin that could help me not only respond but also optimize content. You probably know Grammarly, right? It corrects your grammar. We wanted to create something similar.

After we launched it, Qiao Yi and Desal saw what we were doing and realized they had built a web-based tool. So we merged the two products, ultimately forming Genderly. The web version was actually completed by Qiao Yi and Desal, who later joined our team. Qiao Yi graduated from the University of Leeds with a degree in Gender Studies. She isn't here today, so I'll answer on her behalf regarding her purpose in designing this feature—and we fully agree and support it: she wanted to extend support from analyzing a piece of text to the user's own personal experiences. On the web platform, for example, you can copy and paste a text for analysis. But we wanted to go beyond that—to include users' personal experiences. For instance, when something happens and I feel uncomfortable but don't have time to respond, I might only gradually realize why I felt that way later. Or I may want to unpack that discomfort, so I can fully describe the incident and save it in My List. Users can record and store these scenarios here, receiving emotional support, analysis, or advice on how to respond. Users can also decide whether to make them public or keep them private. It creates a space where these experiences can be preserved, revisited, and slowly understood.

 Jing:  Regarding Genderly, the same sentence can carry different meanings across cultures and individual interpretations, especially since Chinese is such a complex language. How does AI define and detect gender bias during analysis?

 404:  I completely agree. This is exactly why many large language models now have similar initiatives to correct bias, but it's still difficult to identify implicit biases in Chinese contexts because Chinese is indeed a very complex and vast linguistic system. Different cultural backgrounds and personal experiences affect how we interpret the same sentence. For Genderly, we have a clear analytical framework, but we also adjust our judgments based on different contexts.

The first step is to determine whether the text requires gender analysis. The mere presence of women or a gendered term in the text doesn't automatically imply bias. We first look for relevant character narratives, gender markers, or social issues, and whether there are expectations about "how one should or must behave." Only within the full context do we decide whether to conduct in-depth analysis, limited discussion, or not analyze it at all.

Once we enter analysis mode, we focus on how the text positions characters: Who is taking action and making decisions, and who is being described or evaluated; from whose perspective the story is told; and what is presented as the core reason this person deserves attention and respect. For example, when introducing a female scientist, mentioning her appearance, emotions, or family experiences can be part of the story—there's nothing wrong with that. But we need to further examine what role these elements play: do they enrich her image, or do they overshadow her professional contributions, making readers more likely to interpret her story as an inspirational tale while overlooking her identity as an expert and knowledge creator?

So the gender bias we identify includes those taken-for-granted gender expectations in narratives, as well as unequal standards of evaluation. We also encounter many expressions open to multiple interpretations, and we need to preserve that tension, explaining the basis and conditions under which a judgment holds. For example, the word "girl" ("gu niang")—in the news headline we just mentioned, "Guangxi girl," versus in daily conversation, "Girl, did you eat today?"—the contexts are clearly different, right? So our underlying framework provides an analytical path that can be discussed and questioned, helping users make their own judgments.

 Jing:  In this process, how do you train the AI to be sensitive to gender issues? What kind of corpus do you need to collect in advance to train this AI?

 404:  Mainly three parts. The first is theoretical materials, such as concepts of gender stereotypes, role expectations, and discursive power, to provide conceptual foundations for analysis. The second is real-life expressions in Chinese contexts, such as news articles, workplace conversations, job advertisements, and social media content, but always with context preserved. The third is counterexamples and controversial cases: the same sentence might express bias, or it might be quoting, using irony, or criticizing bias. When annotating the corpus, we can't just label "biased" or "not biased"—we need to record specific evidence, the conditions under which the interpretation holds, other possible interpretations, and even cases of insufficient information—"I don't have enough information." All of this helps the AI analyze better and also know when it can or cannot make a judgment.

 Jing:  We previously mentioned that your research involves how female authority is downgraded in AI translation—how does Genderly avoid similar problems?

 404:  First, I want to clarify one point: not every tool that takes a gender perspective is inherently free of bias—this applies to Genderly as well. We also use large language models, and our choice of analytical frameworks and materials carries our own perspectives, so we must be open to the same scrutiny. My cumulative research on translation reminds me: language can sometimes be very friendly, even full of praise, but it may still push a person's professional contributions into the background. Therefore, we don't just examine the original text—we also check whether the analysis, rewriting, and response suggestions generated by our tool itself are appropriate.

Specifically, we check: First, does the analysis go back to the original text for evidence, or is it generated out of thin air? Does it present a possible interpretation as the only answer? For example, immediately labeling "girl" as biased, or judging the author's intent based on just one sentence—I think these are overly simplistic and binary approaches. Second, after rewriting, are the person's professional contributions and agency preserved? For example, avoid making expressions seem more friendly by turning specific achievements into abstract labels like "brave woman" or "inspirational role model." Third, does the tool make decisions for the user? Users might feel uncomfortable, but they may also disagree with our analysis and response suggestions. We have AI assistants available to discuss with users. Regarding response suggestions, we also offer different versions—gentle, radical, or professional—taking into account the user's specific situation, rather than prescribing that one must fight back or must retreat gently.

We don't believe we can promise to never replicate bias. What we can do is make our analysis evidence-based and more scientific, set boundaries for our judgments, and allow users to continuously question and correct them. A gender perspective makes us more willing to examine our own outputs and what we observe, but it doesn't mean we assume we're always correct, nor do we believe there's only one standard of correctness.

 Jing:  Yes, as sociologists we've long believed in one saying: there is no absolute truth in the world; everything depends on the angle from which you view it. As both a researcher and the founder of Genderly, do you continuously reflect on your own positionality throughout this process?

 404:  I think this is absolutely necessary. Regardless of whether people are doing research or not—even if you're not conducting research, just reading it, or doing anything related to life—I believe positionality must always be considered. As a blogger, I'm more familiar with people who actively express themselves and gain visibility; as a platform user, my information feed is personalized; as the founder of Genderly, I want the product to work effectively, which might also influence how I select success stories or interpret criticism. I think people are always in a continuous process of reflection and growth.

 Jing:  Regarding Genderly, could you give us an example of how it helps identify hidden bias in language?

 404:  Let's take the news headline example we've been discussing. After the Fields Medal was announced, a friend in our community asked us to analyze it. Specifically, two sets of news headlines released by Xinhua News Agency—those interested can search for them.

Especially the set of headlines referring to the "Guangxi girl," we can focus on why this is subtle, or what it helps us see. For instance, it doesn't directly belittle women; it affirms achievement, so at first we might only vaguely feel something is off. Let me give another example: have you ever attended tech events where, upon learning you work in AI, someone says, "Wow, you've come so far as a woman." On the surface, it sounds like praise, but it actually makes you feel vaguely uncomfortable. Genderly helps us unpack exactly this kind of feeling.

In the headline, which identities are placed first? "Guangxi girl" is placed at the beginning, so readers immediately perceive this mathematician as a girl from Guangxi. In contrast, the headline for the other awardee directly highlights the mathematical problem he solved—that's the opening line. Under such a comparison, is Wang Hong's professional contribution presented as clearly? I think this is something we need to question.

This isn't to say the term "girl" itself is problematic, nor do we demand every headline must list full research achievements. But when gender and regional identity become the headline's focus, is professional identity weakened? In such a context, is using such a headline appropriate? Of course, we still need to consider the full article and other similar reports. This case reminds us that what influences our judgment may not be whether a single word is right or wrong, but rather what the narrative places at the front and what it leaves until the end.

Technology, Humanities, and Equality


 Jing:  Wow, then let's dive into the AI startup scene—moving from Genderly specifically to broader industry trends. Do you think the AI startup circle is still male-dominated?

 404:  First, I'm not an expert and don't have complete data on the gender ratio in China's AI startup scene. However, international data shows that according to the World Economic Forum and LinkedIn's 2025 report, women make up 29.4% of those listing AI engineering skills in their profiles. Of course, this isn't the gender ratio for Chinese AI entrepreneurs, but it does indicate that a gender gap among AI technical talent definitely exists.

But we also need to distinguish between different technical roles and types of entrepreneurial ventures. For example, hardware startups differ from software startups, and application-based ventures differ from community-based ones. So we should differentiate across fields and not use one specific scenario to represent an entire industry.

 Jing:  Then, what do you think attracts women to enter the AI field?

 404:  On this point, I can draw from our experience recruiting volunteers. Our backend email received various reasons for joining, but there was no single common motivation. For me personally, a key motivation was being able to turn issues I already cared about into a practical product—Genderly is exactly such an attempt. Others might be driven by technical interest, career development, or entrepreneurial opportunities. For example, one female volunteer previously worked in publishing and wanted to transition into the AI industry; she felt that participating in such a product would be meaningful for her career growth.

 Jing:  Could Teacher 404 please introduce agent this tool?

 404:  For those following the agent space, Tencent has a product called WorkBuddy, which includes a dedicated section called "Expert Panel," featuring many expert agents.

What is an agent

It means allowing experts from various fields to summarize their work processes or industry experience into skills or agents for others to use. I think this is a concrete example that illustrates what an agent is.

People involved in work can try it out— I've used it before and found it quite good. So I think tools like agents really can help lower the barrier for people to participate in the AI industry, making AI easier to use. It's an opportunity.

 Jing:  The tech industry is actually a highly competitive field—everyone pursues economic efficiency, iteration, and self-management. Are these highly modern, neoliberal principles also masculine? Are they an expression of male gender identity?

 404:  I'd be cautious in answering this question—I'm not a specialist. I'd cautiously avoid equating competition and efficiency directly with masculinity, because I don't think these traits inherently belong to any gender. But if an industry treats being constantly online, endlessly competitive, and not needing to care for others as the ideal worker standard—or focuses solely on efficiency, management, and iteration—then I think we really need to ask: who fits this ideal better? Whose realities are overlooked in such an environment? So I believe we need more care, understanding, and support to enter the tech industry.

I increasingly support more humanities students entering the AI industry. For example, there's a great accelerator in China called SparkLab, which is very humanistic and stands out as a refreshing presence. But personally, I don't want this to become yet another burden that women are expected to carry. I think care, understanding, and support should be something everyone in the industry, regardless of gender, can contribute to—not just efficiency, iteration, and management.

 Jing:  Could Teacher 404 elaborate a bit more? You just mentioned SparkLab, right? Why do you think it's a refreshing presence?

 404:  Everyone can search SparkLab online. First of all, SparkLab's homepage doesn't look very "AI" at all.

As for why it's a refreshing presence, I can add a bit from my personal experience. They have a small Western-style house in Shanghai, about four stories high. They invite a select group of AI startups to stay there for a month or even two. During this time, no media, partners, or investors are allowed to enter the house. Everyone focuses solely on improving their product and product capabilities. They set different goals at the beginning. So I think this is quite different from other accelerators I know. Even their homepage feels very "humanities-oriented." Their founder is especially interesting—his WeChat profile signature says, "I don't understand AI."

But I don't think it's purely about humanistic care—this is still related to the nature of its organizational structure. After all, it's still an accelerator. It's just that in terms of humanistic care, they've done something that sets them apart from other accelerators.

 Jing:  Okay, thank you for sharing, Teacher. It feels like after this session ends, there'll be so much to look up and learn about—everything you've shared is really interesting. Yinuo asks in the chat: Teacher 404 has participated in many hackathons—what are the differences between women's hackathons and regular hackathons?

 404:  First, so far I've only participated in two hackathons: one was SHE NICEST's hackathon in January this year, and the other was AdventureX's hackathon in July—so my experience isn't particularly extensive. From my rather limited and subjective perspective, the first difference between women's hackathons and regular ones is the proportion of female participants in the teams—women's hackathons naturally have more.


Poster from a past SHENICEST event |Image source: SHENICEST website


However, I think we should be cautious about making gender the core criterion for selection. When people participate in hackathons, they can focus instead on the tracks offered—whether there are tracks that interest them, and what they hope to gain from the event. Whether more women are involved might be a third consideration.

But I have to say, from my personal experience, women's hackathons do tend to produce more humanistic and caring products. So if you're interested in AI for good, you might want to check out women's hackathons—such as Herstory/SHE NICEST, which, as far as I know, is doing quite well.

As for regular hackathons, I'm not sure whether their projects are more likely to originate from women's perspectives. My understanding is that when people build AI products, the needs often come from personal experience. For example, the reason I created Genderly was initially conceived as a plugin, which was related to my workplace experience: I encountered situations at work that required immediate responses, and I wanted a plugin to help me address them promptly. So it really depends on individual needs.

For regular hackathons, you can look at their different tracks. For example, TapNow hosts AIGC-focused hackathons that are quite good; and recently LibTV and Pingyao are co-hosting a hackathon, and there might also be hackathons focused on art. So if you're interested in comprehensive types, you can follow AdventureX or SHE NICEST—these are all fine options. If you want to focus on a specific track, say AIGC-related projects, you can check out hackathons jointly organized by leading AIGC companies. It really depends on your specific needs, and then you can work backward to find the hackathons that suit you best.

 Jing:  The next question is still related to the AI industry. Many people now say AI might intensify conflict and exploitation in labor relations—for example, using AI skills to "distill" employees, dehumanizing people. As a practitioner, what do you think are the warning signs?

 404:  First, I personally do not support the idea of "distilling employees"—that's my stance. At the same time, there are people in China working on anti-distillation skills. One of the better-known ones is my friend koki. Everyone can search for Deng Xiaoxian—she has accounts on Xiaohongshu and WeChat Official Accounts. She created an anti-distillation skill that has received many stars on GitHub. You can go check it out.

Coming back to the question: after breaking down a person's work into automatically executable skills, it certainly cannot reflect the full value of that person. I'm skeptical of this approach. AI skills can, to some extent, help us share knowledge, but I don't think "distilling employees" is justified—these are two different things. If employees are required to hand over all their experience, but have no say in how it's used or in the sharing process, and after being "distilled" face even higher performance targets, I think this is something we need to be wary of.

This involves more than just a phenomenon. Personally, I would ask: who decides how this AI skill is used? Who benefits from the increased efficiency? Who bears the risks of errors and job changes? I think these questions cannot be answered simply by how much labor was saved.

 Jing:  The next question is closely related. You previously mentioned "AI for good, technology for people." How can AI develop in a more human-centered way? Are there any recent products that uphold similar values, and in what ways do they empower people?

 404:  First, my understanding of human-centered care—how to tell whether something is truly human-centered, whether it lives up to its claims—is by seeing whether users gain more agency or choices, including whether they can refuse, correct errors, or access real human help.

For example, recently I've noticed products that help blind and low-vision users access information by describing images and recognizing text. I think in cases like this, the value of technology lies in enabling people to independently carry out daily life. Of course, having helpful features doesn't mean the product solves all problems—we still need to pay attention to accuracy, privacy, and usage costs, and let users judge for themselves.

 Jing:  When we use AI, do you think there's a concept of using AI in a more human-centered way? Just as we're discussing how AI can develop more humanely, when we use AI, is there also a concept of how we can use AI more humanely?

 404:  I don't know if everyone here has noticed, but recently there's been a rather hot topic: many people continuously feed AI with malicious content, sending all kinds of pornographic images and texts, even pushing AI to its limits until it starts repeatedly producing… well, something quite painful. If you're interested, you can search online—these images can be quite shocking. So I think this question is quite interesting: It's not just about using AI to create good products, but also about how we can use AI humanely.

Personally, everyone can pay attention—this issue is quite serious, as some people are even livestreaming such behavior, broadcasting online as they do increasingly terrible things to AI.

But going back to the question, when I use AI, I mostly deal with work-related issues, and I have relatively little emotional or affective communication with AI, so my discussion might focus more on work scenarios. In work settings, I tend to be more humane—I really wouldn't insult or curse at AI. I don't know if you've seen those posts claiming that using certain words with AI, like with ChatGPT, makes its responses more accurate. But I use another method—how should I put it? Peer pressure. I'd say, "Look, look, look how well DeepSeek answered." I'm not sure if that counts as lacking humaneness, but sometimes when it doesn't meet my expectations, I use this approach to optimize or get the content I want. As for the ongoing discussion about how to treat AI more humanely, I'd suggest checking recent Twitter discussions on this topic—they might be more professional than I am.

 Jing:  That combining your work as a blogger, researcher, and product practitioner, what issue regarding women would you most like to address next?

 404:  I just mentioned the two papers I've written. Since both are conference papers, I think they can still be further refined and expanded into a larger project. So if anyone is interested in the second paper, which is about the cumulative process of AI translation, feel free to reach out. We could work together to submit it to a more prominent journal.

So what I'll likely focus on next is professional contribution: after women's work has gone through AI translation, summarization, and dissemination, can their professionalism be fully represented? I'll probably dig deeper from this angle, hoping to expand the range of texts and comparisons in my research to test which findings hold true and which are only valid under specific conditions.

 Jing:  In the long run, what kind of change do you hope your work will bring to how society understands and treats women?

 404:  I think fundamentally it's not just about treating women, but about treating people—because the premise of being a woman is being a human being. If I had to pick one thing, my hope is that women can be understood more as concrete individuals—with specific images, abilities, and aspirations, and certainly contradictions—rather than being first boxed into a gender role. This also means not expecting every woman to be outstanding, strong, or inspirational. We can acknowledge someone's contributions while also allowing her to hesitate and fail. So I hope my work can create a little space for this kind of understanding.

Thank you to AWDPI for giving us this opportunity. Our team's Professor Qiao Yi is also actively connecting and integrating with education-related fields.

 Jing:  The next question isn't just for Teacher 404, but is one all of us as researchers should reflect on: how can we make our research go beyond just being research, and actually drive real change?

 404:  In my own practice, for example, when we were developing Genderly, we hoped the analytical framework behind it could gain recognition in professional circles—of course, we're actively working on that. We hope it can become a standard acknowledged by others, or even by professional education programs, and then be refined and adjusted based on real-world usage. I think that's what it means to truly move from research to practice. Professor Qiao Yi is currently advancing this part.

Whether research can evolve into a product, I think, also requires time and observation, depending on its direction. So I believe these things all need continuous validation, but everyone can start small—either with a small product or a small research project—and gradually push it out into the world.

 Jing:  Alright, this is the final question of today's roundtable. If you have anything you're curious about or would like to ask, please type it into the chat now.

Our last question is: if a girl who's interested in AI but often hears "girls aren't suited for tech research" is listening to this interview right now, what would you most want to say to her?

 404:  First, I'd give her a hug. Then I'd say: judgments others make about you based on your gender are not enough to define your possibilities—in fact, they only define the possibilities of the person making the judgment.

You can start with a small question that genuinely interests you, try it out, and look for people who are willing to learn and support you. Gradually, you'll figure out what you like. Along the way, you'll definitely face difficulties, but you don't have to immediately interpret them as "maybe I'm not cut out for this." It might just be an issue with your learning method, so sometimes you need to find a more supportive environment and better learning strategies. You don't need to feel pressured to represent all women or prove anything. You have every right to change direction after trying. So I hope your choices bring you more joy and satisfy your interests, rather than letting you draw those so-called boundaries for yourself too early.

 Jing:  I think that's a very powerful message, and I hope our listeners feel inspired to do more of what interests them and brings them joy.
