One

Overview of Asian Women in Brazil


The classification and characteristics of Asian women in Brazil can be explored from aspects such as their ethnic background, immigration history, and social status. Most Asian women are immigrants or descendants of immigrants, primarily concentrated in major cities such as São Paulo. Among them, Chinese women are an important part of the Asian community in Brazil. Their ancestors mainly migrated from Guangdong, China, to Brazil at the end of the 19th and beginning of the 20th centuries, initially engaging mostly in agriculture and commerce. In recent years, with China's economic rise, an increasing number of Chinese women are playing significant roles in Brazil's economic and cultural spheres. Secondly, Japanese-Brazilian women are also relatively numerous; Brazil has the largest population of Japanese descendants outside Japan. Their immigration history dates back to the early 20th century, when they primarily worked in agriculture, especially coffee farming. Today, Japanese-Brazilian women excel in fields such as education, healthcare, and business management (Aljazeera, 2021). Korean immigrants mostly arrived in Brazil in the late 20th century, primarily working in trade and service industries, and are characterized by their strong emphasis on education and family (Buzzfeed, 2022).


Overall, the number of Asian women in Brazil is growing, the community is expanding, and their level of education is continuously rising. As society places greater emphasis on diversity and inclusion, the representation of Asian women across various industries is gradually increasing (Agência de Notícias IBGE, 2022).



Education Level

Brazilian women generally have a higher level of education, especially in higher education. According to 2018 statistics, 18% of Brazilian men aged 25 to 34 had received higher education, while the proportion among women in the same age group reached 25% (China News Service, 2018). In addition, the average years of schooling for Brazilian women is 10.1 years, compared to 9.6 years for men, showing women's advantage in education (China2Brazil, 2023). The OECD's "Education at a Glance 2022" report indicates that 52% of women in Brazil graduate within three years of completing a bachelor's degree program, compared to 44% of men. In higher education, Asian descendants perform particularly well. According to a 2009 survey conducted by the University of São Paulo, although Japanese Brazilians make up only 1.2% of São Paulo city's population and less than 4% of university entrance exam takers, they account for around 15% of those who pass the exam. Children from Chinese Brazilian families also tend to achieve excellent academic results, with many entering top universities in Brazil (Americas Quarterly, 2019).



Employment Situation

Overall, the labor force participation rate of Brazilian women has increased over the past few decades, rising from 54% in 2000 to 62% in 2019 (Mukhopadhyay, 2023). However, their employment outcomes do not match their educational attainment. Data shows that the employment rate for Brazilian women aged 25 to 34 with higher education is 82%, while it drops to 63% for those with only technical education, and is only 45% for women without any higher education (China News Service, 2018). Furthermore, according to the International Labour Organization (ILO), women still have fewer employment opportunities and lower wages than men (ILO, 2018). Statistics indicate that Brazil is one of the countries with the largest gender pay gaps in Latin America and the Caribbean. In 2022, the average monthly income for women was 617 Brazilian reais, far below that of men (Statista, 2022). For Asian women, although they have higher employment rates in technical and professional fields (OECD, 2022) and earn significantly more than white women in self-employment (Tiefenthaler, 1997), they still face many challenges. For example, racial discrimination experienced by Japanese-Brazilians at work negatively affects their career well-being (Takenoshita, 2006).



Intergenerational Differences

Asian Brazilians demonstrate high intergenerational mobility in education and occupation, especially in higher education and professional fields. (Garcias and Kassouf, 2021) . This mobility is reflected not only in increased income but also in greater diversity of career choices and improved job stability. The first generation, through persistent effort, accumulated certain economic capital, providing better educational opportunities and resources for the next generation (Ferreira and Veloso, 2006). At the same time, they built social capital through family, community networks, and social connections, helping their children access more internships, job referrals, and career guidance (Takenoshita, 2013). As a result, compared to the first generation, second-generation immigrants have seen significant improvements in employment competitiveness and income levels (Garcias and Kassouf, 2021).


For example, first-generation immigrant women often work in low-skilled, low-income jobs such as food service, retail, and cleaning. Although these jobs are demanding and offer limited income, they provide stable financial support for their families (Morita, 2019). Over time, with the accumulation of economic and social capital, their children—the second and third generations of Asian women—have made significant progress in education and careers.


Two

Workplace Treatment and Rights Protection



Basic Labor Rights

In Brazil, foreign workers must obtain a work permit (Autorização de Trabalho) to work legally. Applicants must hold a valid passport (Multiplier, 2024), provide proof of at least two years of relevant work experience or a university degree in a related field (Rippling, 2024), and submit a work contract with a Brazilian company specifying job responsibilities, salary, and employment duration (Europortage, 2024). It should be noted that the work permit is generally applied for by the employer (K&L Gates, 2024). Only after the work permit is approved can the applicant proceed to apply for the corresponding work visa (VITEM V). All documents must be in Portuguese and notarized (Multiplier, 2024).


Once immigrants obtain a work permit, Brazil's Consolidated Labor Laws (CLT) protect the basic rights of all workers, including immigrants. Key labor rights include:


(1) Working hours: The standard working day is 8 hours, with a weekly limit of 44 hours (Monteiro, 2019); overtime must be compensated at an additional rate, typically 150% of the normal wage (Boundless EOR, 2024).

(2) Minimum wage: The statutory minimum wage in Brazil was set at 1,412 Brazilian reais per month (approximately 282 USD) in 2024, effective from January 1, 2024 (WageIndicator, 2024).

(3) Work protection and safety: Employers must provide a safe working environment and comply with occupational health and safety standards (MTE, 2024); any form of workplace discrimination, including based on race, gender, age, religion, and nationality, is prohibited (Monteiro, 2019).


Although Brazil's labor laws provide extensive protections on paper for all workers, including migrant workers, there are significant shortcomings in actual enforcement. Solida A report by rity Center (2024) shows that many migrant workers report having their wages illegally withheld or not paid on time. Moreover, although Brazil passed the Federal Gender Pay Gap Law in 2023, requiring large companies to report wage disparities every six months, many migrant women still cannot receive equal pay compared to local workers (JDSupra, 2023). Another issue that cannot be ignored is that migrant workers face certain restrictions in accessing public sector employment. Although they may enjoy equal rights with citizens in the private sector or as self-employed individuals, when it comes to public sector jobs, they often do not receive the same treatment or opportunities as local citizens (Migration Data Portal, 2024).



Social Security

Brazil's social security system also grants migrant workers the same rights as Brazilian citizens, ensuring they enjoy basic economic and social security (UNHCR, 2024) .


in work and life. First, all migrant workers employed in Brazil must register and pay social security contributions to access benefits such as pensions, disability allowances, and other welfare programs (DLA Piper, 2024). These contributions are shared between employers and employees, with the employee's portion deducted directly from monthly wages at rates varying by income level; the employer's contribution is typically 20% or 22.5% of the employee's salary, depending on the industry (Boundless EOR, 2024). Additionally, self-employed individuals are also required to pay social security taxes (PWC, 2024).


As long as migrant workers are legally employed and have paid social security contributions, they are eligible for pensions. Brazil's pension system consists of three main schemes (Abrapp, 2024). The first is the public mandatory system (RGPS), also known as the general social security system, which covers workers in the private sector. This system operates on a pay-as-you-go (PAYG) model, funded by payroll taxes (OECD, 2021). The second is the public servants' pension system, specifically designed for civil servants, which typically offers more generous benefits than the RGPS (OECD, 2021). The third is the private pension system (RPC), including occupational and individual plans, usually managed by employers or individuals through financial institutions (Deloitte, 2024). It should be noted that workers must meet a minimum contribution period (usually 15 years) and the statutory retirement age to receive a pension (PWC, 2024).


Migrant workers who become unemployed can apply for unemployment benefits (Seguro-Desemprego), but must meet a series of conditions. First, applicants must have worked continuously for at least 12 months within the 24 months prior to unemployment and have paid social security contributions during this period. Second, applicants must register as job seekers immediately after becoming unemployed and demonstrate that they are actively seeking new employment. Applicants must also prove that the unemployment was involuntary, such as due to company layoffs or contract expiration, rather than voluntary resignation (Deloitte, 2024; OECD, 2021).


For migrant workers with children, the Brazilian government also provides family allowance (Salário-Família). The amount of this allowance depends on household income and the number of children. Specifically, the lower the household income, the higher the allowance received (Deloitte, 2024). Additionally, families receiving this benefit must meet certain basic conditions, such as ensuring their children attend school regularly and undergo routine health check-ups (PWC, 2024). This allowance system not only effectively reduces the financial burden on families but also actively encourages families to prioritize children's education and health through conditional cash transfers. This approach plays a positive role in breaking the intergenerational cycle of poverty (Siqueira et al., 2013; Veras & Oliveira, 2018). Such policies not only reflect the Brazilian government's concern for vulnerable groups, especially children's well-being, but also demonstrate its emphasis on investment in education and health.


In terms of healthcare, migrant workers can access free medical services through Brazil's public health system (Sistema Único de Saúde, SUS), including basic medical care, preventive services, and emergency care (UNHCR, 2024). If a migrant worker is injured at work or suffers from an occupational illness, they may apply for sickness benefit (Auxílio-Doença). If the injury is caused by employer negligence or unsafe working conditions, the employer must provide additional financial compensation (Massuda et al., 2018).


Notably, many migrants are forced to work in the informal economy due to various reasons. In such circumstances, they often lack formal employment contracts and legal protection, which directly leads to significant difficulties in accessing formal social protection benefits. (International Labour Organization, 2017) . This situation not only affects their quality of life but also reveals shortcomings in society's protection of migrant rights. Moreover, this issue is particularly pronounced among women and Afro-Brazilian migrants (Bastos et al., 2017). Additionally, racial health disparities remain a significant challenge in Brazil. Many remote and impoverished areas suffer from inadequate medical facilities and scarce healthcare resources, making it difficult for ethnic minorities living in these regions to access timely and effective medical care (Massuda et al., 2018).



Leave policies

Migrant workers in Brazil are entitled to the same leave policies as national citizens.


1.

Basic leave


According to Brazil's Labor Code, all employees are entitled to 30 days of paid annual leave after completing 12 months of work (Ogletree Deakins, 2024). Employees may divide their annual leave into up to three segments, provided that one segment is at least 14 days long. Brazil also observes several national public holidays, such as New Year's Day, Carnival, Easter, Labor Day, and Christmas. Special paid leave may also be granted in exceptional circumstances, such as marriage, the death of a family member, or illness (Boundless EOR, 2024). During sick leave, workers continue to receive wages, with the specific duration and compensation level depending on the worker's length of employment and medical condition (Europortage, 2024).


2.

Maternity and paternity leave


Brazil's maternity and paternity leave policies legally guarantee corresponding paid leave for all employed workers, ensuring that new parents have time to care for their newborns and adjust to family life.


The Labor Code divides the leave granted to female employees during pregnancy and postpartum into two parts. The first is basic maternity leave. All female employees are entitled to 120 days (approximately four months) of paid maternity leave, during which their wages are covered by the social security system (Monteiro et al., 2019). The second is extended maternity leave. Companies participating in the "Empresa Cidadã" (Corporate Citizen) program may offer employees an additional 60 days of maternity leave, extending the total maternity leave to 180 days (approximately six months). During this extended period, employees continue to receive full pay (Boundless EOR, 2024). The timing of maternity leave is flexible—mothers may begin their leave up to 28 days before the expected delivery date or after the baby's birth (Europortage, 2024).


After returning to work, mothers have the right to two 30-minute breaks per day for breastfeeding or milk expression. This provision applies during the first six months after the child's birth (Rimes et al., 2019). During maternity leave and for five months after its conclusion, female employees are protected from dismissal, and employers may not terminate their employment for any reason (MTE, 2024). Furthermore, pregnant migrant women workers are entitled to maternity allowance (Salário-Maternidade), which includes prenatal and postnatal medical care as well as wage compensation during maternity leave (MTE, 2024).


In addition, paternity leave policies support new fathers' involvement in family life. This policy consists of two parts: the first is basic paternity leave. All male employees are entitled to five days of paid paternity leave following the birth of their child, during which wages are paid by the employer (Boundless EOR, 2024). The second is extended paternity leave. Companies participating in the "Empresa Cidadã" program may offer employees an additional 15 days of paternity leave, bringing the total to 20 days. During this period, male employees receive full pay (Global Expansion, 2024).


WBL Index

According to the *Women, Business and the Law 2024* report, Brazil's WBL index score is 85.0. This score reflects significant progress in the country's legal environment for promoting women's economic participation, yet certain challenges remain. First, although Brazil has relatively strong legal protections in areas such as mobility, workplace rights, and pay, legal gaps persist in marriage, women's rights, and pensions (World Bank, 2024). Moreover, while Brazil's legal framework offers some support in entrepreneurship and asset ownership, in practice, many women face obstacles such as difficulty accessing financing, lack of social support, and market entry barriers, all of which limit their economic activity (Pandey, 2023). Research also indicates that cultural factors play a significant role in influencing the WBL index. Traditional beliefs and gender role divisions still exist within Brazilian sociocultural contexts, negatively affecting the practical impact and enforcement of laws to some extent. (Malaquias et al., 2022) .


It should be noted that although the WBL index provides a useful framework for assessing the legal environment for women's economic participation, it does not conduct detailed investigations specifically targeting foreign workers, and therefore, further research and data collection are needed regarding the specific circumstances of Asian women.


III

Marginalization and Discrimination


Although the Brazilian government has introduced various policies aimed at strongly supporting migrant workers, the reality falls short of expectations. Migrant workers continue to face numerous challenges when integrating into Brazil's labor market. Among these, language barriers, cultural differences, and social prejudice have become obstacles preventing them from securing stable employment (Siqueira et al., 2013). Particularly for refugees and undocumented migrants, employment conditions are even more difficult, often limited to low-paid, unstable jobs (Veras & Oliveira, 2018).


Furthermore, Brazil's overall economic situation and labor market conditions profoundly affect the integration process of migrant workers. In recent years, weak economic growth and persistently high unemployment rates have intensified competition between local and migrant workers (Hering & Paillacar, 2016). In this challenging employment environment, Asian female migrant workers face multiple layers of marginalization and discrimination, manifested in the following aspects:



Racial bias and stereotypes

Asian women are often perceived as "outsiders," even if they were born and raised in Brazil. Specifically, the physical characteristics of Asian women—such as skin color, eye shape, and hair type—visually distinguish them from Brazil's mainstream population (primarily white and mixed-race groups), marking them as "non-local" in society. Moreover, although many Asian women speak fluent Portuguese from childhood, some people assume their native language is an Asian language and regard their Portuguese as merely "a foreign language learned," a linguistic bias that further reinforces their "outsider" identity. At the same time, Brazilian society holds certain cultural stereotypes about Asian communities, such as viewing Asians as hardworking, introverted, and socially reserved. This "outsider" status exposes them to invisible racial bias in the workplace. For example, studies show that Asian women are often excluded during hiring processes, even when they possess qualifications equivalent to other candidates (Siqueira et al., 2013). Moreover, even when their education and work experience are comparable to those of male colleagues, their wages remain significantly lower. Employers may also underestimate their abilities and potential during recruitment and promotion due to stereotypes (Gerard et al., 2021). Research indicates that Brazil's labor market exhibits significant racial wage gaps, affecting not only between Black and white workers but also between Asians and other racial groups (Leite, 2008).



Gender discrimination and occupational segregation

Statistics show that women hold less than 13% of managerial positions in Brazil (LatAm Journalism Review, 2021). Although this data does not specifically differentiate Asian women, it can be inferred that the proportion of Asian women in managerial roles is likely even lower. The reason is that Asian women face both racial exclusion and gender-based occupational segregation and wage disparities. They are often confined to specific industries and positions, such as service sectors and low-skilled jobs, which typically offer low wages and limited advancement opportunities. This not only restricts their economic independence but also deprives them of voice and influence in the workplace. Within Asian cultural contexts, family responsibilities are often seen as women's primary duty, placing additional burdens on Asian women as they navigate their careers. They must not only confront workplace challenges but also balance family obligations with professional development. These cultural expectations undoubtedly intensify the challenges of work-life balance, thereby affecting their career advancement (Klasen, 2019). This dual pressure requires Asian women to exert greater effort in the workplace to overcome multiple barriers and achieve their professional goals. Additionally, Asian women frequently experience microaggressions in the workplace—seemingly unintentional but discriminatory remarks or actions, such as inappropriate jokes, derogatory comments, or unfair work assignments (Massuda et al., 2018).


Encouragingly, Asian women have adopted various strategies and efforts in response to racial discrimination and marginalization. Many have enhanced their competitiveness by improving their education levels and professional skills. Against the backdrop of Brazil's relatively low overall higher education enrollment rate, Asian women's enrollment and completion rates are significantly higher than the national average (ResearchGate, 2018). At the same time, social media has become an important tool for Asian women to showcase their abilities and build professional brands. They create groups on social media platforms such as Reddit and Facebook—for example, the "Brazilian Women Professional Network" group on LinkedIn and the "Asian Women in Business" group on Facebook—actively sharing knowledge, participating in industry discussions and cross-cultural exchange activities, building connections with women from other ethnic and cultural backgrounds, and collectively addressing challenges in the workplace (Adeleke et al., 2020), thereby gaining greater support and opportunities in their careers.
