On the afternoon of September 27, 2025, a film screening event on the theme of combating domestic violence, hosted by the Asian Women Development Plan International (AWDPI) Australia National Office, successfully concluded in Melbourne!


The event was facilitated by the AWDPI Australia Cooperation Development Lead, Aizi Chang and featured a special guest speaker, Tina Ji, partner at Central Land Lawyers, who delivered an in-depth presentation integrating real-life cases, legal provisions, film analysis, and practical advice. The screening attracted nearly 40 online and in-person attendees, and received an enthusiastic response on site.

Film and Theme Recap


The screening was carefully curated by AWDPI Australia cooperation development members, Ricky Wang who selected several iconic scenes from the film *Kim Ji-young, Born 1982*, to spark meaningful discussion.


The film, through its delicate storytelling and female perspective, reveals the hidden "inequality" and psychological oppression within families and society, resonating deeply with the audience.



Focusing on scenes from the film relevant to Australian family law, Tina Ji the speaker provided professional legal analysis on three key themes:


  • Verbal degradation and psychological harm

  • Economic control and family power dynamics

  • Seeking help and practical pathways


During the Q&A session, many in-person attendees shared personal experiences and asked for concrete, real-world guidance. The atmosphere was sincere and open, with many participants finding strength through dialogue, and gaining a deeper awareness that:


domestic violence does not only occur in cases of extreme physical violence;

any relationship dynamic that makes someone feel

controlled, fearful, or degraded

deserves to be seriously heard and addressed.


This screening was not only a fusion of law and cinema, but also a collective conversation about understanding, empathy, and courage.


Three Thought-Provoking Topics on Combating Domestic Violence


Drawing on Australian family law and real court cases, Tina Ji  three issues worthy of deeper reflection were shared.


The Cross-Cultural Nature of Domestic Violence | 1


Domestic violence is not an issue confined to a single culture; it is often interpreted and judged differently across cultural contexts.


As a result, we may fall into "taken-for-granted" misconceptions about behaviors occurring in real life..


For example:

  • Does a husband's infidelity constitute a "moral flaw" that can expedite divorce proceedings?

  • Can verbal degradation and emotional attacks, if causing severe psychological trauma, be considered evidence of domestic violence?

  • When a husband demands his wife quit her job to care for the family under the guise of "love," does this constitute "economic control" under the law?


When sharing a case she once handled, Tina Ji  she noted: many behaviors considered "invisible harm" in East Asian cultures may not be interpreted the same way under Australian law. Judges from different cultural backgrounds may have varying standards for defining concepts such as "fear," "control," and "threat."


She particularly emphasized:


under Australian law, whether an act constitutes "domestic violence"
depends not only on the behavior itself,
but also on whether it makes the person feel afraid, controlled, or threatened.


In other words, certain words regarded in East Asian cultures as mere "sarcasm" or "emotional expression" may not be legally defined as domestic violence if they do not cause a clear sense of fear. Because of these cross-cultural differences in understanding, many victims often lose opportunities for protection due to "cultural mismatch" when seeking help or gathering evidence..


This is precisely the core mission and direction of AWDPI Australia's ongoing advocacy against domestic violence:

we aim to support every victim of domestic violence from a cross-cultural background so they are no longer overlooked due to cultural misunderstandings, ensuring every voice in need of being heard is truly seen by the law and society.


How to Legally Gather Evidence | 2

During the section on evidence collection, Tina Ji she used real cases to explain in detail the types of evidence commonly accepted by Australian family courts in domestic violence cases. She emphasized that collecting evidence in a reasonable and lawful manner is a crucial step in ensuring victims' rights are recognized.


Commonly accepted forms of evidence include:

  • photographs (e.g., injuries)

  • screenshots of text messages, WeChat conversations, or emails

  • audio or video recordings

  • witness statements

  • medical records, psychological evaluations, or GP diagnosis reports


These materials often provide strong support for victims in court. However, Tina Ji particularly stressed the importance of legality in the evidence-gathering process.


She reminded attendees:

when collecting evidence, never use methods that may violate laws or privacy regulations.

Safety and legality in evidence collection must always come first.


For example, viral videos of "confronting a mistress" may attract public attention online, but such actions could lead the victim to break the law in Australia. As for whether secret recordings or covert filming can be admitted as evidence, this is subject to judicial discretion based on the severity of the case. In some instances, courts may accept such evidence, but privacy risks and legal disputes may also arise.


Accessing Support and Protection | 3


During the Q&A, the AWDPI Australia team and Tina Ji highlighted that Australia offers diverse protection mechanisms for victims, including:

  • free translation services

  • legal consultation and aid

  • psychological support and counseling

  • post-crisis accommodation and protection


These systems provide important institutional safeguards, enabling victims to gradually recover with dual support from the legal and social systems.


However, these mechanisms do not always function seamlessly in practice. In real cases, victims often face long waiting times for assistance, limited responsiveness of resources, and insufficient cultural sensitivity. For individuals from diverse cultural backgrounds—especially Asian women—lack of cultural sensitivity, language barriers, and difficulties in building trust after psychological trauma may cause hesitation or confusion when seeking help.


For this reason, AWDPI has consistently worked to build bridges between systems and individuals. Through cross-cultural communication support, psychological care, legal resource linkage, and community companionship, we hope every Asian woman facing hardship will no longer feel alone or isolated by language or culture.


Conclusion


This film screening was not merely an interpretation of a movie, but a profound dialogue about understanding, respect, and protection. 


The AWDPI Australia National Office will continue to advance cross-cultural anti-domestic violence efforts through advocacy, education, and practical action, ensuring every woman can live in safety, dignity, and care, and receive the support and strength she deserves.
