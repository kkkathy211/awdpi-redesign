Film Introduction:

"Prima Facie" is a one-woman play written by Australian playwright Suzie Miller, which premiered in 2019 at the Stables Theatre in Sydney. It then made its international debut in 2022 at the Harold Pinter Theatre in London's West End, following its Australian premiere. This production starred renowned actress Jodie Comer, marking her stage debut.


The story centers on Tessa Ensler, a young and brilliant defense lawyer. Tessa is an experienced and sharp-tongued British barrister who has achieved repeated success in her career through exceptional advocacy skills. In court, she firmly believes in the rationality and neutrality of the law—until one day, wearing a green dress, she becomes intoxicated and is sexually assaulted by Julian, a male colleague she knows. This incident leaves her shocked and hesitant, but she ultimately decides to report it.


It is the first time in her life that she stands on what was once her "opposite side"—the victim's position—re-examining the law and herself. However, as she enters the police station, undergoes questioning, and cooperates with the investigation, she gradually discovers that her statement is deemed "insufficient evidence, no case to answer" within the legal framework. In this process, she begins to realize the fragility of victims' voices within the justice system..


When standing at the intersection of power structures, burden of proof, and moral expectations, each case she once won may have meant that certain voices were systematically suppressed..


Constructed truth.




The title "Prima Facie," derived from Latin, means "at first glance" or "based on initial appearance." This legal term refers to facts presumed to be true based on surface-level evidence.

 

Playwright Suzie Miller mentioned in interviews that the original title for the play was "On the Face of It," but she later changed it to "Prima Facie" because the Latin phrase more directly reflects the protagonist Tessa's experience: as a defense lawyer, she is accustomed to advocating for defendants based on prima facie evidence, but when she becomes a survivor of sexual assault, she discovers that the legal system treats victims in ways vastly different from her prior understanding.


Miller stated in interviews that she hopes this work reveals the subjectivity and limitations of the legal system in handling sexual assault cases, as well as the difficulties survivors face in seeking justice..


In the play, Tessa did not come from a wealthy background. When she was admitted to Cambridge University, the dean warned her and the other freshmen: "Look to your left, then look to your right—one-third of you will not graduate." This meant that in this elite institution, there was more competition than support among peers. Even someone as intelligent as Tessa could not avoid falling into self-doubt and timidity, even wondering if she would be the one eliminated. However, many years later, she finally grew into a confident and spirited, successful female lawyer who believes in the legal system.


She skillfully controls courtroom rhythms, excels at linking evidence into a complete chain, and knows how to guide witnesses into giving statements favorable to her side. At that time, she believed truth was not an objective, inherent existence, but rather a legal narrative that could be constructed, shaped, and ultimately accepted. In one case, when an intern asked the defendant during communication, "Honestly, did you really do it?" she immediately interrupted: "You're dancing on the edge of ethics. You can't ask if he really did it—just go by what he says."


This is precisely her understanding of "truth in law"—not the subjective "reality" experienced by victims, but the version of truth recognized and admitted by the legal system and accepted by the court.


The symbolism of the green dress



In many theatrical and cinematic works, the green dress has quietly become a turning point in the fate of certain women. It is not merely a combination of fabric and color, but a prop rich with deep metaphor in aesthetics. This prop symbolizes the threshold at which women enter a different state of life.


For example, in the American film *Gone with the Wind*, adapted from the novel by female author Margaret Mitchell, the protagonist Scarlett O'Hara, after enduring a series of sufferings and trials, quickly awakens—"Marriage is not just a romantic union, but a means of survival." So she puts on a green dress made from curtains and tells herself: "Tomorrow is another day."


Another example: Camille Doncieux, the young wife in Monet's early painting *The Woman in the Green Dress*, whose fate became tied to the ups and downs of her husband's career from the moment she wore that green dress. Ultimately unable to endure, she died in poverty in 1879 at the age of 32.


When love is worn down by reality and freedom is stripped by society, their marriages plunge them into both material and spiritual poverty. Thus, under constant "exploitation," no one offers these women any way out.


Until that night when she wore the green dress, Tessa reached her own turning point—not a romantic beginning, but yet another refined act of "sexual exploitation" under class-based power imbalance..


In the book *Iris's First Love Paradise*, the scene that ultimately drives Iris to madness is unforgettable to most readers. To intimidate another victimized girl, Mr. Li uses scout ropes to bind the exhausted, naked Iris into the shape of a crab. Iris feels as if her soul has detached; she floats in mid-air, watching her own body. She goes mad. She says, "Good, my soul is leaving my body. I will forget this humiliation, and when I return, I will be whole again."


In *Prima Facie*, there is a strikingly similar moment. During the sexual assault, as Julian pins down Tessa's hands and covers her mouth while committing the act, Tessa says: "I feel myself leave my body. I am there, but I am not."


The beastly acts of the perpetrators, the trampled dignity, the ensuing shame, judgment, and doubt. The pain is real, undeniable. But fortunately—Tessa's soul finally returns to her body.


She chooses to walk into the police station. But after a series of recollections, interrogations, and psychological turmoil, she can no longer calmly and professionally state the legal definition of rape as a lawyer. Instead, she repeatedly whispers, "I don't know," "I'm not sure"—vague words that feel like a self-protective mechanism. She must personally swallow the full bitterness of these words to truly understand their weight.


It is precisely at this moment that she begins to waver and question the legal system she once loved and relied upon.


The law has never truly understood women.


The "truth" the law pursues is not equivalent to objective truth, but rather the truth admissible in court. This kind of "truth" is highly formalized. It demands—witness testimony must be clear and consistent, case narratives logically coherent, and emotional factors must not interfere with judgment. The particularity of sexual assault cases lies in how they inherently disrupt the neat narrative the law desires. Victims often experience, fragmented memories due to fear, shame, and trauma. Moreover, women are often labeled as "illogical" due to their post-assault behaviors, such as not reporting, not resisting, or continuing contact.



This leads to a ridiculous paradox: The "truth" required by the law in sexual assault cases is precisely what victims cannot provide. The law does not lie—only that its so-called "truth" is not allowed to carry human colors.


Through the sieve of these institutional systems, what remains are fragments filled with a woman's choked whisper of "no," the limpness and numbness in the bathroom, the countless silent nights of humiliation. The law's truth is clear, but the truth of rape is chaotic. The former demands linear memory; the latter requires constantly rebuilding the self from a field of ruins.


Finally, after countless painful days and nights, Tessa stands in court. She repeatedly questions herself internally: "Did I resist?" "Was I too drunk?" "Why couldn't I escape?" She tries to attribute the shame to herself. But she fails to realize that these questions are no longer judgments of fact, but a form of self-attack quietly implanted in her consciousness by society. At certain moments, the whole society seems to whisper softly: "You are guilty." You drank, so you are guilty. You wore makeup, so you are guilty. You dressed provocatively, so you are guilty.


This world is too skilled at making women "responsible" for their own wounds. Thus, the perpetrator's guilt is gradually stripped away and draped over the victim's fragile shoulders. Shame is internalized, becoming part of a woman's body, as instinctive as the flow of blood. No one has the right to use the saying "a gentleman does not stand under a crumbling wall" to lecture survivors. The phrase emphasizes self-protection, not the justification of harm. Even if someone "stands under the wall," the real question is not "why stand there," but "who is pushing the wall."


In the second half of the play, Tessa declares in court: "I'm in a peculiar position now. Usually I stand at the defense bench, but today I stand here as the plaintiff, the witness, the victim." She looks around—everyone is male. Even the person guiding her to her seat is a man. The entire structure of the system, from trial and investigation to debate and the criteria for judging "credibility," seems to be a script written by men.


"Sexual assault cases revolve on the wrong axis," she says. Because the law has never truly understood how women remember, how their bodies react, or the silence born of shame. And when courts still demand a "neat and complete chain of evidence," they are in fact demanding that a woman's trauma must "qualify" for its legal form. She says, "I see all the women who came here before me, and all those who will come after."


She points at us beyond the screen. She shouts: "One in three women has experienced sexual assault. Look to your left, look to your right—she is one of us." Just as her college dean once said upon her arrival at Cambridge: "Look to your left, then to your right—only one in three of you will graduate." But she is no longer the timid, insecure girl she once was. She shouts: "The legal system made me look like a liar. It broke me apart. I am broken, but I am still here. I will not be silent. Some things must change."


After immense trauma, she rebuilds a new truth from the ruins of shattered beliefs: "Not the perfection of logic, but the honesty of one's emotions; not a consistent narrative, but a truthful response rooted in trauma." Emotion and pain are, for the victim, evidence that lingers like a shadow.
