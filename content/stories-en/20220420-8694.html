Overview of Domestic Violence Against Women in Thailand


1

Background of Research on Domestic Violence


According to the latest data from the World Health Organization (WHO), during their lifetime, one in three women (approximately 736 million) experience physical or psychological violence from an intimate partner, or sexual violence from a non-partner. Among young women aged 15–24, one in four has experienced violence from an intimate partner.


However,

these figures represent only the reported tip of the iceberg;,

the actual numbers may be even higher..

With the global pandemic of COVID-19,,

incidents of violence have intensified..


source:Freepik


The United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA) estimates that six months of lockdown, during which women are isolated at home with violent partners or family members, could lead to an additional 31 million cases of violence against women and girls. This report will focus on domestic violence against women in Thailand, aiming to present the social significance of this issue through contextual illustration and conceptual analysis, and to offer recommendations for addressing the challenges faced by abused women, with the hope that summarizing this experience may provide reference for women in similar situations in other countries.



2

Explanation of Domestic Violence Issues


Definition of Domestic Violence in Thailand

According to Thailand's current 2007 Anti-Domestic Violence Act, "domestic violence refers to any intentional act that harms or is likely to harm the physical, mental, or health condition of a family member, including acts that intentionally cause physical, mental, or health damage, as well as acts that use force or violence contrary to ethics and morality to compel a family member to do or refrain from doing something, or to accept behavior they dislike, excluding negligence." Here, "family members" include spouses, former spouses, unmarried cohabiting partners or former cohabitants, children, adopted children, and other individuals living together in the same household.


Domestic Violence in Thailand


Thailand is listed by the United Nations as one of the ten countries with the highest rates of domestic violence. From 2008 to 2016, Thailand averaged around 1,000 domestic violence cases per year. The most sensational case was the 2013 murder of Jakkrit. Jakkrit was a Thai shooting athlete who represented Thailand at both the Beijing and London Olympic Games. He had long subjected his wife to domestic violence, driving her to hire a hitman to assassinate him. This case prompted greater public attention to domestic violence in Thai society.


source:Freepik


Forms of domestic violence in Thailand mainly include physical violence (such as shoving, punching, kicking, and other physical harm), psychological violence (such as mocking, verbal abuse, belittling, emotional neglect), sexual violence (such as forced sexual relations), and economic violence (such as restricting a partner's financial autonomy) (Liu Lulian, 2019). According to statistics from Mahidol University based on a national survey of 2,280 households, 34.6% of women or other family members experienced domestic violence, including 32.3% experiencing psychological violence, 10% physical violence, and 5% sexual violence.



The causes of domestic violence are relatively complex,

but in Thailand,

traditional cultural factors are particularly prominent.



During the Ayutthaya period, the 1359 Marriage Law stipulated that a wife was the husband's property. Thailand's first systematic legal code, the Three Seals Code, clearly classified wives into three categories: principal wife, concubine, and sex slave. Men were allowed to have extramarital affairs, while women committing adultery would face severe punishment. Polygamy was not abolished in Thailand until 1930. Moreover, although Thailand is a Buddhist country, since 1928, women have not been allowed to undergo ordination as bhikkhunis (fully ordained nuns) in Thailand. A spokesperson for the National Office of Buddhism stated: "No one prevents women from going abroad to receive ordination as bhikkhunis, but they cannot do so within Thailand, and Thai monks cannot conduct the ordination ceremony. That's just how it is." These value systems have persisted to the present day and have significantly contributed to the prevalence of domestic violence in Thai families.


source:Freepik


3

Significance of Research on Domestic Violence


Violence against women and children is one of the most widespread human rights violations. A multi-country study conducted by the World Health Organization in 2005 found that 15%–70% of women reported having experienced physical or sexual violence from an intimate partner. Domestic violence, especially violence against women, has become a matter of international concern. The United Nations has adopted international instruments such as the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women and the Declaration on the Elimination of Violence Against Women, calling on countries worldwide to take concrete measures to eliminate domestic violence and discrimination against women. (The Anti-Domestic Violence Law of the People's Republic of China, 2015)


Thailand has a tradition of polygamy, with men holding higher status than women in the family. Domestic violence has long been regarded as a private family matter, and due to the deep influence of Buddhist teachings, both perpetrators and victims remain silent about domestic violence. Under the combined influence of these factors, Thailand has long been one of the countries in Southeast Asia with high rates of domestic violence. According to reports from China News Service, domestic violence cases in Thailand surged during the pandemic. "Home quarantine," originally a measure to combat the COVID-19 pandemic, according to statistics from Thai government organizations, has led to an increase in domestic violence cases during the pandemic due to people spending prolonged periods at home.


source:Freepik


As a participating country in the Belt and Road Initiative, Thailand is a partner of China and an important link in building a community of shared future characterized by political mutual trust, cultural inclusiveness, and economic mutual assistance. Chinese women and migrant groups living in Thailand, as important participants in economic activities, deserve attention and recognition.


Against the new backdrop of the rampant spread of the novel coronavirus, this report analyzes the current situation of domestic violence against Chinese women in Thailand, historical background,, religious background, and social background, analyzing the root causes of the high incidence of domestic violence in Thailand, providing self-help methods for victims and pathways to seek social assistance. Discussing domestic violence against Chinese women in Thailand will benefit the personal well-being of Chinese women, family happiness, and social stability, and will also have profound significance for friendly exchanges between China and Thailand. At the same time, research and analysis of domestic violence in Thailand will also provide reference and inspiration for further study of domestic violence issues in China.


source:Freepik


 

Analysis of Background Causes of Domestic Violence Against Women in Thailand


1

Macro Social Background of Domestic Violence Against Women in Thailand


Historical Background


Engels stated in The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State: "In class society, women as a group are oppressed through the specific form of the family." Men hold economic dominance; existence determines consciousness. The economic status inequality between men and women determines their unequal social status. Thai women have experienced periods of relative gender equality in the family during the Sukhothai era, a period in the Ayutthaya era when women had only obligations and no rights, and a period during the Bangkok era when women had relatively equal rights in education and family status.


source:Freepik


In ancient Thailand, women held very low status within the family. They were expected to uphold the values of respecting father and husband; the traditional moral belief that "children should be filial to parents, younger siblings respectful to elders, and wives should care for husbands" has been passed down since ancient times. The old Thai saying, "Men are human, women are cattle," reveals the phenomenon of women's oppression in ancient Thai society. Besides tirelessly managing household chores, raising children, serving husbands, farming, and earning income, women also had to endure verbal abuse, beatings, and even being pawned or sold. Women had no voice at home and were denied access to education, resulting in a lack of resistance, with women subconsciously choosing dependence on and obedience to men.


Thai men could have multiple wives, and the number of wives became a measure of wealth, status, and merit, fostering a corrupt social trend of competition. Thai people often judge a person's character by the amount of "merit" they have accumulated, yet do not consider abusing wives and daughters as diminishing one's merit.


source:Freepik

Religious Background

 Interruption of the Bhikkhuni Tradition


Buddhism is primarily divided into two major schools: Theravāda Buddhism (also known as the School of the Elders) and Mahāyāna Buddhism (including Chinese and Tibetan Buddhism). Theravāda Buddhism is mainly rooted in South and Southeast Asia, while Mahāyāna Buddhism is primarily established in China and other Northeast Asian countries. In early Buddhism, the monastic community consisted of four groups: bhikkhus (fully ordained male monastics), bhikkhunis (fully ordained female monastics), upāsakas (lay male practitioners), and upāsikās (lay female practitioners) (Huang Yunjing, 2014).


source:Freepik


After more than two thousand years of historical change, the Han Chinese Buddhist tradition has preserved the fourfold community of monastics until today; whereas in the Theravāda tradition, due to wars and other reasons, the bhikkhunī ordination lineage was lost after the 11th century. Since then, in Theravāda Buddhist countries, female monastics have no longer been able to receive full bhikkhunī ordination and can only observe eight or ten precepts, known as dasasīlamātā (ten-precept nuns), They lack formal religious status and identity, and can only rely on bhikkhu monastic communities, practicing in male monasteries.


Brenna Artinger, president of the Alliance for Bhikkhuni, said in an interview with online media VICE: "The monastic community wants every lineage to be traceable back to the Buddha. Therefore, they believe that if a lineage has died out, it cannot be revived." Thus, Thailand's Sangha Supreme Council does not support the restoration of the bhikkhunī lineage.


Since 1928, Thai Buddhism has banned women from becoming monks. According to this rule, only men can be formally recognized as monks and students in Thailand. This regulation was reaffirmed twice, in 2002 and 2014, by Thailand's core Buddhist administrative body, the Sangha Supreme Council, and has not been abolished.


The Thai government attributes this to long-standing traditional issues rather than gender discrimination. A spokesperson from the National Office of Buddhism stated that no one prevents women from receiving ordination abroad to become bhikkhunīs, but they cannot do so within Thailand, nor can Thai monks conduct such ordination ceremonies.


source:Freepik

 Modern Thai Women's Struggle


Starting from the 1920s, outstanding female monastics in Thai Buddhism and socially conscious individuals successively launched three waves of movements to revive the bhikkhunī lineage. Their main goal was to reestablish the bhikkhunī ordination system and bhikkhunī saṅgha in Thailand.

1

The First Movement

In 1928, political activist Nai Kulin encouraged his two daughters to ordain as nuns. Nai Kulin donated his property to be converted into a temple named Wat Nafiwong, where his two daughters and eight other female monastics could live and practice. Authorities took forceful measures, sending police to the nunnery to enforce orders. When police arrived, the other female monastics removed their robes under pressure, but sisters Sālā and Jūdī refused. As a result, police forcibly stripped off their monastic robes and imprisoned them. After their release, the two sisters retained their identities as sāmaṇerīs and bhikkhunīs and continued their struggle. However, under immense pressure, they changed the color of their robes. One day, the elder sister disappeared after being abducted while on an alms round, marking the end of Thailand's first movement to revive the bhikkhunī lineage.

2

The Second Movement

In 1956, Voramai Kabilsingh (1908–2003) ordained as a nun, marking the beginning of a new wave of the bhikkhunī revival movement in Thailand. She established a dedicated nuns' monastery, wore yellow robes previously reserved only for male monks, and pioneered a self-sustaining Buddhist monastic system in Thailand. In 1971, Voramai traveled to Taiwan to receive full Mahāyāna bhikkhunī ordination, a landmark event in Thailand's second attempt to restore the bhikkhunī saṅgha. After her return, the bhikkhunī caused a major stir. The Thai government and senior Buddhist monks refused to recognize her bhikkhunī status, but took no forceful action against her, allowing her to continue pursuing her Buddhist ideals and passing her conviction on to her daughter, Dhammananda—leader of the third wave of the bhikkhunī movement.

3

The Third Movement

In 1992, Thailand ended military rule and entered a period of democratic politics, The political and social environment became freer and more open, allowing diverse voices to be expressed. The social status and rights of female monastics received unprecedented public attention. The third wave of the bhikkhunī revival movement focused on restoring the bhikkhunī saṅgha and engaging in social work.

4

Subsequent Struggles

In 1993, another key figure in the movement, Khenyeta, encouraged by Dr. Dhammananda, then teaching at Thammasat University, traveled to Sri Lanka to receive sāmaṇerī ordination. In 1994, Khenyeta submitted a petition to the Thai government, demanding legal recognition of dasasīlamātās' status and identity, and the following year, began establishing Thailand's first Buddhist college for female monastics (Mahapajapati Theft College).


In February 2002, the first sāmaṇerī ordination ceremony in Thailand was held at Songdhammakalyani Monastery. Bhikkhuni Saddha Sumana from Sri Lanka served as the preceptor, with eight bhikkhunīs from Sri Lanka, Taiwan, and Indonesia presiding over the ceremony, joined by six bhikkhus from Thailand and two from Tibet. Thailand's mainstream English-language newspaper, The Bangkok Post, reported the next day with approval: "Yesterday, Thailand held its first women's ordination ceremony, marking a new chapter in Thai Buddhist history."


On February 28, 2003, Dhammananda traveled again to Sri Lanka to receive full bhikkhunī ordination in the Theravāda tradition, receiving the Dharma name Dhammananda, becoming the first Thai bhikkhunī ordained according to the Theravāda tradition. Later that year, Dhammananda, as a bhikkhunī, took residence at Songdhammakalyani Monastery founded by her mother, becoming its second abbot. In addition to accepting fully ordained bhikkhunīs, the monastery also welcomes laywomen for short-term ordination. She also established a shelter—Home of Peace and Love (Home of Peace and Love), providing refuge for women forced into prostitution and survivors of sexual violence.


source:Freepik

Current Status of Thai Bhikkhunīs


Bhikkhunīs in Thailand still lack legal recognition Due to the absence of explicit recognition and privileges, they face difficulties sustaining their monastic communities economically. For example, Thai bhikkhunīs must pay temple taxes to the government, receive relatively fewer donations from lay supporters, and must pay all public transportation costs themselves (Dhammananda, 2020).



Social Context


This article will focus on domestic violence between spouses. This is the most common and narrow definition of domestic violence. Because women are naturally physically weaker than men, domestic violence between spouses often involves women as victims and men as perpetrators. This article primarily examines domestic violence in the narrow sense—violence against women.


Domestic violence is a global issue, prevalent worldwide. According to data released by the World Health Organization, approximately one in three women globally (about 736 million people) will experience physical or sexual violence by an intimate partner in their lifetime—a figure that has remained largely unchanged over the past decade (World Health Organization, 2021). As one of the countries with high rates of domestic violence, Thailand faces a severe situation. According to statistics from the Office of Women's Affairs and Family Development under the Ministry of Social Development and Human Security, Thailand averaged around 1,000 domestic violence cases per year from 2008 to 2016. A Thai family survey found that the rate of domestic violence in Thailand is as high as 34.6% (Liu Lulian, 2019).


source:Freepik


During the COVID-19 pandemic, Thailand's domestic violence problem became even more pronounced. According to statistics from Thai government organizations, prolonged time spent at home led to an increase in domestic violence cases. The Department of Women's Affairs and Family Development, Ministry of Social Development and Human Security, reported that domestic violence cases reported via hotlines rose from 144 in February 2020 to 154 in March (Europäische Zeitung, 2020).


Dr. Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus, Director-General of the World Health Organization, said: "Violence against women is widespread in all countries and cultures, harming millions of women and their families, and has been exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic. Unlike COVID-19, violence against women cannot be stopped with a vaccine. We can only combat it through sustained and unwavering efforts by governments, communities, and individuals—changing harmful attitudes, enabling women and girls to access more opportunities and services, and fostering healthy, mutually respectful relationships." This reflects the impact of the new circumstances brought by the COVID-19 pandemic on domestic violence and the new challenges it poses to anti-violence efforts. (World Health Organization, 2021)

Micro-level Family Background of Domestic Violence Against Thai Women


In today's world, where women's self-awareness is awakening and calls for women's rights are growing louder, women's liberation has achieved notable progress. We see more and more women enjoying equal rights with men. Thai women not only enjoy freedom in marriage but also have equal rights to divorce and participate in family decision-making. Women have stepped out of the home and into society, successfully integrating into the broader social community.


In terms of marriage, the system has evolved from the traditional "arranged by parents, decided by matchmakers" to today's free love and self-determined marriages. In 1930, King Rama VI introduced a new marriage law, replacing the old polygamous system with monogamy. In the new era, Thai women's marriage concepts have undergone radical changes. Women dare to pursue love, and many Thai women are even fond of single life.


In the past, a woman's status depended on her husband's social standing, but now, with increased education levels and income, ⟧157⟧ Thai women have broader life prospects. Women can achieve a certain social status through their own efforts, greatly raising their expectations for marriage. Thai society, blending Eastern and Western cultures, is inclusive toward single and divorced women. Although discrimination against women still exists to some extent, urban, single, middle-class women have relatively independent living spaces., Thai women enjoy broader life prospects, and through their own efforts, they can achieve a certain social status, greatly raising their expectations of marriage. Thai society, which blends Eastern and Western cultures, is inclusive toward single and divorced women. Although discrimination against women still exists to some extent, urban single middle-class women have a relatively independent personal space.


source:Freepik


However, serious domestic violence still exists in Thailand. A random survey of 2,800 women around Bangkok found that 44% of women had suffered violent abuse by their husbands or partners. Domestic violence severely threatens women's health and profoundly impacts their lives, leaving them in a constant state of fear and insecurity. However, most people do not regard domestic violence as a social issue, resulting in serious public awareness gaps. To address this, the Thai government has established volunteer groups in all 50 districts of Bangkok to help prevent the recurrence of domestic violence incidents: by researching violence against women and children, increasing public awareness campaigns, and promoting correct societal understanding of such acts; simultaneously advancing and improving legal protections for women and children to provide institutional support for vulnerable groups.


From the equal status of men and women in the family during the Sukhothai period, to the tragic reality of Thai women being confined to fulfilling duties only during the Ayutthaya period, and then to relatively equal education and family status during the Bangkok period, it is evident that with social transformation, Thai women have successfully stepped out of their homes and integrated into society. Through continuous efforts by women at large, their social status has undergone earth-shaking changes. Compared to women in other Asian countries, Thai women enjoy higher status—ranking first in the Asia-Pacific region's women's social status survey with a score of 92.3. Yet the idea of "women manage the household, men manage external affairs" remains the dominant model of family division in Thai households. Managing household chores and caring for the family is still considered a lifelong career for women. In summary, over the more than 700 years of development from the Sukhothai period to the present, Thai women have achieved significant improvements in family status; however, achieving true gender equality in mindset will require ongoing efforts by Thai women at large.


source:Freepik


 

Current avenues for Thai women suffering domestic violence to seek help

"

Government agencies

The Ministry of Social Development and Human Security was established under the "2002 Ministry Reform Act" and oversees the Bureau of Women Affairs and Family Studies, whose Protection and Career Development Division further supervises the Women Affairs and Family Development Centers. Article 13 of the "2007 Thai Anti-Domestic Violence Act" stipulates that the Ministry of Social Development and Human Security shall issue orders, measures, and procedures as prescribed to provide temporary relief to victims of domestic violence, and in cases of reconciliation, assist in withdrawing complaints or charges. As the specific implementing body, the Women Affairs and Family Development Centers receive support from the Ministry, including knowledge transfer and budget subsidies.


In 2013, Thailand began establishing One-Stop Crisis Centers (One Stop Service Crisis centers) , in hospitals, dedicated specifically to assisting women suffering from domestic violence. Victims can call 1300 to seek help. The center operates 24 hours a day, awaiting calls from women, and closely collaborates with the Women Affairs and Family Development Centers to provide follow-up assistance. Through a digital system, the center continuously monitors communities with high rates of domestic violence and tracks the subsequent recovery status of victimized women.

"

Civil society organizations

Many non-governmental organizations (NGOs) also provide multi-dimensional protection and support.

Pratthanadee Foundation that Better Me Program focus on offering career planning, financial management, English language training, and other skills to women in unsafe environments, and assign one-on-one female mentors to help them gradually achieve financial independence, ultimately escaping abusive environments. Meanwhile, the project has also released a survival guide for women to learn for free (https://pratthanadee.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Better-Me-Survival-Guide.pdf). Women can call+66 (0) 2 331 4731 to obtain needed support.

Shero primarily works to end domestic violence in Thailand through free legal consultation and psychological counseling. It also actively promotes the formulation of policies to protect women's safety. Women can contact the organization via[email protected].

"

Police department

The Anti-Domestic Violence Act stipulates that, when necessary, the police should intervene promptly to protect victimized women. In emergencies, Thai women can call the police hotline 191 to seek immediate assistance and protection.


However, Deputy Police Chief Chatchawal Suksomjit of the Royal Thai Police's Office of the Criminal Court stated, "Many police officers, due to their heavy workload, often treat domestic violence cases as private matters and fail to give them adequate attention." (Thai News Service, 2007). To ensure that female victims of domestic violence receive necessary protective measures, the police department could establish specialized domestic violence protection units to professionally and efficiently handle such cases.

"

Legal avenues

In June 2007, the Thai Parliament passed the "2007 Thai Anti-Domestic Violence Act" (hereinafter referred to as the "Domestic Violence Act"), which criminalized marital rape (Feminist Majority Foundation, 2007). The Domestic Violence Act is currently in force in Thailand, allowing victims of domestic violence to sue perpetrators under the charge of marital rape.


However, the Domestic Violence Act still contains controversial provisions. For example, the Thai Criminal Code stipulates that engaging in sexual acts with minors aged 13–15 should result in a 20-year prison sentence and a fine of 8,000–40,000 Thai baht. However, if the minor consents to the act, the other party may avoid punishment by marrying the minor. This provision is theoretically intended to humanize the law, but in practice, due to an imperfect judicial system, the definition and proof of "sexual consent" remain in a gray area. Given minors' lack of economic power and physical strength, they are more likely to be forced into declaring "consent," and after being compelled into marriage, may face prolonged domestic violence within marriage. More broadly, due to widespread gender income inequality in Thailand, even adult women often struggle to access legal resources equal to those of their spouses, making legal recourse unrealistic for many Thai women.
