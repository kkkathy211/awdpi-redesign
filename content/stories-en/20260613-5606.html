The female pianist Clara Schumann once said,

"A woman should never have the desire to compose."


Whether in the visual arts or music, women's names are far fewer than men's. In fact, it is not that there were no geniuses—only that history has selectively forgotten them.



In the field of "painting," works by female artists were highly likely to be attributed to their fathers, husbands, or male colleagues; they were excluded from official academic systems and forbidden from attending life-drawing classes; their creations were often seen as "boudoir amusements" or "emotional expressions" rather than "serious art.".

In the "musical world," for centuries, social norms, the heavy burden of family responsibilities, and systemic neglect within the industry together erected towering walls, making the creative path extremely difficult for countless talented women.


Today, we will retrace this hidden thread woven by female creators, see their names, and understand their struggles and expressions. This is not only an acknowledgment of the artistic world they have built, but also a correction to art history, and above all, a protest against historical injustice.

 The obscured brushstrokes: I will take up my brush to avenge myself upon you 

✦

01

The Renaissance "exception": Sofonisba Anguissola


In 16th-century Italy, it was almost impossible for a woman to become a professional painter, yet Sofonisba Anguissola became a remarkable exception. Born into nobility, her talent was praised by Michelangelo, and she became a court painter for King Philip II of Spain—her life was nothing short of legendary.


Yet despite such success, many of her works were misattributed to other male painters over the following centuries. It was only through rigorous modern art historical research that these masterpieces were restored to her. We must acknowledge the harsh reality: even the most acclaimed female artists of their time had their artistic achievements constantly at risk of being erased by a patriarchal society.


Sofonisba Anguissola

"The Game of Chess"


Her representative work, "The Game of Chess," does not depict grand mythological or religious scenes, but captures a vivid, natural moment of her sisters playing chess. This kind of family portrait, full of life and psychological depth, injected fresh vitality into the rigid tradition of portraiture at the time.


02

Painting as a weapon: Artemisia Gentileschi


If Anguissola was an elegant survivor, then Artemisia Gentileschi, a generation later, was a roaring rebel.


At the age of 17, she was raped by her father's colleague, the painter Agostino Tassi, and during the subsequent seven-month public trial, she was subjected to torture to verify her testimony. This traumatic experience transformed into one of the most powerful images in art history.


Artemisia Gentileschi

"Judith Slaying Holofernes"

In her famous work, "Judith Slaying Holofernes," blood spurts from the neck as Judith and her maidservant calmly and resolutely carry out the beheading. Unlike the same subject painted by male artists, which often carried erotic overtones, there is no ambiguity here—only a collective act of female justice and revenge.


Gentileschi repeatedly depicted strong, autonomous biblical female heroines throughout her life, using her brush to build a temple of female power. As the first female member of the Florentine Academy of Arts, she proved through her powerful Baroque style: dramatic intensity and emotion are by no means the exclusive domain of male painters.

03

Ink and dust: the dilemma of Chinese talented women


When we turn our gaze to the East, we find that the situation of talented women in ancient China was even more complex, trapped in the narrow space between "talent" and "virtue," "fame" and "chastity."


Take Ma Shouzhen (courtesy name: Xianglan) as an example. This extraordinary woman, one of the "Eight Beauties of Qinhuai" in the Ming dynasty, excelled in poetry and painting, especially renowned for her orchid and bamboo paintings. Her orchid leaves were sparse and elegant, earning her the name "Xianglan," and she was highly admired among literati. Yet no matter how high her artistic achievements, her identity as a courtesan always kept her on the margins of orthodox art history, and her works were often categorized as "courtesan's brushwork," greatly diminishing their artistic value.


Her talent was firmly confined by her social identity.



In poignant contrast is the Song dynasty female poet Li Qingzhao. Born into a scholarly family, she became famous for her extraordinary talent. Her early poetry was delicate and graceful; later, amid the chaos of national collapse and personal displacement, it matured into the solemn and heroic sentiment "Live as a hero, die as a spirit." Yet discussions about her in later generations often revolve around anecdotes about her marriage and the label of "graceful school,"


overlooking her full stature as a scholar and literary innovator, as well as the deep patriotism hidden in her poetry. Modern pioneers: "The obscured muses"


04

As we move into the late 19th century, the center of artistic revolution shifted to Paris.


The birth of Impressionism is seen as the beginning of modern art, and


Berthe Morisot was an indispensable founding member. She worked alongside Manet, Renoir, and others, her brushwork lively and her colors bright, with an exceptional sensitivity to light and shadow. Yet in the eyes of contemporary critics, the Impressionist exhibitions were merely the nonsense of "five madmen and one woman." This phrase, while seemingly acknowledging her presence, in fact reduced her to a mere decorative addition.


Berthe Morisot, "The Cradle"


Similarly, in the German Expressionist group "Der Blaue Reiter" (The Blue Rider),


Gabriele Münter played a crucial role. She was not only a core member and key patron of the group, but also an active explorer of modern artistic language. For a long time, however, art history recorded her only as the partner of artist Wassily Kandinsky, burying her own artistic achievements beneath this famous relationship. She once bitterly wrote: "As a single woman… I was excluded." Gabriele Münter, "View from the Grisbroug Window"


One great woman after another faded into the dust of history—women who were in fact the driving forces and pioneers of artistic movements, yet ultimately became faint footnotes in the margins of history books, obscured by time and narrative. How can one not feel heartbreak and sorrow?


Echoes in the modern and contemporary era: crossing East and West


05

Since the 20th century, more and more Asian and Chinese diaspora women artists have begun to break boundaries in stronger, more self-defined ways.


Yayoi Kusama (Japan)


transformed her mental hallucinations into endless dots and net-like universes, evolving from an ostracized "mental patient" into a globally recognized art icon. Pan Yuliang (China)


traveled to Paris twice, merging traditional Chinese brushwork with Western modernism, particularly using bold and sincere self-portraits of the female nude to challenge Eastern aesthetic and moral taboos, firmly inscribing the modern image of Chinese women in a foreign land. They are no longer merely passively resisting erasure, but


actively carving out unmistakable, uniquely female coordinates on the global art map with highly distinctive personal signatures. The silenced melodies: a woman should not have

The Middle Ages to the Renaissance: the first voices in the convent 

✦


01

In medieval Europe, the church was the absolute center of musical life, and most important musicians were clergy serving the Church. At that time, women were absolutely forbidden from singing in churches, and their musical activities were strictly confined within the walls of convents.


After the fall of the Roman Empire, monasteries became centers for preserving Western European classical culture and Christian civilization. Nuns here could learn to read, write, sing hymns, and copy and bind ancient texts. This relatively secluded environment, paradoxically, provided rare stability and continuous learning opportunities for a few women of noble birth who were dedicated to scholarship.


In the 12th century,



Hildegard von Bingen (1098–1179) emerged under such circumstances. At age 8, she was sent to a convent due to "divine visions," and at 50, after leading nuns to establish an independent convent, she truly gained creative freedom. She was a theologian, physician, and composer—an exceptionally rare "female public intellectual" of the Middle Ages. She composed a collection of about 80 religious songs—


"Symphonia armonie celestium revelationum" (Symphony of the Harmony of Celestial Revelations) Her religious drama,. "Ordo Virtutum" (The Play of the Virtues), is considered the earliest surviving morality play (a precursor to opera). Nearly all roles in the play are sung by female voices, with the sole "Devil" character having no melody, only shrieking spoken lines., Unfortunately, her music was long regarded as merely a religious accessory, and her independent value as a composer was only rediscovered in recent decades.


The Baroque to Classical period: prohibitions in public spaces


02

In the 17th century, with the opening of Europe's first public opera house—the Teatro San Cassiano in Venice—music began to move from churches and courts into civil society. Yet women were still unable to become conductors or instrumentalists in court or opera orchestras;


their musical activities were largely confined to private salons. Barbara Strozzi


(1619–1677) embodies the fate of this era. As the most prolific publisher of secular vocal music in 17th-century Venice, her works such as "Lasciatemi morire" (Let Me Die) are emotionally intense, breaking the gendered constraints of vocal expression at the time. Yet as a student of composer Francesco Cavalli, The emotions are intense, breaking the gender expression constraints of vocal works at the time. Yet as a student of composer Francesco Cavalli, She could only perform for aristocrats in her adoptive father's private salon, never allowed to step onto the public theater stage. Though her works were coveted by nobility, she was excluded from mainstream music history.


After 1664, she vanished from historical records; it was not until 1999 that scholars confirmed her death in Padua in 1677, bringing her forgotten brilliance back to light.


At the same time, Francesca Caccini (1587–1641) of Italy became the first known woman to compose an opera. She created operas and sacred music for the Medici court and was one of the highest-paid musicians of her time. Her 1625 ballet-comedy*La Liberazione di Ruggiero*is the earliest known opera composed by a woman that has survived in complete form. Despite such achievements, her place in music history remained overshadowed by that of her father, Giulio Caccini—one of the founders of early Italian opera.


03

19th Century: Domestic Imprisonment, Educational Deprivation, and Self-Censorship


The 19th century marked the peak of European musical salon culture, where music became a "necessity" for the middle class to display wealth, status, and cultural refinement. Yet for female musicians, it was an era of institutionalized discrimination at its height.


Educational deprivation was their primary obstacle.


In most European conservatories, male and female students were taught in separate rooms with staggered schedules. Women's course options were long restricted to voice, piano, and harp; violin, cello, and wind instruments were considered "male domains." More critically, advanced subjects essential for composition—harmony, counterpoint, fugue, and orchestration—were explicitly reserved for male students in many institutions. This systemic denial of knowledge from the outset directly cut off all possibilities for women to create large-scale, complex works.


On the other hand, the dual discipline of family and society gradually internalized into a harsh form of self-censorship among women.


Fanny Mendelssohn (1805–1847), hailed alongside her brother Felix Mendelssohn as a "child prodigy of the Mendelssohn family," received a clear warning from her father: "Music is a profession for Felix, but for you it can only be a hobby… A young woman's only profession is to become a housewife." Even after marriage, despite her husband's support, she could only host musical salons in her Berlin home. Since women were forbidden from publishing works publicly, many of Fanny's compositions were released under her brother Felix's name.


Clara Schumann (1819–1896) faced an even more complex struggle. A piano prodigy who achieved fame at age 12, she later juggled the roles of "piano star," "devoted wife," and "mother of six children." In her 1839 diary, she wrote: "A woman should not aspire to compose—no woman has ever reached the highest level; why should I expect to be the exception?" This internalized self-doubt and self-denial could suppress creativity more effectively than any external prohibition.


Finally, gender discrimination in publishing and performance became the final barrier suppressing female composers at the time.


Professional publishers generally believed works bearing a woman's name lacked commercial value and seriousness; unless vouched for or co-credited by a male relative, publication was extremely difficult. Top symphony orchestras routinely refused to perform works by female composers, citing reasons such as "lacking strength" or "naive orchestration"—views that collectively deprived women's works of opportunities for public validation and preservation.


Yet even within this tightly sealed discriminatory system, some women managed to break through.


In the United States, Amy Beach (1867–1944) displayed prodigious talent from childhood, but was barred from formal music education due to her gender. Nevertheless, she taught herself orchestration and counterpoint, composing the*Gaelic Symphony*(premiered in 1896), the first symphony composed and published by an American woman. The debut of the *Gaelic Symphony* successfully challenged the social prejudice that "women cannot compose large-scale works."


04

20th Century: War, Civil Rights Movements, and the Awakening of Women's Music


The two world wars of the 20th century unexpectedly created brief performance opportunities for female musicians. During WWII, with large numbers of men conscripted, all-female jazz bands emerged, such as the "International Sweethearts of Rhythm." Its members composed and recorded music; Sister Rosetta Tharpe, known as the "Godmother of Rock and Roll," directly influenced rock pioneers like Elvis Presley, Little Richard, and Chuck Berry.


The 1960s American civil rights movement became a pivotal turning point for women's music, especially for Black women's musical expression. "Freedom songs," a musical form critiquing racial segregation and celebrating freedom and equality, spread widely during the movement, shaping civil rights discourse and strengthening Black collective identity.


African American composer Julia Perry (1924–1979) studied at Juilliard and in Europe, winning the Guggenheim Fellowship twice. In 1965, her*Short Piece for Orchestra*was performed by the New York Philharmonic, becoming the first work by a Black woman composer performed by the ensemble. In midlife, she suffered a stroke that paralyzed her right side, yet with extraordinary determination, she taught herself to compose with her left hand, completing her Twelfth Symphony from her hospital bed.


05

Voices from Asia: The Recording Industry, Colonial Contexts, and National Identity


In the early 20th century, with the spread of recording technology, Asian women musicians began to leave their voices in new ways.



India's Gauhar Jaan (1873–1930) recorded India's first commercial records in 1902, pioneering the "three-minute music" model—condensing lengthy traditional performances to fit record durations, laying the foundation for the commercialization of Indian music. As a professional performing artist, she recorded over 600 songs in more than a dozen languages, yet due to her ethnic identity, she was deliberately overlooked by mainstream music history.



In Shanghai, China, Liang Ping (1926–unknown), a graduate of the Shanghai Conservatory, joined the Pathé Records Company at age 15, becoming one of the few female singer-songwriters in the "shidaiqu" (era music) industry. She could interpret masterpieces by composers like Li Jinguang and Yao Min, and also composed original works such as*Dad Who Never Ages*. Yet within the male-dominated narrative of music history, her creative achievements were similarly long neglected.



In the People's Republic of China, Huang Zhun (1926–2024), one of the first generation of film composers, composed scores for dozens of films including *The Red Detachment of Women*, *Number Five of the Women's Basketball Team*, and *The Herdsman*. To compose the "Red Detachment March," she traveled to Hainan three times for field research, interviewing veteran soldiers from the original Women's Detachment, and found inspiration upon witnessing the scene of Hong Changqing's martyrdom during filming. She worked quietly behind the scenes throughout her life—her works household names, yet her name largely unknown to audiences—until her passing at age 98, when this "master of behind-the-scenes melodies" finally received the historical recognition she deserved. Contemporary Chinese Women Breaking Boundaries:

Letting the World Hear Our Voices

✦

01

Chen Xiaoxia—The "Dreammaker" Behind the Golden Melodies


In the Chinese music industry, Chen Xiaoxia's name is synonymous with hit songs. Having crafted signature songs for artists like Vivian Chow, Faye Wong, and Angela Zhang, she remained behind the scenes throughout her more than forty-year career.


It was not until late 2025, at age 70, that she held the extremely rare solo concert "COMPOSER: Old Wings," completing a transformation from writing emotions for others to fully expressing herself on stage. Her "reverse journey" broke the fixed role that "successful behind-the-scenes creators need not step into the spotlight."


02

Wu Man: The "World Architect" on the Pipa Strings


Wu Man's story as a pipa player is an expedition that shattered geographical and cultural barriers.


At 26, already a teacher at the Central Conservatory of Music, she moved alone to the United States to promote the pipa from scratch. She achieved success through cross-genre collaborations with ensembles like the Kronos Quartet and became a founding member of Yo-Yo Ma's "Silk Road Project," serving as a cultural bridge.


Later, she returned to rural China to deepen her roots, then brought indigenous art forms like Hua Yin Laosheng to world stages such as New York's Carnegie Hall. What she made the world hear was not just the pipa, but the diverse, vibrant voices of the Chinese land.




Throughout music history, women musicians across different eras and regions have consistently faced similar barriers: the ruthless denial of creative rights, the suppression of social identity by domestic roles, the systematic erasure of their works for various reasons, and the reduction of their personal achievements to mere footnotes within male-dominated narratives.


From Sofonisba Anguissola to Artemisia Gentileschi, from Fanny Mendelssohn to Clara Schumann, from being marginalized as "exceptions" to being obscured as "muses," the history of female creators is one of resistance and reclamation. Even today, they continue to break through commercial labels, cultural stereotypes, and role constraints.


The next time we stand before a work of art or listen to a melody, perhaps we should ask one more question: "Whose story is still waiting to be discovered?"


Because every act of "seeing" and "hearing" is an act of writing a more complete and truthful human cultural history. When these once-silenced voices gather into a river, what we will witness is not merely the mending of history, but a magnificent audiovisual revolution.
