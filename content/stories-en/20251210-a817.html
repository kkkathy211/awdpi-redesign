Every year from November 25 to December 10, the United Nations' "16 Days of Activism against Gender-Based Violence" focuses global attention on the threat of violence faced by women and girls.


This year, the campaign highlights a rapidly spreading and urgent issue—"Eliminating Digital Violence Against All Women and Girls." From online harassment, privacy breaches, and deepfake pornography to cyberstalking, digital spaces are becoming a new battleground for gender-based violence.


Meanwhile, this year marks the fifth anniversary of AWDPI (Asian Women Development Plan International). Over the past five years, this transnational nonprofit network has consistently worked to listen to and support Asian women living overseas, particularly through its flagship project Avoice, providing systematic assistance to overseas Chinese women experiencing domestic violence.


To combat gender-based violence, especially the harms of digital gender violence, international efforts are continuously evolving and upgrading, striving to create a safer online environment for women through innovative legal frameworks and technological tools. AWDPI is also engaging in this cross-linguistic, cross-cultural, and cross-digital-border struggle, seeking to provide multifaceted support to women enduring the shadows of violence.





The Multi-Layered Harms and Structural Challenges of Digital Violence

Digital violence is not a virtual "secondary harm," but a real, ongoing, and destructive form of oppression.


In today's society, the virtual and the real are deeply intertwined and inseparable. Gender-based violence on digital platforms is both an extension of real-world gender inequality and gender conflict into cyberspace, and at the same time increases and intensifies the likelihood of women facing harm in the physical world. Enabled by technology, digital violence transcends time and space, posing serious threats to women's careers, social lives, and mental health.


Digital violence takes many forms, including:

  • Online harassment and bullying: Sending insults, threats, or sexually suggestive content via social media, instant messaging, or email. For women in the public eye, such harassment is often larger in scale and more organized, aiming to force them out of public discourse.

  • Privacy breaches and non-consensual sharing of intimate images: Including "revenge porn" (distribution of private images without consent), doxxing, and malicious exposure of personal information. These acts not only violate privacy but also lead to social shaming, employment discrimination, and even offline violence against victims.

  • Technology-facilitated stalking and surveillance: Abusers use spyware, location tracking tools, and smart devices to monitor women's movements and communications, intensifying fear and isolation. In cases of intimate partner violence, these methods often intertwine with real-world control, making it difficult for women to escape.

  • Misuse of emerging technologies such as deepfakes: Using AI to fabricate pornographic images or statements of women for defamation, blackmail, or character assassination. Such content spreads rapidly and is difficult to remove, causing long-term damage to women's reputations and psychological well-being.


Digital violence against women often does not occur in isolation, but intersects with each woman's national, racial, and migrant identities.


According to AWDPI statistics, over 60% of female survivors of sexual violence struggle to access timely assistance due to language barriers, visa dependency, or social isolation. It is not difficult to imagine that digital violence would further exacerbate their plight. After experiencing online harassment, they often choose to remain silent out of fear of identity exposure, negative impacts on their visa status, or community rejection.


According to data from the U.S.-based "Stop AAPI Hate" organization, nearly two-thirds of reported incidents between 2020 and 2022 involved female victims, with racist rhetoric frequently combining gender stereotypes such as "submissiveness" and "exoticism," making foreign women particularly vulnerable targets.


Beyond visible physical harm, "invisible violence" is spreading through digital platforms. Non-physical harms such as psychological control, verbal humiliation, and digital harassment are often ignored due to "difficulty in collecting evidence" or "lack of recognition." In 2022, "gaslighting" became the Word of the Year for Merriam-Webster, reflecting growing public awareness of emotional manipulation, yet legal and social support systems still lag behind in addressing these intangible forms of violence.






Legal Innovation and Systemic Responses by the International Community


World Bank statistics show that globally, only fewer than four in ten countries have laws prohibiting online harassment and stalking. This means approximately 1.8 billion women—one-third of the world's female population—44%lack legal protection in digital spaces. Even in countries with such laws, low enforcement efficiency, complicated reporting procedures, and difficulties in evidence recognition often deter victims from seeking justice.


The harm caused by digital violence is profound and complex: it erodes women's autonomy and sense of safety, weakens their willingness to participate in society, and may even force them to leave workplaces, schools, or public platforms. More alarmingly, it sends a dangerous message—that cyberspace is a lawless zone, and women should not speak freely.


In response to the global spread of digital violence, governments, international organizations, and civil society are accelerating responses in legislation, policy, and public awareness. In recent years, several groundbreaking laws have been enacted, marking the formal entry of gender violence governance into the "digital era.".


On April 29, 2024, the European Union formally adopted the landmark Directive on combating violence against women and domestic violence, which officially came into force on May 20, 2024. It aims to comprehensively combat violence against women—including cyber violence, domestic violence, forced marriage, and female genital mutilation (FGM)—through prevention, protection, prosecution, and support measures, and requires member states to strengthen legislation and resource allocation. This is the EU's first legal instrument specifically targeting gender-based violence, marking a milestone. The directive not only criminalizes female genital mutilation and forced marriage across all member states but also


for the first time at the EU level, classifies digital gender-based violence as a criminal offense, explicitly prohibiting cyberstalking, non-consensual sharing of intimate images, and online harassment. The law sets minimum standards for member states and requires them to establish specialized support services. In 2021,


Mexico passed the "Olimpia Law" (Ley Olimpia), named after digital violence survivor Olimpia Coral Melo, which clearly defines "digital violence" as a crime, covering (Olympia's Law) harassment, threats, dissemination of intimate content, and doxxing. It requires platforms to quickly remove such content and provide legal and psychological support to victims. In 2022, Spain passed the "Only Yes Means Yes" law.


This law places "sexual consent" at its core, requiring that sexual acts be based on clear, voluntary agreement. Equality Minister Irene Montero stated: "No woman should ever have to prove she experienced violence or intimidation in order to be respected." Although primarily focused on sexual violence, its "affirmative consent" principle offers valuable reference for identifying online sexual harassment and coercion. Additionally, the International Labour Organization (ILO) adopted


Convention No. 190 (C190 - Violence and Harassment Convention, 2019 (No. 190)) in June 2019. This is the first international treaty in history aimed at eliminating violence and harassment in the world of work. It explicitly recognizes that "domestic violence also affects the workplace" and requires employers to take measures to protect employees affected by domestic violence. It also, for the first time, includes


"gender-based violence and harassment" in global labor standards. Over the past five years (2020–2025), the ratification process of this convention has accelerated globally and has been approved in countries including the United Kingdom, Australia, Canada, France, and Spain. Beyond legislation, shifts in social attitudes are equally crucial.



The impact of the #MeToo movement has continued to deepen in recent years, pushing the public to gain deeper understanding of the relationship between sexual violence and power structures. The principle of "survivor-centered" approaches is gradually becoming the standard for global nonprofit organizations, judicial systems, and media when handling cases—respecting victims, avoiding secondary harm, and providing comprehensive support.


However, challenges remain. Uneven law enforcement, high technical barriers to digital forensics, lack of cross-border cooperation mechanisms, and inadequate responses to marginalized groups (such as migrant, disabled, and LGBTQ+ women) are still shortcomings in global governance. These challenges represent the directions the international community must work toward in the future.





How can we help women who suffer from gender-based violence?

Over the past five years, the global fight against gender-based violence has seen breakthroughs, but also unprecedented and complex challenges. From the shadow of the pandemic to the spread of digital violence, from rising racial hatred to climate crises, these social conflicts and natural disasters have not only intensified the complex situations women face but also demand more inclusive and profound response frameworks from nations worldwide.


AWDPI, founded in 2020, has grown precisely within this broader context. As a key project currently operated by AWDPI, Avoice reaches over 30 countries and regions across Europe, North America, and the Asia-Pacific through online platforms, providing continuous, systematic support to every overseas Chinese woman experiencing domestic violence, covering multiple dimensions including language, psychology, law, community, and emergency networks.


Avoice not only offers crisis intervention but also strives to transform the structural conditions that enable violence—through legal empowerment, psychological recovery, and community connection, helping women evolve from "survivors" into "activists."


Avoice's support framework includes::


Language Support

  • Multilingual services: English, French, German, Russian, Dutch, Czech, Portuguese, Italian, and more

  • Translation Assistance: Provide interpretation and translation services, assist in organizing and submitting help-seeking materials.

  • Sign language support: Offering online sign language communication and information delivery for deaf and hard-of-hearing individuals


Psychological Support

  • Counseling services: including expert psychological counseling and peer-based mental health support, offering personalized emotional guidance as well as psychological intervention for perpetrators.

  • Mental health programs: developing therapeutic curricula and learning materials to support emotional recovery.


Legal support

  • Legal Information Database: Compile and translate laws from various countries related to domestic violence and digital violence, establish a Chinese-language legal database, and produce public legal education handbooks

  • Case-specific legal advice: providing legal analysis and actionable recommendations for survivors.

  • Outreach assistance: sharing information on local legal aid resources and helping connect survivors with lawyers and relevant organizations.


Community support

  • Women's mutual aid communities: building communities for Chinese women overseas to promote peer support and experience sharing.

  • Online learning activities: organizing events on domestic violence prevention, self-protection, legal education, psychological healing, and gender awareness raising.


Overseas assistance network

  • Assistance information database: consolidating information on shelters, medical services, legal aid, psychological support, and financial assistance across countries.

  • Resource linkage: assisting in contacting local organizations and providing fast, effective referral services.


Storytelling and amplification support

  • Listening to and documenting the experiences of survivors of domestic and digital violence, sharing their methods and stories of overcoming adversity.

  • Interviewing anti-domestic violence practitioners to disseminate effective intervention models and institutional experiences.

  • Presenting the impacts of domestic violence from multiple perspectives, including those of children, family members, and other affected groups.


Looking back in history, in 1995, the Fourth World Conference on Women adopted the Beijing Declaration, loudly proclaiming "Women's rights are human rights." Thirty years on, this wave has swept across the globe, its meaning expanding from physical spaces into the digital realm.


AWDPI and the Avoice project were born out of this very mission. Whether immigrant women facing domestic violence or international students suffering from digital platform-based abuse, Avoice strives to build a soft yet resilient safety net to catch lives that might otherwise fall.


The 16 Days of Activism will end, but the struggle will not stop. Digital violence continues to evolve, and so must our responses—through more comprehensive laws, more inclusive perspectives, closer international collaboration, and deeper societal awakening. This path is long and difficult; the journey toward safety and dignity for women has never ceased—We may not be able to end violence immediately, but we can let more people know that survivors are not alone.


On this journey, AWDPI walks side by side with countless activists.
