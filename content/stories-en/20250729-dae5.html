Today, AWDPI (Asian Women Development Plan International) celebrates its fifth anniversary.


Since its founding in 2020, AWDPI has been committed to eliminating gender discrimination and sexual violence against Asian women overseas, building a transnational support and rescue system, and advancing community empowerment and advocacy.. Over the past five years, an increasing number of volunteers have joined this warm and determined movement, offering companionship and attentive listening to light a glimmer of hope for survivors rebuilding their futures.


In the initial piece, we heard their courage and perseverance when facing difficult moments of seeking help, and also saw their thoughtful reflections on gender equality and transnational mutual aid networks. This follow-up piece turns our attention to the real, everyday moments in their lives. It is these fragmented yet precious glimpses that reveal how AWDPI turns "support" and "companionship" into a force that transcends geography.


Let us continue to draw closer to this group of kindred spirits who turn small lights into a blazing torch, and record more stories of hope and courage.





Zhou Wenxin, Human Resources Department

Volunteer for 3 years




"I want to create a world of love and equality for women."


Last year, I attended the organization's annual meeting and met the team leaders and fellow volunteers in person for the first time.


Because I've always worked in HR, I was already familiar with everyone's names and had discussed work with them before, but that was all. Yet during that meeting, I met the real people behind the names and avatars on Feishu, each one vibrant, talented, and united by shared values.


We wrote Nüshu script together, made tangyuan and qingtuan rice balls, created collaged poems and posters, practiced sound baths, toured the city, and shared meals, talking about our present and future—both personal and organizational—over three deeply fulfilling and joyful days. Afterward, I thought: I want to see everyone again. I want to keep working, keep striving to create a world of love and equality for women.


My HR role doesn't involve frontline rescue work. But I remember once at an event, a team leader shared that she was once a survivor who had sought help from the organization and was deeply grateful for the timely and effective support she received. After healing, she found the organization's mission profoundly meaningful and wanted to help other survivors, so she joined and eventually became a leader. Hearing her story moved me deeply—selfless, warm, and powerful female strength.

Volunteer for 2 years




"We are not alone."

I mostly interact with applicants, not directly with the women we support. But from their resumes, I see people around the world struggling with and striving to address the same issues. Beyond realizing how widespread this shared challenge is, I've come to understand that


each of us is not alone—we are all making efforts in our own ways. Realizing this gives me a special sense of strength. I We each are not alone—we are all making efforts in our own ways. Realizing this gives me a special sense of strength.


Previously, I was assigned a special recruitment task to help launch a new department. After working hard with the team, we finally got it up and running. Seeing the plans everyone submitted turn into high-quality events, I truly felt how our efforts were being transformed into tangible value. Ji Xuan (Rayna), Xiao Hongshu Lead, Communications Department




Volunteer for one and a half years 

"No words needed—precisely because we share the same goal."




Every time I check the backend of Xiao Hongshu and see new followers and views,


I feel a new possibility emerging—that women in need around the world might access useful information and support. Seeing our official account gain more attention, and knowing that every recruitment post brings more kind-hearted partners into the AWDPI family, I feel that persistence has meaning.


At the same time,


in building the Xiao Hongshu community from scratch, I feel we can now spread our activities more directly and raise our organization's visibility. Although my co-lead and I are in different time zones, we always respond to content needs with great tacit understanding.


Precisely because we share the same goal—helping more women in need find support—we need no words to smoothly complete our publishing tasks together.




Kylan, Communications Department

Volunteer for 4 years




"Care from friends, colleagues, and even people we don't know well really matters."


As the designer of the *Guidebook for Combating Domestic Violence Among Chinese Women in the UK*, from the moment I saw my colleagues compile such detailed information, I could already imagine the significant impact this guidebook might have.


It wasn't until later, when I saw photos of it being used in offline workshops, that the reality truly hit me. The guidebook also received strong positive feedback in subsequent activities. In our design, some techniques that fellow team members often found "odd" were actually imbued with gender meanings.


For example, modernist design evokes associations with masculinity, while Art Nouveau style evokes femininity. These associations are deeply tied to the prevailing social contexts of their times, so balancing gender elements in design is also a meaningful consideration.


In a past conversation, a casual check-in with a colleague revealed that she had suffered serious harm in a past relationship with a man. She had never expressed this on social media, nor shown it in workplace interactions, but had silently struggled and fought through it alone.


I was deeply shocked—on one hand, saddened that such gender-based harm was happening so close to me; on the other hand, I realized we need proactive care from friends, colleagues, or even people we don't know well. That kind of care really matters.




Wang Leyi, External Liaison, Psychological Support Department 

Volunteer for 1 year and 3 months




"In that moment, I felt the incredible power of human resilience."


I once had a client who initially came in urgently requesting psychological counseling.


After several counseling sessions and some major life upheavals, one day she suddenly told me: "I think I no longer need counseling." She shared with me insights from her recent conversations with others, and reflected on her own emotional journey over this period.


Finally, she said: " I've figured out what I truly need, and now I feel at peace. The things that once tormented me no longer cause me such pain. " In that moment, I felt a profound sense of innate human resilience, and the extraordinary strength within this survivor herself.


In that moment, I was deeply moved to the core. I hope this strength can help all good-hearted people overcome hardship and move closer to their ideal shores.




Tingfan, Writing and Reporting Department

Volunteer for 1 year




"As long as it's written down, there's a possibility of solving it."


To be precise, I came here believing this was more than just volunteer work.


Through each interview and article, I've seen the significance of giving voice to women in difficult situations: as long as it's written down, there's a possibility of resolution. I've also reflected, through documentation, on the relationship between anti-violence efforts and the law, and how the definition of related terms affects legal language.


Although I haven't directly worked with sexual violence survivors, I believe our work is deeply connected to anti-violence efforts. Whether it's commentary on current hot topics or the sexual harassment toolkit we've built, these contain the potential to convey a violence-free set of values. These things can influence others and contribute to the anti-violence cause.


What impressed me most was writing a film review of *Her Own Will*. At the time, it happened to be the last day of screening in my city. After leaving the cinema, my teammate Siyu and I truly cried as we divided up the writing tasks. Talking with her, we both saw the personal struggles portrayed in the film, yet felt helpless to change them.


I believe nothing I write could ever match the scenery in her eyes.




Xiao Rui, Empowerment and Support Department 

Volunteer for 1 year and 4 months




"This is not just volunteer work—it's my way of engaging with public issues and contributing a small light to society."


Last year, during AWDPI's online forum, I was assigned to write summaries for several sessions and responsible for compiling records of several debate events on women's rights and feminism.


These discussions were deeply insightful, and many comments greatly benefited me. I also realized the power of knowledge and ideas flowing within public spaces. When I shared the summaries in the group, I saw many teammates actively respond and continue the conversation. In that moment, I felt that my words could actually become a bridge that sparks dialogue and connects people. Although my work mainly focuses on community management, content publishing, and promotion, when organizing and sharing articles, books, and recruitment information related to women's issues,


I've increasingly felt the power of information. Sometimes, simply sharing a volunteer recruitment post brings many responses. Seeing so many female friends willing to step forward and actively participate, I realize:


change begins with connection, and every small action counts. Practice itself turns out to be the most powerful statement. Though we may be in different places, we are connected through the internet, amplifying our voices together to create possibilities of support and change for more women.


The moment that impressed me most was when a member in the group saw a Chinese woman seeking help on Xiaohongshu: she was suffering domestic violence from her foreign husband, had almost no family or friends nearby for support, and was in grave danger. We quickly organized within the group, trying to contact local volunteers and connect resources for her. At that moment, I deeply realized that this volunteer work goes far beyond daily tasks or information sharing—it concerns the safety and lives of real individuals. And this silent solidarity and support moved and inspired me profoundly.




Xu Zhongrui, Language Support Division 

3 years in volunteer work




"This work begins with passion, but does not end there."


Language services involve many aspects. Through this work, I've realized there are many issues I previously didn't understand or pay attention to. By participating, I've gained deeper and more comprehensive insights into women's issues.


For me, this is not just volunteer work—it's more like an ongoing learning process. Like writing a research paper, it requires constant information gathering and refining my thinking on women's issues. I believe this process is crucial to the development of feminism.


The Italian film *Tomorrow Will Be Better*, a feminist film, received a warm reception after its release in China.


Many scenes in the film are almost direct reflections of our daily realities—women suffering violence, facing employment discrimination, denied equal education, being in vulnerable positions within families, and even to some extent being deprived of the right to freely pursue love.


And our work resembles a scene like this: All those committed to feminism form a wall, raising awareness about the importance of protecting women's rights and inviting more people to join, surrounding and confronting these injustices.




Qian Yan, US National Office

1 year and 2 months in volunteer work




"The women we assist are never just a simple label—like a female immigrant of a certain nationality—but real, living individuals, with loved ones they deeply care for and immense strength they continuously give for those they love."


The experience that impressed me most was working with a woman whose case was extremely complex and who had also suffered severe psychological trauma due to long-term abuse.


Her situation involved not only domestic violence and immigration status issues, but also other legal disputes, requiring highly detailed and complicated legal information and strategies. To help her clarify her thoughts, I spent considerable time compiling a detailed legal research report, organizing as clearly as possible the potential legal pathways, applicable laws, and contact information for relevant agencies.


However, in follow-up communications, I found that her psychological state made it difficult for her to maintain trust in the judicial system. Even issues already explained and included in the report, she would repeatedly ask about in the support group, expressing confusion and doubt. Often, her questions weren't due to misunderstanding the content, but stemmed from a lack of security toward the entire judicial system—she feared being "taken advantage of," that "judges might be biased against Asians," or believed "lawyers wouldn't help me anyway."


Seeing her repeatedly ask questions in the group, I sometimes felt helpless, but still tried my best to respond to each one, explaining in different ways, patiently confirming whether she understood, and avoiding making her feel blamed or interrupted. I also tried to comfort her, listened to her deep distrust of the US judicial system, told her which rights she could claim, which processes she could safely use, and provided her with several free or low-cost legal service resources, hoping she could at least take the next step.


This experience was unforgettable for me because it made me truly realize: Providing legal support is not simply about "giving information." Often, what survivors need is not a one-time answer, but the gradual building of trust and a sense of safety amid chaos and fear. For legal resources to be useful to them, the prerequisite is that they dare to use them, want to use them, and believe they won't be yet another tool that harms them. What we do may seem small, but it could be the last straw they can hold onto.


It is precisely because of such moments that I am even more determined to carry out this volunteer work with greater care and compassion, because it's not just about transferring information—it's about proving to them: You are not alone, your voice deserves to be heard, and your rights deserve to be defended!




Lin Qi, Case Coordination Division 

3 years and 8 months in volunteer work




"Our existence is like a faint light, helping women in unfamiliar distress feel the possibility of being understood."


When reaching out to women in need of assistance, I found that many have long endured abuse due to the belief in "not airing family dirty laundry in public," and are even threatened by abusers with "disrupting family harmony." They are not only afraid of being stigmatized if they seek help from local institutions, but also fear being blamed if they confide in relatives or friends back home. Moreover, differences between domestic and overseas legal systems, along with language barriers, make even basic procedures for asserting their rights extremely difficult.


This made me deeply aware: rescue cannot stop at "providing resources," but must build a "culturally appropriate support network." We must not only help women escape personal danger, but also dismantle societal prejudices like "victim-blaming." Gender-based violence is fundamentally the result of cultural norms, institutional structures, and power imbalances.


In this work, what impressed me most was witnessing a client's transformation—from being someone in need of help, to self-help, and eventually to helping others. It's not uncommon for women we assist to proactively apply to join our team after their situation improves. We've found more and more women want to use their own strength to help others.


Perhaps those who have walked through darkness are more eager to light a source that illuminates others. This "from being helped to becoming a helper" transformation in identity precisely embodies the core meaning of our commitment to public service.




Gou Zhiyi, Case Coordination Division 

1 year in volunteer work




"On a broader level, case services are a tangible way to advance gender equality; on a personal level, they are a way to combat feelings of helplessness in the face of reality."


I often see social news about gender issues—about 95% of it is negative—and in those moments, my sense of helplessness becomes especially strong. But when I focus on case work, I find an anchor, a sense that by impacting real individuals, the world is at least getting a little better on a micro level.


My observation is that women experiencing violence are generally trapped by two factors: lack of resources, and unfair structures and systems.


Although the core values and service techniques underlying global anti-domestic violence and anti-gender-based violence efforts are shared, the challenges faced by survivors in cross-cultural contexts are often deeper. Due to residency status, language difficulties, and social isolation, resources and measures available to citizens—such as calling the police, litigation, or shelters—can be extremely difficult for foreigners to access.


Therefore, case workers need not only strong gender awareness and anti-domestic violence knowledge, but also cross-cultural perspectives and higher trauma sensitivity—areas I hope to study more deeply in the future.


What impressed me most wasn't a specific event, but a realization—recognizing my own limitations, and learning to truly respect every choice made by those seeking help. Since my professional role is as an anti-domestic violence lawyer, the clients I encounter in real life usually have very clear intentions to seek help, and I'm used to controlling the pace and pushing things forward. But in this online support work, some people who initially communicated well suddenly go offline; or when we list many resources, they hesitate or retreat instead.


At first, I felt quite anxious, but as my experience grew and I learned more trauma-informed knowledge, I gradually realized that having too high expectations of clients might turn into blaming them. As survivors, they are not unwilling to act, but are making the safest choices possible within their capacity. Therefore, in my current work, I've learned not to see myself as too central, not to set too many preset goals, and not to treat "solving problems" as the sole value of case services. I respect clients' self-determination—even if that means choosing hesitation or temporarily not taking action—I will understand and accompany them.




Chang Jingzi, Head of Legal Support Division

3 years in volunteer work




"Providing legal aid at AWDPI, I've entered the lives of real, living individuals. Behind every case are profound stories, filled with pain, helplessness, sorrow, or anger."


These three years of volunteer work have coincided with my key growth phase from legal theory to practice—from academic foundation during overseas studies, to commercial practice at a law firm, to now expanding my perspective in corporate legal work. While these experiences involved abstract legal texts or seemingly glamorous commercial matters, they often left me feeling distant from real human lives.


Every time we provide legal aid to a client and help her out of hardship, I am deeply aware that this is not just volunteer work—through legal research and advice, everything we do may, in some way, influence her future and change the trajectory of her life.


These genuine moments of helping others always reawaken my original purpose in choosing law: to help, to comfort, and to bring change through the law. Many women who have experienced violence often come to us only after exhausting all other options, or when they are already in dire financial straits.


In their stories, I often see despair and helplessness, even deep doubts about their self-worth. They may have endured countless moments of suffering and silence before reaching out. And when they finally take that step to seek legal assistance, they are often physically and emotionally exhausted, emotionally overwhelmed, and may have already lost hope for the future.


When communicating with these women, we often face their emotional fluctuations and psychological barriers. At times, a client may express anger, fear, or even indifference, unable to effectively articulate her real needs, or may be skeptical of our support. These situations remind me that gender-based violence is far more than just surface-level abuse and resistance; it is a multidimensional issue that reaches deep into individual psychology, culture, social environments, and historical backgrounds.


For many overseas Asian women, they not only endure the harm caused by gender-based violence, but also often face additional pressures such as language barriers, cultural gaps, social isolation, and unstable immigration status. In such difficult circumstances, seeking help is an extremely difficult decision. They may fear being abandoned by their families due to the violence, or worry about facing greater legal risks due to their immigration status.


In such cases, the most important thing in providing legal aid is first establishing trust with these women. We need to patiently listen to their voices, understand their pain and fears, and give them sufficient time and space to express themselves.




Xia Yan, Legal Support Division

Volunteer experience: 3 years and 4 months




"What sustains us in the long run is not just passion, but a team culture of mutual support and shared growth through adversity. This, more than any single successful case, is what I cherish most."


Recently, we received direct negative feedback from a client for the first time. The issue arose because her follow-up request involved a practical matter of marriage law in a specific jurisdiction.


She asked us to replace the current legal support volunteer with someone experienced in that jurisdiction. However, at that time, our team did not have any members who fully met her requirements. Considering the seriousness and professionalism of legal procedures, and since her situation was not an emergency threatening personal safety, after careful discussion, we offered what we believed to be the most responsible recommendation: to seek a qualified local lawyer who could handle the matter for a fee.


However, this suggestion was met with strong skepticism. She accused us of being dismissive and evasive, and went on to completely reject our entire case support effort, including the work of the assigned coordinator and psychological support colleagues, before ultimately leaving all support groups. Honestly, in that moment, the entire support team felt shocked and disheartened.


As an unpaid volunteer service, we had invested immense time and emotional energy, only to receive such an outcome—the sense of powerlessness was very real. But that difficult evening, no one blamed the client, nor did anyone fall into self-criticism or internalized guilt asking, "Was it my fault?" Instead, we spontaneously comforted and encouraged each other in our internal team group.


The first thing we did was to practice empathy and understanding. We repeatedly reminded each other: the client is experiencing profound psychological trauma, and her heightened emotions and instability are completely understandable. Her rejection was not directed at us personally, but was an outward expression of her trauma.


The second thing we did was to review and reaffirm our principles. We re-examined our response process and confirmed that our recommendation was made based on professional judgment and a sense of responsibility toward the client. We became even more resolute in our principle: Beyond our capacity, we will never offer irresponsible "help" just to "appease" someone.


That day, I deeply felt the maturity and warmth of this team. We were able to endure hardship, rationally separate the client's emotions from the value of our work, support each other in processing negativity, and then keep moving forward.




In these ordinary yet touching stories, we see the most authentic side of AWDPI volunteers: They are not always all-powerful; they also face difficulties and challenges. Yet, through every patient listening, every quiet act of protection, small sparks of light gradually gather into a constellation that illuminates the path ahead.


It is precisely this persistence from volunteers that ensures women in crisis are no longer alone, and allows the support network within the Asian women's community to continuously grow, turning "mutual watchfulness" into a real, rooted force. The second interview ends here, but the story of AWDPI continues, and the efforts of every volunteer go on without pause.


Let us carry this warmth and courage forward, and look forward to more companions joining us, letting light protect light, and action answer hope.
