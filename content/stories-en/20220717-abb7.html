The Qur'an, Believers, and State Politics:

The Veil as a Symbol within Islamic Societies

●


Muslim women who follow Islam typically wear a veil in front of men who are not close relatives. (Vyver, 2017) The veil, collectively known as "hijab," covers women's hair and neck, extending down to the chest. In addition, there are other types of face and body coverings with different names, varying in style but similar in function, all used to conceal women's heads and bodies.


Image source: ABC NEWS


In Muslim societies, the veil is first and foremost a religious symbol. The practice of wearing the veil is considered a requirement of Islamic law (Sharia), a legal system derived from the Qur'an and other sacred Islamic texts. It governs rules for Muslim life, from how to pray to acts of charity and fasting. Because the original text of the Qur'an is limited in scope and coverage, Islamic scholars use these primary sources to derive answers to specific issues not explicitly addressed in the scriptures (Stancati, 2021). Whether women must wear the veil is one such issue. Therefore, even among Muslim believers, understanding of this matter stems from, yet extends beyond, the literal text of the scriptures.


Although the Qur'an does not explicitly mandate that female believers must wear the veil, it contains certain verses describing the Prophet's wives—for instance, instructing male believers to speak to the Prophet's wives from behind a curtain, or commanding the Prophet's wives and believing women to wear outer garments and veils when going out. However, different Islamic schools of thought interpret these verses differently. Some argue the Prophet was implying that men should speak to all women from behind a curtain; others maintain that ordinary women are not the Prophet's wives and therefore do not need to speak from behind a curtain or wear a veil. Beyond academic debate, in daily life, Muslims generally believe the Prophet prescribed veil-wearing for women, a belief supported by faith in, obedience to, and emulation of the Prophet. Chapter 24 of the Qur'an, "The Light" (An-Nur), states:


"Tell the believing women to lower their gaze, guard their private parts, and not display their adornments except what is normally apparent, and to draw their veils over their chests, and not reveal their adornments except to their husbands, or their fathers, or their husbands' fathers, or their sons, or their husbands' sons, or their brothers, or their brothers' sons, or their sisters' sons, or their female servants, or their slaves, or male attendants who lack vigor, or children who are not yet aware of women's nakedness. Let them not stamp their feet so as to reveal what they hide of their adornments. O believers! Turn to Allah in repentance, all of you, so that you may succeed." (24: 31)


Image source: Pixabay


Chapter 33 of the Qur'an, "The Confederates" (Al-Ahzab), similarly contains verses suggesting women should cover their bodies, but more specifically explains the purpose of covering—to avoid harassment:


"Prophet, tell your wives, your daughters, and the believing women to draw their outer garments around them. That is more suitable so that they may be recognized and not harassed. God is Most Forgiving, Most Merciful." (33: 59 )


Muslim scholar Memon Madani (2011) interprets these two verses as admonitions from the Prophet to his companions. Many of these companions, Madani notes, already held higher status than the Prophet himself. If even these early, devout individuals were bound by the rule of covering, then later generations have even greater need for it, as with the approach of the Day of Judgment, impulses, self-centeredness, sexual indulgence, and rape will increase.


"Who among us can claim to have greater self-restraint than the Prophet's noble companions? Who can claim our women are more devout than the Prophet's wives?" 


Thus, guided by doctrine, and building upon its religious symbolism, the veil becomes a symbol of gender segregation. Centered on the Islamic principles of "purity" and "modesty," the Qur'an and the lived practices of Muslim believers have actively developed a series of restraints and taboos. Beyond women wearing veils, the Qur'an contains extensive rules and etiquette governing men's clothing and conduct, marriage customs, morality, religious life, and daily life. Overall, if interpreted literally from the Qur'anic text, the requirement for women to wear the veil aims to maintain distance between the sexes, and this distance serves the doctrine of "purity and modesty," originally intended to prevent social disorder and religious impropriety caused by sexual indulgence and sexual crimes. (Martin, 2016)


However, it is important to note that while the Qur'an views wearing the veil as a way for women to maintain purity and modesty, the text contains numerous instances of gender discrimination, descriptions, and regulations. For example, the text emphasizes a wife's obedience to her husband, legitimizes polygamy, and imposes more "nakedness" (awrah) rules exclusively on women—such as "women must not speak in a soft, alluring tone," "must not perform the call to prayer or loudly recite pilgrimage chants," and "must not leave the home." The Qur'an and Islamic scholars often attribute sexual crimes and bodily indulgence to women, and the requirement for women to wear the veil effectively places on them a gendered responsibility that is not theirs to bear. Like any feudal society around the world, traditional Muslim society is patriarchal, treating men and women differently in politics, daily life, and social governance.


Therefore, in a broader sense, rather than simply viewing the veil as a tool of male domination over women in Muslim countries, it is better understood as a cultural feature projected outward by traditional Muslim nations as patriarchal societies. The interpretations of Muslim scholars resemble a broader version of "victim-blaming," a perspective that objectifies women and exists widely in both Eastern and Western societies. However, for Muslim scholars, the Qur'an, as the divine word of Allah, gives them greater confidence to implement these interpretations.


Image source: PNGTREE


Interestingly, from the individual believer's perspective, the veil is also a symbol of socioeconomic privilege. In fact, the practice of wearing the veil existed in geographical Muslim societies even before the advent of Islam. In ancient times, the veil was not only a mark of noble status but also used to distinguish women's class and social standing. Elite women in ancient Mesopotamia, Byzantium, Greece, and the Persian Empire wore veils as a sign of respect and high status. In ancient Mesopotamia, laws strictly prohibited female slaves and prostitutes from wearing veils based on a woman's class, rank, and occupation; violations were met with severe punishment (Ahmed, 1992).


Similarly, under Ottoman rule, Arab Muslim women used the veil as a symbol of class and a distinctive lifestyle. Since wearing the veil reduced the efficiency of laboring women, a woman who wore a veil signaled that she belonged to a sufficiently wealthy household and a class that did not rely on physical labor for livelihood (Esposito, 2016; Bullock, 2010). From this perspective, the act of Muslim women wearing the veil carries rich economic, cultural, and social meanings. State political intervention further complicates the act of wearing the veil, making it no longer merely a purely religious practice. On the other hand, this indicates that Muslim women's willingness to wear the veil is historically and socially conditioned; although state interference plays a role, from the perspective of elite women themselves, their willingness to wear the veil was not solely out of obedience to Islam or male domination.


Although the veil is not inherently a tool of gender domination, due to its religious and cultural symbolism, veil policies often become tools of political rule. We argue that the relationship between veil policy and political rule generally follows this pattern: when ruling authorities decide to implement extreme religious policies, they promote strict gender segregation under the banner of "purification" and fundamentalism, and this strict gender segregation leads to strict veil policies. As previously mentioned, requiring women to wear the veil represents gender segregation; under authoritarian rule, gender segregation thus transforms from a purely religious act into a tool for promoting extreme politics.


Image source: Pixabay


Most Muslim societies have historically experienced significant turmoil, ruled at various times by Western colonizers, secular governments, and extremist regimes. Therefore, the strictness of veil policies has constantly shifted, influenced by the ideologies of those in power. Besides Afghanistan, the veil-wearing policies in Saudi Arabian and Iranian societies are also typical. Saudi women began wearing veils before the unification of the kingdom in 1932; this tradition was introduced from Turkey during the period of Ottoman rule, and Saudi Arabia later enforced strict veiling policies. These policies began to loosen in the early 21st century and were abolished in 2018 by Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, who allowed women to freely decide how to dress (Abdulaziz, 2018). In Iran, all forms of veils were banned in 1936, but after the Islamic Revolution in 1979, the government mandated that women wear Islamic veils, and in 1983, the criminal code was amended to punish women who appeared in public without veils with flogging (Bucar, 2011).


As a military and political organization advocating Islamic fundamentalism and radical Islamism, the Taliban's historical rule in the last century and its recent policies after regaining power both reflect characteristics of authoritarian politics. Between 1996 and 2001, when the Taliban controlled Afghanistan, they interpreted Islamic doctrine in an extremely strict manner. This interpretation extended to gender policies and, subsequently, to veil policies. Under Taliban rule, Afghan women were not merely required to wear veils, but to wear the "burqa"—a full-body covering with only a small slit to see outside (Martin, 2016). The religious principles of "purity" and "modesty" were pushed to extremes. In February 1998, the Taliban's religious police banned all women from entering the streets of Kabul and issued new regulations ordering people to paint their windows black so that women inside homes could not be seen from outside (Lacayo, 2001).


After regaining power in 2021, the Taliban has attempted to project a more moderate image than before. However, reports indicate that women are still required to wear the burqa, are prohibited from leaving home without a male relative, and female teachers and journalists are banned from working. From media reports around the world, we find that the Taliban's veil policy first sends a clear signal of strong gender-based control to the outside world. The veil, as a prominent and easily recognizable religious and cultural symbol, conveys information about the Taliban's gender politics and national political orientation. Thus, Afghan women wearing veils and burqas have, in numerous news reports, become the primary representatives of oppressed populations in an Islamic religious-political society. The inherently oppressive aesthetic of the veil and burqa resonates with the external imagination of the suffocating social atmosphere characteristic of authoritarian politics (Bullock, 2010).

Veils, the Post-Colonial Era, and Identity:

The World Beyond Islam

●


1

Veils and the Post-Colonial Era


· France's Secularism and the Veil

Secularism (Laïcité)—or the separation of religion and state—is one of the most important legacies of the French Revolution, representing France's comprehensive renewal and serving as the foundation of its democratic republican politics. This principle is enshrined in the constitution, meaning religion cannot serve as the basis for laws, cannot receive government subsidies, and religious activities are not permitted in public schools, which are responsible for shaping the nation's future. Although secularism demands the separation of religion and state and requires government neutrality toward all religions, its precise requirements remain ambiguous. On one hand, it can be interpreted as the government not interfering in private religious practices and remaining neutral toward religious expressions in public, unless such expressions disrupt public order. On the other hand, the principle can also be interpreted as requiring the government to ban any religious influence—including rituals and attire—from appearing in public spaces in order to maintain its secular character.


Image source: PNGTREE


After World War II, France introduced large numbers of immigrants from North Africa and the Middle East to fill labor shortages caused by the war. This policy not only brought rapid economic development to postwar France but also made Islam the second-largest religion in the country after Catholicism. In response to changing demographics, France adopted an "assimilation" policy, requiring religious practices to be confined to the private sphere in order to maintain neutrality toward all religions. However, after conflicts arose between Muslim women and schools over headscarves, the French government passed legislation banning students from wearing "conspicuous" religious symbols in any public school. Before the bill was passed, a commission established by then-President Jacques Chirac concluded that Muslim women were "forced" to wear veils, while also acknowledging that these women "chose" to wear them under conditions of oppression. Nevertheless, wearing a veil in school was "clearly" a symbol of Islamic fundamentalism.


Based on this report, the National Assembly viewed Islam as an oppressive religion, and wearing a veil was seen as a symbol of Islamic propaganda and a manifestation of extremist Islam attacking the French regime. Moreover, since the veil was considered "mandated" and a form of male control over women, the ban on veils was said to liberate oppressed Muslim women and protect students of other faiths from being "harassed" by such "conspicuous" religious symbols. Although on the surface the law continued France's tradition of secularism, the reasons for its passage and the statements made by the National Assembly revealed the French government's ambiguous stance toward the veil: On one hand, the veil symbolized oppressed Muslim women; on the other, their clothing was swiftly equated with the symbol of Islamic fundamentalism, representing "intimidation" and "armed movements.".


· The Burqa and America's "War on Terror"
After the 9/11 terrorist attacks, then-U.S. President George W. Bush portrayed the attacks as representing "a new kind of evil." To prevent this evil from spreading in the Middle East, he declared that "this crusade, this war on terror, will take time," and soon launched the wars in Afghanistan and Iraq. Even with bipartisan support, Bush needed to convince the American public and the international community that invading another country was not an act of revenge but a justified and necessary action. Under this need for public justification, Muslim women oppressed by the Taliban government and forced to wear the burqa became convenient symbols. Women in burqas were frequently—and even "excessively"—featured on the covers of news reports, forming a stark contrast with armed al-Qaeda fighters. This narrative skillfully framed the U.S. military invasion of Afghanistan as an advanced, free Western civilization "rescuing" Afghan women suffering under the brutal violence of the Taliban regime. Many American feminists were inspired by this. For example, First Lady Laura Bush, the first first lady to deliver the Weekly Presidential Radio Address, stated that "the war on terror is also a war for women's rights and dignity," and that al-Qaeda was responsible for the oppression women faced.


Undeniably, under Taliban rule, women's freedom and rights were severely restricted, and women were indeed required to wear burqas in public, with harsh punishments for those who disobeyed. However, when the burqa became a symbol of Taliban oppression, this narrative shifted focus from a regime's oppression of women to the singular, symbolic representation of a traditional garment. This crude treatment effectively constructed a contrast between a "free" Western civilization where clothing choices are available and an "oppressed," backward Islamic civilization of the East, creating a colonialist narrative similar to the "white man's burden."


Behind this symbolic representation, what is first obscured is the diverse cultural meanings of veils and burqas in different social contexts. The veil has at times been seen as a symbol of autonomy in various countries: in Turkey, Egypt, and Algeria, women wore veils while resisting their governments; in Iran's 1979 revolution, the veil was a symbol of women's resistance to Western colonialism; even after 9/11, the loose burqa was used to secretly transport textbooks to underground schools, hide cameras recording Taliban atrocities, or help women escape government persecution.


From these examples, it is evident that the monolithic narrative in American media and government discourse about the veil—and the deliberate disregard for its positive meanings—reflects a paternalistic mindset. The narrative of the "women in need of saving" assumes that Muslim women lack sufficient agency or the ability to change their own circumstances, thus justifying American intervention in so-called brutal regimes for their well-being. However, this political narrative has not genuinely improved the situation of Afghan women: the U.S. government failed to track civilian casualties caused during military confrontations with the Taliban, and Human Rights Watch reports further indicate that certain directives from the U.S. and its allies resulted in significant, avoidable harm and human rights violations. Nevertheless, the veil has been firmly established as a singular symbol of oppression, even becoming associated with terrorism, making the act of removing it a symbolic gesture of "liberation" and embracing freedom and progress. Consequently, when refugee women or Muslim immigrant women voluntarily choose to wear the veil, their autonomous decisions and underlying reasons are often ignored, leading to unfair treatment in employment and daily life.

2

Veils and Identity


· German Refugee Women
In August 2015, Germany saw a large influx of refugees, the majority of whom came from war-torn Syria, Afghanistan, and Iraq. While seeking refuge in a foreign country, these refugees—especially refugee women—faced not only the challenges of a new language and culture and uncertain legal status, but also discrimination due to wearing the hijab. In a survey targeting refugee women in Germany, the vast majority believed that the hijab (hijab) was a cause of discrimination, fear, and hostility from others. At the same time, for some refugee women, wearing the hijab allowed them to maintain their religious faith while going out to study German, participate in social activities, and obtain the legal right to remain in Germany. For others, choosing to wear the hijab was one of the few things over which they, as refugees, women, and members of a religious minority, had any control. Zulikha, a 42-year-old woman from Afghanistan, said,


"I know taking off my hijab would make my life much easier. But I won't do it. This is my body, my faith… I have lost everything—my house, my home, my political influence, my independence… The hijab belongs to me, and I will not take it off."


For them, even while suffering negative consequences from wearing the hijab, the very act of wearing it represents their right to choose—their choice to resist societal discipline over their clothing, their choice of what they wish to wear. Because in a sense, both prohibition and compulsion strip away the right to choose. They point out that the freedom in Germany is only "the freedom they (i.e., Germans) define (a narrow version of freedom)," but "I (we) don't have the freedom to wear the hijab." For refugee women in Germany, wearing the hijab is a demonstration of their own rights, representing their resistance against Western society's discipline over women's bodies. In this sense, the hijab has become a fusion of structural constraints and agentic performance.


Image source: Pixabay


·Muslim Communities in Post-9/11 America
After the 9/11 terrorist attacks, an increasing number of second- and third-generation Muslim immigrants in the United States chose to wear the stigmatized hijab. To mainstream Americans, Islam has long been portrayed as a backward and oppressive Eastern culture. American media often use derogatory tones to describe Muslim communities; in 2001, researchers found that out of 900 films featuring Muslim characters, only three portrayed them in ways that defied mainstream stereotypes. American textbooks have even depicted Muslims as barbaric and irrational groups. Under these circumstances, Muslim women in the U.S. hoped to challenge these stereotypes by wearing the hijab. As early as the 1980s, the wife of a Saudi ambassador said, "When a luxury scarf falls on my shoulders, you think it's elegant and fashionable; but when I use it to cover my hair, you think I'm oppressed." .


After the 9/11 attacks, when the hijab became a symbol of Islam—and even of the "enemy"—for Muslim communities in the United States, wearing the hijab became the most tangible expression of their resistance against the stigmatization of Muslims by mainstream culture. They boldly declared their religious alignment with "America's enemies," and the attacks they subsequently faced made visible the hostility that had previously been hidden within media and culture. At the same time, the media coverage of 9/11 also forced Muslim communities to realize they needed to change mainstream perceptions. These second-generation immigrants used their language advantages to communicate with the public, linking the act of "wearing the hijab" to core American values such as "religious freedom" and "freedom of speech," thus legitimizing their embrace of Islam.


Three

Autonomy, Self-Protection, and Contemporary Culture:

The Perspective of Muslim Women

●


In Islamic traditional views, the hijab is seen as a social and moral regulator that grants women respect and protects their chastity and livelihood. Over time, wearing the hijab has acquired social value and become a symbol of culture and identity (Mizel, 2020). At the same time, with population mobility and the spread of global culture, even within Islamic cultures, styles of dress have become increasingly diverse, reflecting different regional and cultural interpretations of religious requirements across various Muslim-majority countries. Today, Muslim women hold varied views and attitudes toward wearing the hijab, and the hijab carries multiple meanings for them.


1

The Right to Choose Whether to Wear the Hijab

—Religious Identity and Self-Expression


In traditional views, wearing the hijab was, before becoming a social behavior and cultural custom, a religious duty and a commitment to moral necessity. Besides religious tradition, cultural and family values also influence Muslim women's views on the hijab. Many Muslim women become interested in the hijab during adolescence, influenced by female family members who wear it. They come to accept wearing the hijab as a necessary step in their transition to adulthood and decide to wear it under family influence. Today, however, the reasons Muslim women wear the hijab have become increasingly diverse, and these reasons may change over time and with social context.


For some women, the hijab has become a means of resisting the excessive display of female appearance. Supporters of this view argue that removing the hijab and outer garments to cater to the male gaze does not equate to women's liberation. In one study, women who wore the hijab said it compelled employers in the workplace to interact with them based on their qualifications rather than their appearance, thus creating a fairer competitive environment. However, in Western countries, research has found that women who wear the hijab are less likely to be hired. Of course, some women choose to wear the hijab for practical reasons. Because the hijab can reduce others' comments about women in public spaces and decrease incidents of harassment on the street and at work.


In many parts of the world, women still face informal pressures—sometimes even physical attacks—regarding whether to support or oppose wearing the hijab. Western societies often wonder: why do Muslim women, living in Western societies without traditional cultural pressures and already possessing the right to dress freely, still choose to wear the hijab? Yet for many Muslim immigrant women in the West, the hijab symbolizes devotion and piety, and wearing it is their own choice. In fact, the choice to wear or not wear the hijab is an issue of religious identity and self-expression, not the religious oppression commonly assumed.


Image source: Pixabay


Therefore, we cannot crudely reduce the issue of the hijab to religion or patriarchal oppression, nor can we ignore the voices of women who are silenced in discussions about the hijab. In a small number of countries, women have no choice in whether to wear the hijab—governments either mandate women to wear headscarves and veils (e.g., Afghanistan under Taliban rule), or prohibit wearing the hijab in certain public spaces (e.g., the French government's ban on Muslim women wearing the hijab in public places).


2

Halo Effect and Self-Protection

—Muslim Women's Views on the Hijab


Researcher Pasha-Zaidi extended the concept of the "halo effect" to propose an internal Muslim "hijab effect," meaning that Muslim women who wear the Islamic headscarf are generally perceived as possessing more positive attributes. Since Muslim women do not wear the hijab in front of other women, there are also certain internal perceptions among women about those who wear it. In traditional Muslim countries, women who wear the hijab are seen by other women as more traditional in social and gender relations, more family-oriented, more obedient to men, more patriotic, more united, and possibly more virtuous (Witkowski, 1999), while women who do not wear it may be seen as "less Muslim."


Different ways of wearing the hijab can also provide visual clues about the wearer's cultural background, offering a glimpse into how cultural norms influence hijab practices. Witkowski (year) studied Lebanese and Kuwaiti women's views on the hijab. He found that Lebanese women were perceived as more "Western" than Kuwaiti women because Lebanese women wear Islamic clothing and the hijab less frequently and are more likely to wear makeup and jewelry.


The choice of whether or not to wear the hijab is also diverse. While some Muslim women (especially Muslim women in Western countries) take pride in wearing the veil and identify strongly with their Islamic faith, others have long struggled for the right not to wear a headscarf. For example, for many women in Iran's authoritarian society, the veil is a symbol of oppression, depriving them of their most basic human rights and freedom of dress. In more extreme cases, such as in Afghanistan under Taliban rule, wearing a veil or even a full burka has become a desperate measure to protect one's life. According to The Guardian (citation needed), some Afghan women have said they do not want to hide behind a curtain-like burka, as wearing it means accepting the Taliban government and granting them control over their lives. For them, donning the heavy veil signifies the loss of women's independence and freedom. Of course, some women have used the burka as a means of concealment to express themselves and resist. For instance, Afghanistan's first independent all-female band, Burka Band, performs fully covered in blue burqas, making a clear protest against the Taliban's Islamic dress code.

3

The Islamic Veil and Contemporary Culture


Western feminist perspectives often regard unveiling as a sign of liberation, tending to equate the veil with female oppression and subjugation, while some Islamic feminists view the veil as a symbol of resistance against materialism and Western cultural imperialism. However, it is undeniable that Islamic clothing elements such as veils and robes are increasingly appearing in Western mainstream fashion industries. As Muslim women in developing countries gradually transition from traditional to modern lifestyles, more and more women are receiving education, entering the workforce, and earning income. As modern Muslim women, while adhering to Islamic dress codes, they are also transforming their styles, adopting veils that align with contemporary fashion aesthetics.


Today, most Muslim countries have embraced Islamic cosmopolitanism, allowing Muslim women to express their individuality through the latest fashion trends—various styles, accessories, and bold colors—as long as they comply with dress coverage requirements. An increasing number of Muslim women are transforming traditional ways of wearing the veil into modern styles, and contemporary, attractive, fashionable veils are becoming popular. The veil thus preserves Muslim women's piety and modesty while also offering them space for self-expression. At the same time, a distinct "modest fashion" movement unique to Muslim women has emerged and become part of consumer culture. The growing demand among Muslim women for fashionable veils and headscarves has given rise to a thriving new industry.


"Modest Fashion" styles

Image source: Composite:WWD/REX/Shutterstock/Instagram/


An important reason behind this surge in modest fashion is that Muslim women outside the Middle East and South Asia are increasingly adopting more conservative dress globally. This shift has occurred over the past 30 years, as religious interpretations in many countries have become more conservative, and this trend has naturally extended to women's clothing. This "modest fashion" is thus also a result of the resurgence of traditional Islamic values intersecting with popular culture.
