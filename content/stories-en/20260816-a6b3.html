News


New report finds,
South Asian survivors of sexual violence struggle to obtain compensation


On July 22, 2026, a new report titled *Good Practice Guidelines for Compensation of Survivors of Sexual Violence in South Asia*, jointly produced by the South Asian Movement for Access to Justice (SAMAJ), reveals why many survivors of sexual violence across South Asia still struggle to receive financial compensation despite legal protections. Beyond devastating psychological and physical impacts, sexual violence often causes significant economic harm to survivors. While governments across South Asia have acknowledged this by establishing financial compensation programs, new research finds these systems are often underfunded, inconsistently implemented, and difficult to navigate, leaving many survivors unable to access the financial support they are legally entitled to. SAMAJ's regional comparison of compensation programs in Bangladesh, India, Nepal, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, and the Maldives found survivors often face complex and lengthy criminal justice procedures before receiving compensation.

As U.S. intensifies global anti-abortion efforts,

African women are dying


The Trump administration has expanded restrictions on the use of U.S. foreign aid funding, banning international organizations that support or promote abortion-related services from receiving U.S. funding—aid potentially amounting to $30 billion. Reports indicate that U.S. conservative anti-abortion groups have long pushed for abortion restrictions both domestically and globally, and the new policy further amplifies their influence. In African countries whose healthcare systems are highly dependent on foreign aid, these actions are undermining reproductive health services. The region has the world's highest rate of unsafe abortions and the highest maternal mortality rate globally—including the world's highest number of maternal deaths per 100,000 abortions. Two decades ago, African nations signed the *Maputo Protocol*, an international treaty that recognizes safe abortion as a fundamental right. Emboldened by the Trump administration's policies, U.S. anti-abortion groups now aim to eliminate even these limited pathways to safe abortion. Local healthcare workers and women's rights advocates warn: as anti-abortion policies and funding restrictions tighten, more African women may face death due to lack of access to safe reproductive healthcare.

Associated Press News (2026, July 18). Women are dying in Africa as US ramps up its global battle against abortion.



News


UN Women report states,

As aid is cut, women-led organizations bearing the burden of humanitarian response are being dismantled


On July 10, 2026, UN Women released a new report titled *Beyond the Breaking Point*, examining the impact of aid cuts. Based on responses from 855 women-led and women's rights organizations across 52 crisis- and conflict-affected countries, the report finds that as global armed conflicts reach their highest levels in 80 years and conflict-related sexual violence has doubled, funding for women's organizations providing life-saving services is drying up. The survey shows that since January 2025, 84% of women's organizations have reported increased demand for their services, while nearly nine in ten can no longer meet service needs during crises, leaving at least one million women and girls without access to essential support—due to the steepest annual decline in official development assistance in history. Women's organizations at risk of closure are operating on the frontlines of the world's most severe humanitarian crises, including in Afghanistan, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and Haiti.

Asian American groups fear Trump's escalating rhetoric

will fuel bias and racism in the U.S.


Asian American groups on Friday condemned President Donald Trump's recent speeches, calling them dangerous threats to minority communities already facing attacks and hostility during a time of growing unrest and division. In a prime-time televised address from the White House East Room, Trump repeated unsubstantiated claims that China interfered in U.S. elections. Analysts say many of the voter registration data points the president cited from previously classified reports are disputed and not new. "This is an extension of racism and xenophobia," said Joanna Young Chinderman, director of an Asian American organization.

News


Rising break-ins targeting Asian Americans in Oregon

prompt changes to public safety laws


Ongoing break-ins targeting Asian Americans in Oregon have prompted state lawmakers to grant local judges clearer authority to deny release to suspects, and state laws may introduce further changes to combat these crimes. Senate Bill 1516, effective March 31, ensures local magistrates can consider community safety and the risk of defendants fleeing the area or failing to appear in court when denying bail. Jenny Jonak, president of the Asian American Coalition (AACO), said repeated attacks have left many local Asian American families frustrated and unsafe. "Despite arrests, prosecutions, public meetings, and repeated warnings, these break-ins continue, and the pattern targeting Asian American households seems endless," she said.

Two Chinese scholars, Wang Hong and Deng Yu, solve century-old problem

win world's highest honor in mathematics for the first time


In July 2026, two Chinese scholars became the first from China to receive the Fields Medal, widely regarded as the highest honor in global mathematics. Chinese mathematicians Wang Hong and Deng Yu were awarded the Fields Medal at the International Congress of Mathematicians held in Philadelphia, USA, from July 23 to 30, 2026, alongside John Pardon of the United States and Jacob Tsimerman of Canada, each receiving 15,000 Canadian dollars (approximately $10,500 USD; £8,000 GBP). Wang Hong, a 35-year-old Chinese mathematician specializing in harmonic analysis, is the third woman in history to receive this prestigious award.

Comedian Jenny Yang supports immigrant communities through

comedy tours in immigrant grocery stores


In 2026, Jenny Yang, a seasoned stand-up and sketch comedy performer, launched the "Good Egg" immigrant grocery store comedy tour to resist the Trump administration's crackdown on immigrants from Asian, Latinx, and Black communities. During the performances, audiences sit between shelves and checkout counters surrounded by Asian snacks, rice bags, and seasonings. Yang even tosses snacks like shrimp crackers into the crowd, adding a lighthearted atmosphere to the show. She weaves together stories of immigration, infertility treatments, and racial issues throughout her act. The tour has expanded from an initial plan of five shows to nearly 20, reaching cities with significant immigrant communities such as Los Angeles, Minneapolis, New York, and San Francisco, and partnering with nonprofit organizations including the Asian Law Caucus to raise funds for local immigrant rights groups.

Australian far-right leader Pauline Hanson loses appeal in racism case

South Asian Senator Mehreen Faruqi wins case


An Australian court on Monday rejected the appeal by far-right politician Pauline Hanson in a racial discrimination case. Hanson had told a Muslim senator to "go back to Pakistan" in reference to Pakistani-born Senator Mehreen Faruqi. Faruqi migrated from Pakistan to Australia in 1992 and is the first Pakistani woman to become a member of the Australian Parliament. She sued Hanson over the comment. A three-judge panel of the Federal Court of Australia unanimously upheld the 2024 ruling, finding that Pauline Hanson's racially discriminatory remarks against Mehreen Faruqi were unlawful and not protected by free speech. "Today, justice has been served," Faruqi told reporters outside the Sydney courthouse. "This victory is for everyone who has ever been told to go back where they came from."
