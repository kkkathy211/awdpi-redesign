The flames of the Israel-Palestine conflict have subsided somewhat, yet the losses brought by the war will require considerable time to heal, and the traumatic memories will persist permanently, difficult to erase. According to statistics from the Palestinian Ministry of Health, 70% of the casualties caused by the conflict are women and children. Against this backdrop, discussing the survival plight of women in religious war zones appears extremely urgent and important. Therefore, this article will combine Khaled Hosseini's novel A Thousand Splendid Suns to attempt, through brief character analysis, to see the sorrow and suffering of civilians amid war, and to see the extraordinarily difficult circumstances faced by women caught in such situations.


1

the current multiple layers of hardship


Although this novel tells the story of two women's resistance against the historical backdrop of Afghanistan, this does not prevent it from serving as a pathway path From it, we can still deeply feel the harms brought by war, the constraints certain religious forms impose on women, and the faint glimmer of hope the author wishes to convey to readers.



The most direct threat war brings to people is physical safety. The protagonist Leila's brother unfortunately died in combat, and her parents also perished tragically in the bombing; even Leila's friend Jiti died, and despite her vibrant life, she could not avoid the fate of being dismembered. Even more heartbreaking is that Jiti's mother had to suppress her emotions to pick up the fragments of her daughter's body piece by piece to reassemble it; and through the accounts of people like Tariq, one can also hear that looting, rape, and indiscriminate killing due to the war are ever-present... Those who appear merely as a set of data in the news are, in the book and in real life, living individuals who should have had their own stories and futures. However, like the collapsed Buddhas of Bamiyan, their lives either came to an abrupt end, disappearing in the flames of war; or, having endured countless scars, grew dim and passed this life in a hasty, rough manner.

Image source: pexel

Under patriarchal systems, without male support, civilian women in war face relatively harder lives, especially women in countries with religious backgrounds. The first impact war brings to Laila is dropping out of school. Despite her intelligence and her parents' openness, they could not overcome their fears for her safety on the way to school. Even before conflict erupted locally, boys bullied Laila; after war broke out, through others' accounts, we learn of even worse incidents such as abduction and rape. Laila, who narrowly escaped a bombing, was forced to marry Rasheed in order to survive the war with her child. Despite Rasheed's heinous nature, he still became the "pillar of the family" who went out to earn food for his two wives and children during that time, and even bought anti-inflammatory medicine when he initially "rescued" Laila. Since religion arises within patriarchal societies, its constraints on women are even more pronounced. Therefore, women with no educational background undoubtedly face greater hardship in such wars, confronting harsher environments. Under the same conditions, their personal access to medical care, employment, physical safety, and efforts to claim various rights and protections are more challenging than for men.


When the past has already perished, how can the living stand alone? Khaled does not focus solely on the material suffering caused by bombings; he also describes in great detail the psychological trauma brought by war, raising a humanitarian voice for people in war zones. This includes the pain caused by the deaths of Ahmad and Noor to Laila's mother, Fariba's descent into madness from grief over losing her sons, her indifference toward Laila's growth, and her refusal to leave the land where her children died, ultimately resulting in both her and her husband perishing in a bombing, leaving pregnant Laila alone among the ruins. It also includes Mariam and Laila's sleepless nights, people painting their cars yellow to look like taxis; and even after the war ends, Laila still frequently recalls how she sought survival amid despair during days of constant shelling—these images are all reflections of war-affected people's inability to find peace. According to Caruth's trauma theory, traumatic memories cannot be forgotten; survivors can only repeatedly re-experience and review them until they accept the trauma as part of their lived experience. A recent paper in the journal Nature also shows that painful memories from childhood can be recorded in human DNA, passed to the next generation, and increase their risk of depression. Even so, do not attempt to offer sympathy, because a fleeting glance at media reports from outside the conflict may be someone else's entire lifetime.


2

Once everything has passed, will the future be better?


When the external war ends, the women in the book do not get a chance to catch their breath. A separate, endless battle unique to women quietly begins. There are two female protagonists in the book: one is Laila, the other is Mariam. Unlike Laila's upbringing, Mariam's family of origin is deeply dysfunctional: she is a illegitimate child, and her mother Nana loves and resents her simultaneously. Despite Mariam's strong unwillingness, she is forced to marry Rasheed and endures his abuse and beatings after marriage. It is precisely because of her timid nature that her eventual act of killing her husband and achieving multiple forms of redemption carries such impact. At the beginning, compared to Laila and others, her image seems closer to that of a marginalized woman—uneducated, required to wear a burqa when going outside, and completely obedient to her husband at home.


But if we move the reading timeline back to the previous Taliban rule, we encounter the following scenes: schools no longer admit female students, laws reinstate stoning as punishment for women; morality police patrol the streets, and Laila cannot even go out alone to visit her daughter placed in a private school; her aunt, a painter who once loved freedom, now wears a headscarf; hospitals refuse to admit female patients, and medicine is not provided to women; even the few remaining female doctors must perform surgery while wearing a burqa... Previously, many online discussions argued that misogyny stems from poverty, and that women's issues are class issues, but class cannot encompass all women's issues. The above passage alone is sufficient proof that the root of women's issues lies in structural discrimination. Unless this structure is deconstructed from within the realm of power, female subjectivity will not be affirmed, and women cannot achieve true self-identification and liberation. The novel's ending is undoubtedly full of light: author Khaled allows Laila to return to an Afghanistan no longer ruled by the Taliban, where everything seems vibrant and capable of rebuilding a better future.

Image source: unsplash

In reality, however, women in the Middle East have little happiness to speak of; their status continues to decline, a cause for concern. In 2023, Iran passed stricter hijab laws; this year, the Taliban regained power in Afghanistan, stripping "Lailas" of various rights, even forbidding them from speaking publicly; in the past November, Iraq proposed lowering the legal marriage age for women from 18 to 9, while also depriving women of divorce rights, child custody, and inheritance rights... More and more religious countries, due to historical and other reasons, are implementing restrictive policies and enacting laws unfavorable to women. Under such social conditions, it is difficult to expect that after violent armed conflicts, ruling parties in these countries will offer sincere and positive responses to local women. The mournful songs of past unrest have not yet faded, and their future paths still offer few broad choices.


When a gun fires on one side, there are no winners—gender issues are no different. Through the novel's example of the absence of female doctors, we can basically infer that such unfair treatment not only harms the rights of local women but also oppresses men, and over time, worsens inequality. But even in such bleak circumstances, one can still intermittently hear the voices of some brave women rising from confinement. Resistance is undoubtedly effective, for these women, even if progress is slow, their very existence is a form of resistance. In the current war situation, they must desperately preserve their lives in order to have the capital to fight for a blood-stained path for future generations.


3

What we can do goes beyond cold observation.


Although the outbreak of war and the establishment of systems have complex historical and cultural causes, this cannot justify military violence against civilians and women. Afghan women's rights media, when showing solidarity with Gaza, made their stance clear: In the face of absolute power, silence is undoubtedly complicity. Therefore, besides practical aid such as donating supplies in one's personal capacity or joining Doctors Without Borders, we can also pay more attention to and discuss such news, speaking out and making suggestions to reduce violence. But please do not compare war-affected countries with other nations, nor bring up safety or luck. As Laila reflects during her argument with Tariq at the end of the novel, safety and luck are themselves coexisting privileges, a form of self-proclaimed superiority from a position of power—such actions are essentially no different from aggression.


In terms of women's rights, we can still speak and write on their behalf, protest injustice, even if our efforts seem slight. I believe this does not contradict respecting religious culture; under divine decree, all beings are equal. After all, women's rights are fundamental human rights. Religion should not serve as a restriction at the level of state machinery; it ought to be cherished as a personal pursuit. If a religion takes challenging human rights as its primary goal, it has strayed from the original divinity bestowed upon it by people. As an Iranian girl said when explaining the situation of wearing headscarves: "Don't say this is something we chose ourselves. No path exists inherently from the beginning. Laws are rigid, but thoughts are fluid and can change. As long as no one starts covering their head as prescribed, the morality police won't intervene too much."

We sincerely hope the flames of war will subside soon and people's lives return to peace; that these women can gain enough choices, break free from their shackles as soon as possible, and go to the world they long for, loving everything they wish to love. May they forever remain brave.
