As an effective means of resisting domestic violence, the term "self-defense" frequently appears in victims' arguments and in courtrooms during judicial trials. However, when violence strikes, blindly taking self-protective actions may turn into evidence of guilt for the victim, overturning what would otherwise be a clear victory and placing vulnerable groups under a dangerous wall.

Therefore, Before bravely protecting ourselves from harm, we should all understand the boundaries of courage and the bottom line of protection, allowing "justifiable defense" to exert its maximum effect within the legal framework.

#

I. The Defense Conditions of Self-Defense



Under normal circumstances, self-defense is defined as the right of a victim to use a sufficient degree of counter-force or violence to prevent themselves from suffering violence.


In the United States, the commonly accepted judicial standard is "(a person) has the privilege to use force that appears reasonably necessary to protect themselves from an apparent, unlawful, and immediate threat of violence by another," and is determined by four main factors: (1) unprovoked attack, (2) presence of a threat involving imminent harm or death, (3) use of objectively reasonable degree of force in response, (4) objectively reasonable fear of injury or death. When these conditions are met, a sufficient defense for self-defense is established.


However, due to limitations such as the time and location of the violent incident, the actual circumstances of self-defense often differ from judicial standards, so related rulings are not "one-size-fits-all," but rather decided case by case, with judges' decisions often wavering.


For example, when protecting oneself, how do we determine whether the force used is appropriate? When does it become excessive defense? What if the victim provoked the attack? What happens if the victim reasonably perceived a threat, even if the threat did not actually exist? What if the victim's understanding was subjectively genuine but objectively unreasonable?



Image source: freepik



On March 28, 1992, in California, a woman shot and killed her partner Albert Hampton at home. When local officers arrived after receiving a report, she immediately surrendered, openly told the police where the gun was, and admitted shooting her partner. She explained, "He deserved it. I couldn't take it anymore. I told him not to beat me again!" During the trial, the defendant was charged with murder using a firearm. The defense argued that the killing was in self-defense and presented expert testimony on the defendant's "battered woman syndrome."


Battered woman syndrome typically refers to a unique behavioral pattern in women resulting from long-term physical, sexual, and emotional abuse by a husband or boyfriend.


Lenore Walker published The Battered Woman Syndrome in 1984, proposing two theories—"learned helplessness" and "cycle of violence"—to explain the psychological changes in women with battered woman syndrome. The 1987 case R. v. Lavallee, [1990] 1 S.C.R. 852 was one of the earliest cases to accept battered woman syndrome as admissible evidence. After continuous development, battered woman syndrome evolved from a psychological and medical concept into a legal defense basis for women experiencing domestic violence.


However, in the Hampton shooting case, the judge stated that battered woman syndrome could not be used to determine the necessity and urgency of the defendant's killing. Due to gender bias in self-defense and controversies over diagnostic criteria, battered woman syndrome is often not accepted as legal defense evidence in practice. The jury found the defendant guilty of intentional homicide using a firearm and sentenced her to eight years in prison. In a subsequent appeal, the Supreme Court overturned the verdict, stating that the defendant's testimony provided a reasonable basis for perfect self-defense against all charges.

II. The Gray Area of Legal Judgments



In contrast to perfect self-defense, there is "imperfect self-defense," which occurs when a person retaliates out of fear of imminent bodily harm, but that fear is objectively unreasonable. Imperfect self-defense does not exempt a person from a violent crime, but it can reduce the charges and penalties involved.



Image source: freepik



Self-defense standards and motivations vary across U.S. states, with relevant ruling principles including "duty to retreat," "stand-your-ground law," and "castle doctrine." These principles largely point to a widely accepted idea: individuals may protect themselves from harm under appropriate circumstances, even if such actions would normally constitute a crime. This makes the United States one of the countries with the strongest legal protections for citizens' right to lawful self-defense.

(States with these ruling principles: https://scharfflawfirm.com/self-defense-laws-u-s/)


However, when these seemingly favorable self-defense laws intersect with domestic violence, the situation becomes complicated.


"Duty to retreat," also known as "responsibility to retreat," has been abolished in most states when non-lethal force is involved, but a few states still require a person to attempt to avoid direct confrontation before using deadly force.


Nicole Addimando endured horrific physical and sexual abuse from her husband for years. In 2017, when he again threatened her with a gun, Nicole shot and killed him. She was convicted of second-degree murder and criminal possession of a firearm in the second degree in Poughkeepsie, New York. Nicole's failed self-defense argument was closely tied to the "duty to retreat." But how can a woman enduring long-term domestic violence be expected to remain calm, retreat to a corner, and repeatedly warn her abuser before shooting when facing a death threat? The "duty to retreat" clearly contradicts the original intent of self-defense.


Therefore, in contrast to the "duty to retreat," many U.S. states have enacted "stand-your-ground laws," allowing victims to directly confront their attackers. Similarly, the "castle doctrine" permits victims to use deadly weapons to defend against intruders within their "personal territory," including vehicles, homes, and workplaces.


Over the years, defenders of "stand-your-ground laws" and efforts to expand gun rights have frequently cited female victims in their arguments. It is common to see cases of women shooting rapists used as justification for broader gun rights. "Calls to repeal 'stand your ground' are anti-women," wrote a Florida legislator. Yet, are "stand-your-ground laws" universally protective of all women? The facts appear otherwise.


Marissa Alexander, a Black woman living in Florida, fired a warning shot at her abusive husband. She requested a "stand-your-ground law" immunity hearing, but it was denied. Had the charges not been dropped, she would have faced 60 years in prison. Data investigations point to the same conclusion: "stand-your-ground laws" appear to fail in protecting women of color. Nationally, in cases where women killed adult men, killings by white women of Black men were most likely to be deemed justified, accounting for 13.5% of cases; in contrast, 2.6% of white women killing white men were considered justified, and only 5.7% of non-white women killing Black men were accepted as sufficient justification for self-defense—revealing clear disputes regarding race and class.

III. Gun Issues and Empowerment Self-Defense


Image source: freepik



In the United States, intimate partner violence occurs frequently and severely threatens the lives, safety, and dignity of American women.


According to the 2010 National Intimate Partner and Sexual Violence Survey (NISVS), nearly 20 people per minute experience physical abuse by an intimate partner, and one in three women has experienced some form of physical violence by an intimate partner. To protect themselves from violence, these women have adopted various resistance strategies.


In the 2007 U.S. Women's Choices of Strategies to Protect Themselves from Violence survey, behavioral strategies were among the most common protective measures. "Being alert to surroundings" (99%) and "locking doors at home" (96%) were the most prevalent.


Among all behavioral strategies, martial arts and self-defense training (19%) were the least commonly used protective methods by women. When asked whether they had ever "avoided or changed things they wanted to do to protect themselves from violence," over 70% of women answered affirmatively. In addition, it is worth noting that apart from gun use, the ways American women protect themselves from violent harm do not differ from those used by women in other developed countries.


Women who have experienced direct or indirect harm are 1.58 times more likely to own a firearm and more than twice as likely to carry weapons other than guns. However, beyond serving as a tool for women to resist violence, firearms are also used by perpetrators as instruments of power, control, and abuse, increasing women's risks of suicide or homicide.


According to the 2016 systematic literature review Nonfatal Gun Use in Intimate Partner Violence: A Systematic Review of the Literature, nearly 1 million women have been shot by intimate partners, and 4.5 million women have been threatened with guns.


Amid escalating gun threats, empowerment self-defense has been proven effective in reducing violence. This program was established in 2003 by an Israeli organization called El HaLev, specifically serving women suffering psychological trauma after assault, as well as other vulnerable groups. The training model includes easy-to-learn physical self-defense techniques, as well as a series of comprehensive strategies such as verbal techniques, awareness skills, and confidence-building exercises.


Additionally, self-defense training shares similarities with exposure therapy (which refers to safely exposing individuals to things they fear and wish to avoid). Participants not only confront simulated attacks but also learn and practice active responses. Jim Hopper, psychologist and assistant professor at Harvard Medical School, explains that over time, these repeated simulations can transform traumatic memories of past assaults into new, empowering "I have power" memories on a large scale.


Research indicates that self-defense training can help women gain a sense of control and ownership over their personal safety, enabling them to respond effectively to the threat of sexual violence.

IV. How to Practice Effective Self-Defense



For victims without prior empowerment-based self-defense training, taking appropriate resistance measures according to the current environment is the most timely and secure approach during the practical exercise of legitimate defense.


If the attacker attempts to grab or drag, biting their hand to cause pain and then escaping can be effective; if carrying sharp objects such as pens or small knives, they can be used unexpectedly for self-defense. Additionally, using fingers, elbows, or heels to strike vulnerable areas such as the groin, eyes, or throat can inflict critical damage. Utilizing hard household items nearby or natural elements like sand, dust, pipes, stones, or bricks can also hinder the attacker's further aggression.


Given that some state laws include provisions related to the "duty to retreat," preparing preventive measures in advance and avoiding harm is the most beneficial form of self-protection. Typically, perpetrators exhibit abnormal behaviors, emotional fluctuations, and verbal provocations before committing violence. Domestic violence survivors can observe and collect clues, issue early warnings, plan safe escape routes, set emergency contacts and safe shelters, and prepare an "escape bag" containing personal identification, property documents, spare clothing, and other essentials for emergencies.


Before violence occurs, try to go to crowded places or seek shelter with family or friends. During the incident, avoid being alone to ensure there are witnesses to support your resistance. After an act of violence, to prevent recurrence, victims should carefully gather evidence (such as phone recordings, text message screenshots, photos of injuries, medical records, etc.). Report the incident to the police promptly to hold offenders accountable, or apply for a restraining order at the local court.


Beyond victims fighting back, ordinary individuals in close relationships, potential victims of any form of violence, and bystanders all play an indispensable role in combating domestic violence.


Morally, witnesses have an obligation to stop domestic violence. Informed individuals should rationally assess the danger and severity of the situation, take timely actions such as reporting and assisting in evidence collection, carefully choose the method, timing, and tools of intervention, and ensure the safety of both themselves and the victim (for example, knocking on the door, ringing the doorbell, or asking to borrow something to pressure the abuser into stopping the violence temporarily); then seek help from capable individuals or call the police directly, continuously monitor the follow-up situation, assess whether the intervention was effective, and pay attention to the victim's mental health.


As a social issue rooted in gender relations, domestic violence has never been merely a private family matter; it concerns the degree of harmony and civility within communities and the space for marginalized voices under power imbalances.


Therefore, whether bystanders or individuals directly involved, mobilizing social forces to address domestic violence constitutes the broader definition of "legitimate defense."

Additionally, each U.S. state has established social services, legal counseling, family mediation, and support groups related to domestic violence, specifically serving vulnerable populations.
