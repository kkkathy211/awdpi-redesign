University of Pennsylvania publishes first Asian student publication in 20 years 

Aiming to give voice to Asian communities


(1) Incident Summary

According to English-language media reports, Hawthorn, an Asian student publication founded by students at the University of Pennsylvania, will publish its first article in early April. The magazine was launched by the student group Rice Up, which is dedicated to amplifying Asian voices and advocating for better Asian representation in media and politics. Hawthorn is the first Asian publication at Penn in nearly 20 years, with a focus on showcasing Asian art, history, and culture. The publication's leaders say its purpose is to raise awareness and understanding of Asian communities within the campus and the broader Philadelphia community.

(2) Commentary Perspective

1. The necessity of Asian-led publications

Jason Zhao, one of the co-presidents of Rice Up, said the last similar Asian publication at Penn was in 2005, when there was a magazine called Propaganda Silk that focused on promoting Asian literature. "Although the Asian community has been rapidly growing every year, Penn has not had an Asian publication for nearly 20 years. Penn needs voices that represent Asians, so we can express our unique perspectives—especially at a time when Asians across the U.S. are facing rising violence."

Recently, the supplementary document of the FBI's annual hate crime report showed that hate crime incidents in the U.S. surged by 11.6% year-on-year in 2021, with 64.5% of victims targeted due to racial bias. Analysts point out that the escalating hate crimes highlight deep-rooted human rights issues in the U.S., including widespread, systemic discrimination against minority groups such as Native Americans, African Americans, Asians, and Latinx communities. However, traditional news and media often overlook the Asian perspective. "Through magazines and writing, we can help people realize that when we understand Asian communities better, we can fight ignorance—the root of discrimination and stereotypes," said James Huang, another co-founder of Hawthorn.


2. Exploring anti-Asian hate from multiple professions and topics


The leaders of Hawthorn said the magazine intentionally features diverse themes to provide breadth and depth on Asian experiences. Articles will cover fashion, music, and food, while also discussing anti-Asian hate in the U.S. and conducting interviews with political figures. Asian musicians, writers, artists, and chefs in Pennsylvania and the wider Philadelphia community are among their focus. The team hopes to collaborate with other student organizations on campus to foster a sense of community that transcends identity boundaries. For example, they are planning to partner with campus music groups to raise funds for Asian education in Philadelphia. Anti-Asian hate is not just a term or a news event—it is the real-life experience and suffering of many compatriots living abroad.

Mistaken identity? 20-year-old Chinese man in Prato slashed in throat, ex-boyfriend of boss's daughter wanted


Chinastreet, Italy, April 26, 2023, Prato: A man launched a surprise attack outside a company in Prato, possibly mistaking his target, and stabbed a worker in the throat. The victim may never speak again.


Attempted murder driven by passion. A man, abandoned by his girlfriend, traveled from Massa to Prato and appeared at the woman's family business, targeting a young employee whom he believed to be a relative of his ex-girlfriend. A Chinese factory worker was mistakenly slashed in the throat. Now, this 20-year-old Chinese man faces the risk of never speaking again, as the knife severed his vocal cords. This horrific incident occurred in February but has only now been reported. Police are tracking the attacker, whose identity has been confirmed as a compatriot of the victim, and the most credible lead points to a crime of passion. He is suspected of attempted murder. It was revealed that the worker was stabbed inside a delivery van belonging to the company where he worked. The attacker, reportedly from Massa where the victim lives, came seeking revenge after his girlfriend left him following another abusive incident days earlier.


When the attacker arrived outside the company run by the ex-girlfriend's family, he noticed the 20-year-old man boarding a minivan. He may have mistaken the victim for a relative of the girl and followed him into the vehicle. He then ordered the victim to start the car and drive over 100 meters away. It remains unclear what happened during the brief journey inside the van. The fact is, on Via dei Confini, several passing Pakistani cyclists discovered the 20-year-old worker lying on the ground outside the vehicle, covered in blood. They called the police, and an ambulance from the 118 emergency center and officers arrived at the scene. The worker was eventually taken to Prato Hospital, where he remained in a coma for several weeks. He was later transferred to Careggi Hospital in Florence, where he underwent delicate surgery on his vocal cords. He is now undergoing rehabilitation but still cannot speak. He may never regain his voice.


It was the company owner where the victim worked who directed the investigation toward the Chinese man from Massa. Since the suspect had been involved in a heated argument with the girl just days before the stabbing, suspicion immediately fell on him. The man has been wanted for several months but has not yet been found. The investigation continues to clarify the chilling details of this case.

Beware, Chinese community! Home robbery cases rising in this Barcelona neighborhood


According to the latest report from Catalonia's regional police force, Mossos d'Esquadra, robberies have recently increased in a very specific neighborhood in Barcelona—Tres Torres, located in the Sarrià-Sant Gervasi district. Chinese man robbed at gunpoint while night fishing in New York

Police appeal for leads


China Overseas News Network, April 28 — A Chinese man in Brooklyn, New York, was attacked and robbed at gunpoint by four masked assailants while fishing along the shoreline near Belt Parkway. The suspects also drove away the victim's car, which had been parked nearby. The local 68th Precinct has taken over the investigation and is urging the public to come forward with any information.


Records show that in the early hours of the 17th, the 44-year-old victim arrived at the shore in a black Mercedes-Benz. He parked along Belt Parkway. Around 12:40 a.m., he noticed a silver car enter the area. Shortly after, two men wearing colorful masks got out of the silver car and ran toward him.


One of the masked men pulled out a black handgun, pressed it against the victim's head, and threatened him to hand over his belongings; at the same time, the other robber searched the victim's clothes and took his car keys from his pocket, then shoved him to the ground.


The two took the keys and drove away the victim's car, heading north on Bell Park Avenue; seeing this, the suspect who had stayed in the silver car followed closely behind. The victim's mobile phone was not stolen, and after regaining his composure, he immediately called the police. However, by the time officers arrived, the suspects had already disappeared. As they had fled onto the highway, their trail became difficult to track, and they remain at large. Although the victim sustained injuries from falling during the robbery, his condition is not serious; police are currently conducting an active investigation.

Police intercept vehicle linked to shooting involving Chinese man

Over 100 pounds of marijuana seized


A 36-year-old Chinese man from Long Island was intercepted by police while driving a white SUV, suspected of involvement in a recent shooting in Flushing. During a search of the suspect's vehicle, police seized over 100 pounds of marijuana and a collapsible metal baton. The 109th Precinct of the NYPD announced on the 28th that the man has been arrested and charged with criminal possession of a controlled substance, criminal possession of marijuana, criminal possession of a weapon, and reckless endangerment. In addition, he is also connected to a 2021 drug case involving a Flushing apartment.


Police stated that at 12:40 a.m. on April 26, the NYPD's 109th Precinct, which oversees Flushing, received a report of a shooting at 143-25 41st Avenue. Upon arriving at the scene, a witness reported that a white SUV, which had just left the parking lot, was involved in the shooting. Police launched a pursuit, and when attempting to stop the SUV, the driver accelerated westbound on 41st Avenue.


Police said the suspect vehicle was eventually intercepted successfully at Beech Avenue and Parsons Boulevard. The 36-year-old driver, Dong De He (Dong De He, phonetic translation), whose registered address is Centerport, Long Island, resisted arrest and refused to be handcuffed. However, police found over 100 pounds of marijuana and a telescopic metal baton in his vehicle. Dong De He has now been arrested and charged with criminal possession of a controlled substance, criminal possession of marijuana, criminal possession of a weapon, and reckless endangerment.


Additionally, police stated that Dong De He is a repeat offender in illegal marijuana possession. On August 15, 2021, after a report of gunfire at an apartment near 137th Street and Latimer Place in Flushing, police raided an apartment where Dong De He lived with another Chinese man, Chen Yunbin (Chen Yunbin, phonetic translation), and seized approximately 308 pounds of marijuana, 1,609 chocolate bars laced with small amounts of psychedelic substances, a quantity of heroin and methamphetamine, a large number of drug paraphernalia, firearms, and nearly 100,000 yuan in cash. Both men were arrested as a result.

Chinese international student sexually harassed by deranged professor:

 I am the emperor, you are the palace maid—if you don't serve me well, I won't pass your thesis...


Recently, a case in South Korea involving a professor sexually harassing a Chinese international student shocked the international student community. Professor A, from a private university in Daegu, South Korea, served as a reviewer for doctoral theses. Student B is a Chinese international student, married, with a child attending kindergarten in China, who was pursuing a doctorate in South Korea at the time.


In 2021, Professor A demanded that Chinese doctoral student B call him "emperor," while she would be his "palace maid." B rejected the professor's absurd request, citing reasons such as "I don't have time" or "I have other commitments." Subsequently, Professor A sent a threatening message, implying that he would not allow B to pass her thesis.


B reported the incident to the university. After an investigation, the school determined that Professor A's actions constituted sexual harassment and violation of student rights, and dismissed him, also revoking his qualification as a doctoral thesis reviewer. B passed her defense in August that year and returned to China. Unexpectedly, during the university's investigation, Professor A continued his misconduct. The school found that, in addition to sexual harassment, he had forced B to send him greeting messages and made her pay for two meals totaling 290,000 South Korean won. After being fired, Professor A refused to accept the decision and filed a lawsuit at the local court seeking to overturn his dismissal.


The judge ruled that A's offensive remarks made when B delayed replying to messages violated a teacher's duty to maintain dignity. Furthermore, the judge noted that A showed no sign of reflection or willingness to improve during the university's investigation.


Ultimately, the court ruled against A, stating that "the disciplinary grounds of sexual harassment alone are sufficient for dismissal." A is preparing to appeal.

UNESCO Global Skills Academy: Empowering Young Women in the Dior Mentorship and Education Program


Dior and UNESCO hosted the latest edition of their global conference celebrating gender equality and women's empowerment. Held in Paris on International Women's Day, the event was attended by young women participating in the Dior Mentorship and Education Program. Nearly 400 psychologists and 300 mentors joined the supporter network for the 2022/2023 program.


Emmanuelle Favre, Senior Vice President of Human Resources at Christian Dior Couture, emphasized the importance of education in the "fight against obscurantism, injustice, and the sacrifice of opportunities around the world" during the opening of the event. She said: "At our level, this is a fight we want to be part of. Education and dissemination are core values of Dior."

Gender Equality in Film: UNESCO

Gives Voice to Women

Film Directors Speak


From February 25 to March 4, the 28th Panafrican Film and Television Festival of Ouagadougou (FESPACO) featured over 170 film screenings and attracted 20,000 participants. This eight-day celebration of African cinema placed the continent's latest creations at the center stage. UNESCO, in partnership with the Embassy of Japan in Burkina Faso, participated in this major event to promote the work of promising young African women directors who recently took part in UNESCO's filmmaking program in Nara, Japan.


Behind the camera, Mayowa Bakare (Nigeria), Okuhle Dyosopu (South Africa), Awa Gueye (Senegal), Joan Kiragu (Kenya), Uren Makut (Nigeria), Lydia Matata (Kenya), Fama Reyane Sow (Senegal), Delphine Yerbanga (Burkina Faso), and Floriane Zoundi (Burkina Faso) explored the city of Nara and its interactions with Japanese culture. This led to the creation of nine short films, weaving the filmmakers' personal experiences with their discoveries of Japan.


The festival also provided UNESCO and FESPACO an opportunity to further their long-standing mission of highlighting the talents of female creators and cultural professionals. On Thursday, March 2, a presentation of UNESCO's global report *Re|Shaping Policies for Creativity – Addressing culture as a global public good* drew an audience of 100. It also served as an occasion to discuss recent developments in gender equality and more broadly, the opportunities and challenges in cultural policy.

Popular TV Series Sparks Calls for Reform of Egypt's Family Law


A television series aired during Ramadan achieved great success, prompting two Egyptian lawmakers to call for a review of the country's guardianship law. In April 2023, Amira El Adly and Mohamed Ismail separately submitted requests to the Speaker of the House and the Minister of Justice, urging them to review a law criticized for unfairly affecting women and harming families.


According to the law, when a father dies, legal guardianship of his children under 21 and his estate passes to the paternal grandfather or a guardian designated by the father, leaving the mother with no legal say.


The 15-episode series *Under Guardianship* premiered on April 7 and aired its final episode on Friday. It tells the story of a mother of two children struggling to make ends meet after her husband's death. In the series, the protagonist, played by Mona Zaki, discovers she no longer has control over her husband's fishing boat—their sole source of income—making it difficult to pay rent. She also learns that the law allows her father-in-law to take the children out of school without her consent.


In a speech to parliament, Ismail called for the formation of a committee to "explore and address" the legislative implications of this 70-year-old law, which creates obstacles for women and fails to reflect "modern developments." Adly requested that the Minister of Justice brief parliament on the issue. She cited numerous complaints she had received from constituents affected by the law.

UN preparing "heartbreaking" decision to evacuate Afghanistan


UN officials say they are preparing a "heartbreaking" decision to withdraw from Afghanistan by May, if they fail to convince the Taliban to allow local women to work for the organization. According to Achim Steiner, Administrator of the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP), UN officials have spent months negotiating with Taliban leaders, trying to persuade them to make an exception to their strict decree banning local women from working for the UN, issued earlier this month.


An estimated two-thirds of the population—28 million people—will need humanitarian aid in 2023, while the U.S. government and other G7 members continue to threaten aid cuts. The Taliban have refused to change their position announced in December, which appears to follow orders from their reclusive leader Hibatullah Akhundzada. Women, who make up about one-third of locally hired aid workers, are considered most vulnerable to any aid reductions. The decree also restricts women's access to education.

Since the Taliban announced on April 12 that Afghan women employed by the United Nations could no longer go to work, 3,300 Afghans hired by the UN—2,700 men and 600 women—have remained at home. But UN spokesperson Stéphane Dujarric said they would continue to work and receive pay. More than 600 international staff members of the UN, including 200 women, are unaffected by the Taliban decree.

"I decided I could do better than them"

While teaching Japanese cooking classes in France, Ai Ishimori felt a strong urge to return to her home country. "Shinzo Abe was prime minister at the time, and a group of older men were doing a terrible job managing the nation," she said. "But I couldn't do anything about it. I was deeply worried about the direction my country was heading."


Five years later, the 38-year-old woman is running in Tokyo's local elections—the first time she has attempted to win a political position—and hopes to begin dismantling male dominance in G7 nations, a country where women are struggling to break one of the world's toughest glass ceilings.


"I decided I could do better than them," Ai Ishimori told The Guardian during her campaign, as she sought a seat in Nerima Ward's local assembly in northwest Tokyo. "Japan has a gender gap in every area of life, especially in politics. But there is a solution—more women in politics."


Japan has one of the lowest rates of female political representation in the world. A decade ago, Prime Minister Shinzo Abe, under the mentorship of the Liberal Democratic Party, pledged at the UN General Assembly to "create a society where women shine," yet Prime Minister Kishida Fumio's cabinet includes only two women.

Self-taught Chinese-American concept designer Jen Zee has become one of the most representative female artists in the gaming industry


Jen Zee is a Chinese-American concept designer from the United States and currently serves as Art Director at game company Supergiant Games. A self-taught artist who entered the field mid-career, she has contributed to numerous projects, creating concept art for popular games such as Hades, Transistor, Bastion, and The Lord of the Rings. Known for her bold and vibrant color palette, her path differs from most game art directors—this Chinese-American woman is not formally trained but self-taught. From amateur hobbyist to professional peak, transitioning from studying Information Science at the University of Washington to becoming Art Director on Hades, she has created many distinctive and widely recognized works, and is now one of the most representative female artists in the gaming industry.


In the world of Transistor, the protagonist Red stands apart from the traditional portrayal of female characters in video games as sexualized and alluring. When discussing her depiction of female characters, Jen said she discovered more ways to make a woman appealing to people of all genders and orientations. Jen Zee's story continues to inspire artists like her who lack formal training—perhaps it is precisely this background that allows one to break free from the constraints of artistic convention and create a unique style and visual world.

Lily Gao, Asian actress in Resident Evil 4, issues statement after online harassment: Please respect Asian women's images

Lily Gao, the actress who voiced Ada Wong in the English version of Resident Evil 4 Remake, faced a wave of online criticism and harassment after the game's release, leading her to deactivate her social media accounts. Recently, Lily Gao reopened her social media and posted a statement:


"Being the first actress to portray Ada Wong in a Resident Evil game is an immense honor for me. I will always be grateful to the producers and directors who chose me. Unfortunately, with the game's release came a familiar sense of alienation. While criticism is expected, it is not the first time an actor has faced racism and sexual harassment simply for being cast. Inauthentic choices perpetuate unhealthy representations, making the communities these characters are meant to represent feel even more misrepresented. We must stop consuming the gendered, sexualized, and mystified image of the Asian woman and respect every kind of Asian woman. My Ada is a survivor—gentle, just, intelligent, and interesting. She is unpredictable, resilient, and anything but a stereotype."


At the end of her statement, Lily Gao listed three female figures who most influenced her portrayal of Ada: Nie Yinniang (from The Assassin, played by Shu Qi), Maggie Cheung (from Irma Vep, played by Maggie Cheung), and Faye (from Chungking Express, played by Faye Wong).
