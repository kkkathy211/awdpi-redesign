Violence against women is a violation of human rights and occurs every day around the world. Globally, one in three women has experienced physical or sexual violence, most often by an intimate partner. While domestic violence and abuse can sometimes be difficult to detect, understanding the signs of abusive relationships enables us to better identify such behaviors and seek or offer help when necessary.


Common signs of abuse




Your partner constantly monitors all your actions,, including where you go and who you are with, and they forbid or prevent you from seeing friends or family, or going to work or school.


Your partner insists you immediately respond to their text messages, emails, and phone calls,, and demands to know the passwords to your social media accounts, email, and other online accounts.


A partner with abusive tendencies may display jealousy, including repeatedly accusing you of infidelity. They may also try to control your spending, medication, or use of contraception, or dictate your choices in daily life such as clothing and food.


They belittle you,, perhaps by insulting your appearance, intelligence, or interests, humiliating you in front of others, or damaging your belongings or things you care about.


A partner with abusive tendencies may display irritability, impatience, or unpredictable emotions,, leaving you never knowing what might trigger their outbursts. They may blame you for their violent emotional episodes and make physical threats or cause harm to you, themselves, or members of your family—including children or pets.


They may physically harm you by hitting, slapping, pushing, shoving, grabbing, slapping, kicking, or biting,, or may use weapons to harm or threaten you.



They may commit sexual abuse, including rape or other forms of coercive sexual acts.) . They wrongly believe that your past consent means you will always consent to sexual activity in the future. They may also believe that agreeing to one act implies consent to further intimacy—for example, some abusers believe that every kiss means sex should follow.


If you report or resist their abusive behavior, they may threaten to turn you over to authorities, claiming you have broken the law.


You can call your local helpline to speak with a professional.


Safety tips for survivors of abuse




If you believe you are experiencing abuse, seek help. Below are suggestions to help you stay safe and access support.


Consider confiding in a trusted friend, family member, or neighbor about your situation and concerns, and work with them to create a plan for when you need help.. For example, establish a secret code, phrase, sentence, or emoji to enable safer communication between you.


Make an escape plan., For instance, tell your partner you need to go to the pharmacy or grocery store, and once you arrive, immediately call for help. To ensure a successful escape, remain flexible and prepare multiple plausible reasons depending on the situation.



If possible, always keep your communication devices charged and functional. Memorize contact numbers of people who can help, such as friends, family, or police. In life-threatening situations, call the police if you believe it is safe to do so.


Try to document the patterns and severity of your partner's violence, as this can help you predict when the abuse may escalate.


You can call your local helpline to speak with a professional.


How to help

If you are concerned that a friend may be suffering from domestic violence or abuse, or if you suspect someone is in danger, review the following suggestions on how to help them access safety and support.


If you are worried about your friend's safety, stay in touch and use your creativity. Avoid arousing the abuser's suspicion so that your communication channel remains open—for example, if you both have children, you could arrange a call involving your children. You could also create a secret code to use in conversations, enabling safer contact with your friend.


Ask your friend which method of communication they prefer. Establishing a secure communication channel is crucial, as the abuser—who may be listening—could be nearby. You should also ask whether your friend prefers instant messaging over phone calls, and whether there is a specific platform or app they would rather use.



Support and believe them. Let them know they are not alone and assure them of your concrete help and support. Recognize that talking about abuse may be extremely difficult for them. If they want to talk, listen carefully and try to empathize with their experience.


Respect their right to consent. Unless you are certain your friend is in life-threatening danger, do not take action without their consent. They understand the safety risks best, so they should be in control of any decisions related to the abuse they are experiencing.


Respect their privacy. Survivors returning to normal life often face safety threats, stigma, shame, and victim-blaming; therefore, their experiences and identities must remain confidential—unless they explicitly agree to share information.


Offer practical help and share useful resources. Let your friend know you are willing to help, and if possible, offer them a safe place to stay, transportation, or other forms of support, which can significantly increase their sense of safety.


Let your friend know they can speak with professionals who can provide expert assistance. You can provide them with information about local services and helplines.
