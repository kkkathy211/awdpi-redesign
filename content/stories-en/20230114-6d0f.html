 Introduction

Whether it is Lila or Lenù, we see in both of them the courage and growth that come from leaping into the abyss, a growth detached from the male gaze and independent of patriarchy. Reading the Neapolitan Quartet allows us to see that with independence comes freedom.

The "Ta" Behind the Neapolitan Quartet


Elena Ferrante (Elena Ferrante, January 1, 1943—), a contemporary Italian anonymous writer, began publishing novels in 1992. Starting in 2011, she published the Neapolitan Quartet, which caused a sensation and became an international bestseller. In 2016, she was included in Time magazine's list of the "100 Most Influential People in the World Today."[1]


Anonymous Writer


Because Elena Ferrante never attends public events and only gives extremely limited written interviews, no one knows her true identity.


As early as before the publication of her debut novel, Troubling Love, in 1991, Ferrante wrote to her editor about not promoting the book: "If this book has any value, that will be enough. I will not attend seminars or discussions, even if invited. I will not go to accept awards, even if I win. I will not promote this book, especially not on television, whether in Italy—where such demands may arise—or abroad. I will only participate through writing, but I will also try my best to keep this participation to a minimum."[2]


People have speculated about her true identity, and for a time "Who is Elena?" became a widely debated mystery. Yet Elena herself has made her stance on anonymity clear in a passage from Frantumaglia (a collection of Ferrante's letters, interviews, and essays spanning over two decades): Once a book is written, the author is no longer needed. If a book has substance, it will eventually find its readers; if it has no value, then so be it. There are many such examples—I love those ancient and timeless books whose authors are unverifiable, mysterious yet full of life. In interviews, Elena has also stated: My identity, my gender, can all be found in my writing.


Image source: Pixabay


Below is an excerpt from Stefania Scattone's interview with Ferrante:


Scattone: Why do you refuse to become a public figure?


Ferrante: It comes from an almost obsessive, compulsive need. When we write, the hardship of writing touches every part of the body. When a book ends, it feels as if a person has been forcibly searched, stripped of all respect. The only thing the author wishes for is to return to wholeness, to their ordinary self—with their own language, thoughts, relationships, and work. Only the work is public; the work contains everything we wanted to say. Now, who still cares about the person who wrote it? Most importantly, the work has been written.


Scattone: Your works don't seem written for readers, but rather like private writing—the only listener being the paper (or computer) or yourself. Is that the case?


Ferrante: That's not true. I write because I hope people will read my books. But when I write, the reader is not the main focus. The main thing is to find a kind of energy, to dig deeply into the story I'm telling. In my life, the only moment that is completely free from others' influence is when I'm searching for language and words, breaking through ordinary expressions, peeling back appearances. Even if I discover that this digging is useless, that there's nothing beneath the surface, that doesn't frighten me.


Elena Ferrante's novels are all set in Naples, Italy. Starting in 2011, Ferrante published four novels that tell the story of two poor girls growing up in 1950s Naples: My Brilliant Friend (L'amica geniale, 2011), The Story of a New Name (Storia del nuovo cognome, 2012), Those Who Leave and Those Who Stay (Storia di chi fugge e di chi resta, 2013), and The Story of the Lost Child (Storia della bambina perduta, 2014).


Her works leave a deep impression with their meticulous depictions of female characters and psychology. In interviews, Elena mentioned: "The women in my writing are strong—they are cultured, self-aware, rights-conscious, and upright—but at the same time, they are also easily broken, becoming dependent on others, sometimes falling into very terrible states. I myself go through such instability; I know this state well, and it has influenced my writing."


Regarding media questions about gender and writing, Elena responded: "I am certain of one thing: I can only feel capable of writing a good story when I adopt a concise, clear, powerful female voice. I've been thinking for some time that we should build our own female tradition—we must never abandon the skills left to us by our predecessors. As women, we must create a strong, rich, and vast literary world, as rich as the literary world of male writers, or even richer..


A woman writer should only concern herself with telling what she knows and feels, regardless of beauty or ugliness, regardless of contradictions, without following any rules, not even those of other women in the same camp. Writing requires great ambition, the ability to break free from all prejudices, and a deliberate form of resistance.". "

Rebirth and Exploration: The Presence of Feminism


The themes in the books—women, violence, rights, friendship, and love—prompt us to reflect on how to realize self-worth and how women can break free from societal discipline.


Childhood—Patriarchy and Violence


The childhoods of the two protagonists, Lila and Lenù, are surrounded by various forms of violence and chaos. In recalling this period later in life, Lenù says:


"I don't miss our childhood at all, because our childhood was full of violence. It happened to us, at home, outside—every day something occurred. But I remember that I never thought the life we lived was bad. This was just life. It was normal. One of the responsibilities we learned in growing up was that we had to make others' lives harder before they could make ours harder."


Though eroded by violence and fear, "they" cannot leave the neighborhood. Not just the children—these children's mothers spend their entire lives never leaving this town. The women in the community harm each other, like caged beasts fighting in captivity. There is blood and violence everywhere, yet to the women of the town, this is normal, because they have long grown accustomed to such a life.


"We grew up watching fathers beat mothers. In growing up, we all believed that no one else could ever touch us—but parents, fiancés, and husbands could slap us anytime they wanted. It was out of love, or for education, a constant education."


Image source: Pngtree


When Lila wanted to continue her education, her father threw her out the window, breaking her arm. As an adult, her first sexual experience was nearly rape, and after marriage, she constantly lived in fear of being beaten by her husband. She was not allowed to go out or receive guests, nor to have her own thoughts. If her husband discovered the letters and diaries she wrote, she would be met with punches and kicks. She faced not only the authoritarian terror from her husband but also discrimination from other women.


Yet Lila, growing up in such an environment, did not become an ideal puppet. Instead, through transgressive acts, she challenged the pervasive forces of surveillance around her, fighting for a sliver of survival. She says: "His beating me means nothing to me; afterward, I become stronger. But he will never make a woman obey him."


Unlike Lila, the more introverted and gentle Lenù learned obedience and how to please others, learning "to masculinize her mind in order to integrate into male culture." She studied hard and entered university because she had witnessed Naples' bloodshed and decay from childhood, all in an effort to escape Naples, to escape the domination of women's bodies and minds by men in the small town.


The Awakening of Female Consciousness—Friendship, Desire


Lenù's rebellion erupted after marriage and after having two daughters. The triggers included her long-unrequited love for Nino, as well as a different kind of domestic violence from her husband—one unlike the Neapolitan style. Her husband did not support her career, refused to help with childcare, and even slapped her. In the book, Mariarosa says: "I hate those overly intelligent men who tell me who I should be." Lenù realized her husband also imposed his views on her. He was accustomed to telling women how to be proper women. He judged her clothing, body, and mannerisms—sounding as if he were helping her become a better version of herself, a so-called perfect woman, perfect wife.


At this point, Lenù realized that no matter how many books she read, how much she tried to escape her origins, how many class barriers she crossed, she still could not escape this male gaze, nor truly integrate into the so-called "male culture." She was the first to leave Naples, and also the first to realize "marriage is not what people think—it's like an institution that strips all humanity from sex." people.


So she decided to escape once again. Her affair, was also a kind of departure, a departure from the "self" she had constructed under societal norms and rules—a well-mannered, docile female image. She once confessed to Nino: "I want to walk my own path, not yours."


Lila could no longer continue her education, so she kept working in her father's shoe shop, designing shoes herself; when she could no longer endure her painful marriage, she fled; even while struggling to survive, she never stopped learning, building her life from nothing. At different stages, both Lila and Lenù experienced disillusionment with marriage and love. Life repeatedly pushed them to the edge of a cliff, forcing them into desperate situations where they shattered themselves, only to rebuild anew.


"We liked sitting side by side: I was blonde, she was dark; I was quiet, she was anxious; I was polite, she was suspicious. We were two opposite people, yet so consistent with each other."


Whether Lila or Lenù, we see in both of them the courage and growth that come from leaping off the cliff when faced with it—a growth detached from the male gaze, independent of patriarchy.

To have independence is to have freedom.


3

Mirror Relationship and Inspirational Significance


Lila and Elena are each other's support, even though at the diverging paths of time, their different choices lead to different outcomes, each stubbornly forging ahead while serving as the other's reference: Lila, forced by family to drop out of school and marry early; Elena, striving through relentless study, yet moving toward "a vulgar struggle to elevate her social status throughout her life.".


Each undergoes her own fears, self-reflection, and redemption, turning to the other in despair: "You must never go through what I've suffered," "You must walk the path I had to abandon." Through each other, they achieve self-identification, gaining in the mirror of the "brilliant friend" the subjective image of the "ideal self."[3].


When Lenù was about to wash Lila's body before her wedding, Lila suddenly said to her: "No matter what happens, you must keep going to school. I'll pay for it. You must keep studying—promise me." Lila's tone and gaze were extremely resolute, "You are my brilliant friend. You must become smarter than any of them, whether boy or girl."


When we all assume the "brilliant friend" refers to Lila, we realize that in Lila's heart, Lenù is the brilliant friend—the only girl in the neighborhood still attending high school. And she transforms all her inner jealousy and resentment into stubborn hopes and expectations. Though she can no longer continue her studies, she still wishes for Lenù to escape this neighborhood in her place. This mutual understanding and protection is undeniably pure and moving.


The Neapolitan Novels depict the suburbs of Naples after World War II, where women's emotions and psychology transcend time and space. The virtue of literature lies precisely in constructing a world through words, capturing real reflections from others' fragmented lives. Though centered on Naples, many female readers find their own reflections in the book. The protagonists' stories and psychological emotions easily resonate with contemporary women—the subtle, shared feelings: love and acceptance mixed with admiration, jealousy, and competitiveness; the illusion and disappointment of romantic longing toward the opposite sex; rebelling against family yet unable to sever blood ties; and siblings, unable to resist desire, rushing one after another toward their own tragedies…


Image source: Pngtree


As Ferrante says in Frantumaglia: "The entire Neapolitan Novels, or indeed the entire history of female writing, should be a mutual reflection and compensation of fragments—the fragments of the 'I,' the fragments of the repressed 'female.' For a female self built upon fragments and contradictions, the mirror is something we should not indulge in. Only by escaping the tyranny of the mirror can women truly see each other, and see themselves."
