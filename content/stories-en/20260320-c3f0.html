At the recently concluded 98th Academy Awards, Chloé Zhao's *Hamnet*was nominated for Best Picture and several other awards, and ultimately secured lead actress Jessie Buckley the title of Best Actress at this year's Oscars.






Image source: @Today FM


*Hamnet* is adapted from Maggie O'Farrell's eponymous historical novel. The protagonist is not the world-famous playwright Shakespeare, but his wife—Agnes. The story traces their love, marriage, parenthood, the grief of losing a child, and eventual reconciliation, sketching a female life trajectory long buried by history.


Though set in the late sixteenth century and not overtly waving a feminist banner, the film is permeated throughout with a quiet yet resolute female consciousness. This consciousness is not a forced imposition of modern ideas, but naturally woven into the fabric of that era, leaving viewers with no sense of dissonance.


Chloé Zhao renders the female gaze as the foundational layer of narrative in an extremely restrained manner—neither flamboyant nor ignorable.


One | Creating Space for the Silent to Speak









This quality is first established through the film's spatial construction and cinematographic language. Zhao transforms natural spaces into material vessels of the female perspective, while simultaneously using specific camera movements and spatial expressions to allow this perspective to emerge in reverse.


The film establishes this dual relationship from the very beginning. Agnes lies on her back in a forest clearing—a place of joy and also of childbirth. Here, Agnes is neither greater than a tree nor smaller than a cluster of wild grass; the spatial background becomes an extension of her being.


The frame places her on equal scale with trees and rocks. This is not a demotion of female identity, nor a romanticized "return to nature" of the heroine, but rather a positioning at the level of human ontology: before entering the system of social discourse, humans are first and foremost part of the natural order. This is the primordial state of life before it is named, before it is inscribed into any narrative framework.


The cave is like a womb, the forest like a home. Between Agnes and this land, there is no hierarchy, no relationship of possession and being possessed, but a mutually dependent symbiosis.







Thereafter, natural spaces continuously serve as containers for female experience, and the specificity of this experience in turn imbues the spaces with new meaning. That old tree has witnessed the child's birth and departure; the swarm of bees reflects the fluctuations of Agnes's inner order. These natural elements are not passive "symbols"; they form a bidirectional reference with the character's emotional state: Agnes perceives herself through nature, and nature gains meaning through her gaze.


Zhao's camera prefers to remain at a certain distance—this distance is not coldness, but an ethics of looking. She allows the audience to observe Agnes from afar, within her spatial context. Zhao does not fragment the female body and emotions into consumable close-ups; instead, she lets the space the character inhabits share the weight of narrative, allowing wind, light, shadow, and sound to enter first, foregrounding the character's connection with nature. In the childbirth scene, there is no hospital, no midwife, no glorification of the mother's body—only the struggle between life and earth, a balance that interprets vitality.







Interestingly, although Zhao's camera rarely judges the character, often her camera movements overlap with the character's gaze: when the eyes rest on a certain environment, the character's inner world simultaneously occupies the present, the past, and the future. Space is not silent, but a vessel for inner emotion, granted the ability to speak through the lens. The camera is no longer an external medium for objective recording; it becomes, together with the character, a vessel of emotion, and its movement itself is a form of speech.


The duality of spatial construction presents another subtle contrast in the film. For Agnes, natural space is always an active, vibrant domain: gathering herbs in the forest, giving birth among the trees, sitting alone beneath the old tree—she is focused and certain. Nature never defines or rejects her; it simply allows her to become herself.


The interior sets in the film, however, are entirely different. Though the kitchen and bedroom are furnished with warmth and detail, the overall tone is gloomy: gray-brown walls, dim lighting, cramped layouts, forming invisible boundaries that trap her. Arguments with William's family, resistance during her second childbirth, and the illness and death of her child all occur indoors, where space is consistently associated with anxiety, conflict, and sorrow. The seemingly cozy decor becomes a subtle irony: it reminds viewers that maintaining a family is an unrelenting burden. In these man-made spaces, she cannot contend with the openness of nature; her emotions are compressed, confined, and worn down.




This contrast is subtly reconciled in the theater sequence. The Globe Theatre is a semi-natural, semi-artificial space: constructed as a stage yet open to the sky. Here, Agnes reaches out to touch the actor playing her son, finding in this in-between space an anchor for her inner world. This is the female character actively carving out a spiritual territory for herself amid passivity, a wisdom and resilience internalized from lived experience. Zhao seems to say: even without a fixed dwelling, living fluidly can itself be a paradigm. Agnes's final peace arises precisely from this realization: mooring and drifting are not opposites—she can be both at once.







The complicity of lens and space ultimately allows the female perspective to emerge from silence: in an era when women were stripped of voice, natural space became their only channel of connection. Precisely because she cannot enter the order of social discourse, her connection with the world is preserved in a more primordial way. When Zhao's lens gazes upon space, silence itself becomes a form of speech. Agnes does not need to pour out her pain to the camera—because the environment has already spoken for her.




Two | Suspending the Narrative of Success, Allowing the Everyday to Emerge




In her handling of narrative structure, Zhao continues her consistent anti-heroic stance and non-linear storytelling—two narrative strategies that are precisely key to the emergence of the film's female perspective.


The film abandons the conventional techniques of traditional biographical cinema; here, there is no depiction of Shakespeare writing *Hamlet*, no moment of sudden inspiration, no interactions with his theatrical contemporaries, and no discussion whatsoever about drama itself. This deliberate "absence" is itself a narrative stance: it refuses to center the male myth as the main storyline. As Shakespeare's success story is set aside, Agnes's lived experience emerges from the cracks of history, and the everyday life that could only remain in the background of traditional narratives is brought to the forefront.







At the same time, in the narrative process, her portrayal gains greater authenticity. The rift between the couple cannot be simply attributed to one party, but stems more from two different frequencies of pain. Zhao does not attempt moral judgment here, and allows disorder to exist. She avoids saturating the story with conflicts of "right and wrong," letting them simply be present. This non-dualistic perspective becomes one of her ways of deconstructing the heroic narrative.


Non-linear storytelling further strengthens the uniqueness of this perspective. Zhao disrupts chronological order, interweaving Agnes's memories, premonitions, and the present moment. This structure itself mimics the way women perceive the world: not as linear chains of cause and effect, but as a web-like, cyclical, embodied and nature-bound mode of experience. As viewers follow the narrative rhythm into Agnes's inner world, touching those primordial experiences that cannot be translated into language—childbirth pain, the void of losing a child, the silence of conversing with nature—they gain the possibility of perception before the screen.


In traditional narratives, grief is often compressed into a single "stage"—something to be experienced, overcome, and transformed into motivation for the next action.


The non-linear structure grants the film another ability: allowing grief to unfold at its own pace, without yielding to the pressure of plot progression. Zhao's camera permits the couple's grief to revisit repeatedly, allows Agnes to re-enter the same pain at different points in time, allows nature to detach and regenerate, allows her to sit in silence, doing nothing. This narrative "blank space" is precisely a form of respect for female emotional experience: it acknowledges that some grief cannot be "resolved," only carried.







Anti-heroic narrative strips the male genius of narrative privilege, while non-linear storytelling offers an alternative framework of perception. Under their combined effect, the female perspective finally gains solid ground. Agnes's pain does not need to be validated by being "written into a play," nor does her daily life require justification through "influencing her husband's creativity." For this reason, the film's climax does not occur at the premiere of *Hamlet*, but at the moment Agnes reaches out her hand—a gesture that is neither reconciliation with her husband nor with theater, but rather the completion of a spiritual handover between herself and her own soul.




Three | A Woman Not to Be Defined



In constructing the character of Agnes, Zhao Ting deliberately steered clear of the traditional historical biopic approach. The label of "witch" assigned to her is not a sensationalized embellishment, but a narrative strategy: using a socially marginalized identity to establish tension between the character and mainstream order.


The film does not deliberately dramatize the oppression she suffered due to her identity, nor does it romanticize her as a symbol of female resistance. Instead, Agnes's "witch" traits are portrayed as extremely mundane: she enjoys wandering through dense grassy fields, understands the properties of herbs, and occasionally can read certain omens from the people she touches... These abilities, which were considered somewhat peculiar at the time, were whispered among the townsfolk as "spooky." But in Chloé Zhao's lens, they are simply the deep, untamed connection she shares with nature.


Agnes never adjusts her own logic of behavior based on others' perceptions. She doesn't care how she is labeled, nor does she attempt to prove anything to anyone. When she sees the young man who loves writing, she walks straight toward him instead of waiting to be chosen; when her husband leaves for London, she stays behind in the countryside to support the family alone; when her son dies, she mourns in her own way. None of her choices are responses to external expectations, but stem from her own judgment of things.







Through subtle depictions of the mother figure, the film gives deeper roots to the protagonist's independence. The brief memories of Agnes with her mother are short yet hold subtle clues: her ability to communicate with nature, her unregulated way of acting—perhaps these were inherited from her mother. But these recollections mostly surface only after she herself becomes a mother, suggesting an intergenerational transmission of female experience: only through raising a child can one truly understand one's own mother. Agnes's life is thus placed within an extended lineage—she is both an inheritor of her mother's experience and a vessel passing it on to the next generation.


Within this construction, the film shapes a subtle feminist thread: it does not aim to create a heroine who confronts patriarchal order, but simply presents a woman who maintains full subjectivity within an existing structure.


Agnes's independence is an entire way of perceiving the world, making judgments, and bearing the consequences of her choices. This independence is so natural—it requires no external validation, nor does it ever seek external recognition. And the faint, underlying thread connecting her to her mother suggests that such independence never arises from nothing, but is quietly passed down from one generation of women to the next through everyday life.




Zhao Ting: "Presence" from the Margins








Image source: @Bethany Mollenkof for NPR


Understanding Agnes cannot be separated from the director Zhao Ting herself. A Chinese woman who grew up in a blended family and left home to study abroad at a young age, she has often spoken in interviews about her long-standing identity confusion. She was uncertain whether she belonged to the East or the West, to which family, or to which cultural tradition. This confusion eventually crystallized into a creative consciousness: she has always been searching for a state of "presence," a fleeting moment of unity with the environment and with the self.


Hence, her films consistently reveal her care for marginalized individuals, and her characters are always deeply entangled with space. Dialogue is minimized, while the camera lingers for long stretches on the interaction between characters and their surroundings. In *Hamnet*, Agnes is precisely the one constantly seeking "unity": unity with the forest, with nature, with loss, and ultimately with her own fate. She does not launch any form of resistance against the era's unfriendly conditions for women; she simply lives out her life cycle in a plain, tree-like, wind-like manner. And this very simplicity constitutes a form of existence with profound tension.


Zhao Ting's camera does not attempt to explain her, but merely accompanies her. This kind of companionship does not exploit pain, amplify sorrow, or reduce the character to a mere vessel for an idea.






Image source: @Golden Derby




Zhao Ting's camera always carries the same question: How does one coexist with loss?


From *The Rider* to *Nomadland*, and now to *Hamnet*, this question reappears in different forms. The play known as *Hamlet* was born precisely out of unspeakable grief over the loss of a child, transformed through artistic energy.


When the weight of loss becomes unbearable, people turn to art as a medium for support and redemption. Great works are not created to conquer death, but to provide a place of belonging for emotions that cannot otherwise be properly settled. This is the ultimate proposition toward which *Hamnet* points.


Perhaps through Agnes's story, Zhao Ting is attempting another reconciliation. The reason *Hamnet* resonates with so many viewers may not be because it tells a famous story from centuries ago, but because it allows every audience member sitting in the darkness to see those ordinary women who silently endure and quietly exist in life.


Their names will never be carved on any monument, but they are the precondition for all stories to emerge.
