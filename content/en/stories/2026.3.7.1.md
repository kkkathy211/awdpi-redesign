"We hold the ballot in our hands,

just as tightly as love letters."

  

—Film *There's Still Tomorrow*


  

In black-and-white imagery, the Italian neorealist film *There's Still Tomorrow* uses a run through history to write a tender yet powerful footnote to women's awakening. When Delia rushes toward the polling station clutching her ballot, this middle-aged housewife, long trapped by domestic chores and violence, finally takes her first step out of the patriarchal prison in that spring of 1946.

  

Directed, written, and starring Paola Cortellesi, the film is set in post-World War II Italy. With the stark brushstrokes of neorealism, it turns its lens on the everyday lives of women wrapped layer by layer in housework, violence, and discrimination, sketching a spiritual map of women's journey from individual awakening to collective resistance amid the interplay of absurd humor and harsh reality.

  

<strong>The generational mirror of women's fate:</strong>

<strong>A dialogue of lives under patriarchal violence</strong>

The slap at the beginning of the film hits like a hammer, shattering Delia's routine: at six in the morning, her husband Ivano's palm lands precisely on her face, without warning, as if this were a rightful morning ritual of marriage. Delia touches her burning cheek, gets up to prepare breakfast for her three children, and wipes down her paralyzed father-in-law, as if the red mark were merely soot accidentally brushed against her skin.

  

This scene repeats for eighteen years straight; her two sons don't even blink when they witness their mother being hit.

  

The director wraps violence in a light piano melody—Ivano's fists rise and fall to the rhythm of a tango, the camera spinning past Delia's stumbling fall. The gentle notes of *O Sole Mio* form an absurd counterpoint to the bruised face. This aestheticization of violence, however, precisely reveals the essence of patriarchy: <strong>Beating has long become the "morning ritual" of marriage; insults have been packaged as "love education."</strong>

  

At the umbrella repair shop, male workers tease Delia while dumping the most difficult tasks on her, and her piece-rate wage is always thirty percent less than the men's. When sewing for a wealthy family, the mistress uses perfume to mask the cheap soap smell on Delia's body, yet still deducts two liras from her pay. Back home, Ivano throws the money she earned with difficulty onto the table, yelling at her for "miscalculating even the cost of bread," while turning a blind eye to his own expenses for prostitution.

  

Most suffocating is the sexual harassment from her paralyzed father-in-law: when Delia wipes him down, his cloudy hand suddenly gropes her buttocks, his mouth twisting into a disgusting grin. This detail cleverly echoes the father's "domestic violence education" to his son: "Don't beat her every day—beat her badly once in a while, so she fears you." This logic of intergenerational violence under patriarchy is laid bare in the dialogue between the two male characters.

  

In contrast to this father-son cycle of violence is the daughter Marcella's harsh question to Delia: <strong>"Why don't you fight back? Why don't you run away?" </strong>In her daughter's eyes, Delia is the embodiment of cowardice.

  

Yet the seemingly happy marriage Marcella is about to enter is precisely the same cage Delia has struggled in for twenty years. The scene where Delia tries to stop her daughter's engagement is filled with black humor: Delia calculates bomb ingredients using spaghetti, designs an escape route with clotheslines, and finally asks American soldier William to blow up the café. This sequence lacks heroic grandeur, instead resembling a housewife's "cooking experiment"—essentially, Delia's desperate, absurd act of fighting absurdity for her daughter's sake.

<strong>From individual escape to collective awakening:</strong>

<strong>The ballot as a symbol of women's awakening</strong>

  

In the wrinkled pocket of Delia's apron lies a letter repeatedly crumpled and smoothed. The audience, like Delia, assumes it is a secret elopement invitation from her former lover Nino, who always said, "Life is better in the north." She secretly saves her wages, sews a new dress from scraps, and even practices her "escape route" in the silence between her husband's snores.

  

Yet when the camera focuses on the words on the paper, everyone holds their breath: <strong>It is not a love letter, but a voter registration card.</strong>

  

This twist is in fact the director's sharpest satire on traditional female narratives. In countless stories, <strong>women are always cast as objects "waiting to be saved" or "fleeing for love." But Delia's choice shatters this fantasy. She is not running into another man's arms, but toward the polling station.</strong>

The camera sweeps across the streets of Rome, showing groups of women clutching ballots and walking toward ballot boxes. Some wear faded wedding dresses, some hold their daughters' hands, some deliberately wipe off their lipstick, because ballots stained with lipstick will be invalidated.

  

<strong>"We hold the ballot in our hands, just as tightly as love letters." </strong>This line hits like a hammer in the film. In patriarchal society, women's love letters are always tied to "being chosen": chosen by a husband, accepted by a family, recognized by society. But the ballot is different—it is the first time women write their own names as <strong>"subjects." </strong>The director uses nearly poetic cinematography to contrast two scenes: one of young Delia passionately in love with Ivano, dancing together in a square while the background music sings, "You are the gift of my life"; the other of middle-aged Delia standing shoulder to shoulder with a group of unfamiliar women, using silent stares to force back her furious, raging husband.

  

The former is a romantic trap; the latter is clear-eyed resistance. When the women hum in unison with closed mouths, there are no lines, no slogans—only vibrations from deep in their throats.

  

This time, silence is no longer a symbol of submission. The women's determined eyes, unified gestures, and wordless melody finally become a silent tsunami, shattering the eardrums of patriarchy.

  

At the polling station, the gaze exchanged between Delia and Marcella becomes the moment that breaks the ice in their mother-daughter relationship, revealing the intergenerational significance of women's awakening.

  

Marcella watches her mother solemnly write her name and finally understands the private savings hidden in her underwear, the silhouette of her mother mending clothes late at night. Her mother's endurance was not cowardice, but paving a path with flesh and blood for her daughter. When Marcella slips the lost ballot into her mother's palm, the generational gap between them dissolves. The two women complete a passing of the torch in female subjectivity. The previous generation accumulates courage through scars; the next generation continues the resistance through action.

  

Black humor and gentle resistance:

  

![image](/media/stories/20260307-100b/0031.webp)
  

<strong>The dazzling expression of feminism</strong>

<strong>Paola Cortellesi's lens is full of Italian humor and romance, yet sharp critique hides in the cracks between absurdity and farce.</strong>

Delia hides her private savings in flatbreads, her underwear, even under the mattress of her paralyzed father-in-law; Ivano performs exaggerated grief at his father's death, sobbing his heart out in front of the church, only to turn around and drink his dead father's wine; the neighbor women sit around the dead father-in-law, ostensibly praying, but in reality sending off this old man who oppressed them their whole lives with the most vicious curses.

  

Yet the most moving moments in the film are those of gentle resistance. When Delia combs her daughter's hair, she says:

  

"You can become whoever you want to be—not because of who you'll marry, but because of who you are." <strong>This sentence is her summary of her own failed life, and her hope for her daughter's future. </strong>At the polling station, an old woman trembles as she wipes off her lipstick; a young girl stands tall as she hands in her ballot. In their eyes there is no fear, only anticipation for tomorrow. These moments make feminism not a cold theory, but a warm force woven into daily life.

  

The feminist expression in *There's Still Tomorrow* is a

  

"gentle rebellion." <strong>It has no grand battle scenes, yet it unfolds silent gender revolutions in the kitchen's grease, the wood shavings of the umbrella repair shop, and the steps of the polling station. Feminist power lies hidden in every act of courage to say "no": refusing an unjust insult, insisting on the right to work, fighting for a daughter's chance to be educated. </strong>Delia's story is not a fictional heroic legend, but a true snapshot of countless ordinary women:

  

They are mothers, wives, part-time workers—yet on a certain morning, they are awakened by a single ballot. <strong>It does not dramatize blood, yet turns bruises into medals; it does not glorify sacrifice, yet transforms mundane daily life into a revolutionary site. It is precisely these everyday acts of female resistance—like the skirt fluttering as Delia runs—that, in the long river of history, will ultimately gather into a wave of change.</strong>
